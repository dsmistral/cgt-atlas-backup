Chapter 15 · Graph Structure: Minors, Perfect Graphs, and the Limits of Coloring

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

The four-color theorem (Chapter 3) is the answer to one question. This chapter is about the questions underneath it. How much structure must a graph have before its chromatic number climbs? Hadwiger's conjecture says: a full clique minor — χ(G) ≥ k forces a K_k you can reach by deleting and contracting edges, a shadow-clique living in the graph's connectivity rather than its edges. Treewidth (Lecture 1) measures how tree-like a graph is, and is the parameter that makes hard problems easy: DP on a decomposition solves coloring, independent set, and a hundred others in linear time on bounded width. List coloring (Lecture 3) asks more of the colorer than the four-color theorem ever did — each vertex brings its own palette — and the guarantees thin out in a way the ordinary theory never signaled: five-list planar is a theorem (Thomassen), four-list planar is false (Voigt's 238-vertex graph). Discharging (Lecture 4) is the technique that tamed planarity: Euler's formula as a currency, degrees as accounts, and the five-color theorem proved in full as the working example, the four-color proof honestly sketched as an industry. Nowhere-zero flows (Lecture 5) are coloring's mirror image — assign values to edges so every vertex balances — and for planar graphs the two theories are literally dual: the flow number of a plane graph is the chromatic number of its dual. Here the chapter is deliberately honest about the frontier: Tutte's 5-flow conjecture (1954) is still a conjecture, machine-verified to n = 6 in this chapter's censuses, while Seymour's 6-flow theorem (1981) is the proved general result. Perfect graphs (Lecture 6) close the book: the class where χ = ω on every induced subgraph, characterized by Lovász's replication argument and the weak perfect graph theorem (both proved here), with the strong perfect graph theorem stated and machine-witnessed in census range.

The deal with the reader is the one the Atlas has enforced since Chapter 1: every number printed below was machine-verified before printing — exhaustive censuses over all labeled graphs to n = 6, exact searches, seeded samplers with recorded seeds — and where a statement is a conjecture, a proof is cited rather than given, or an optimality is beyond the chapter's computational reach, the text says so in those words.

Notation. A graph minor of G is any graph obtained by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and contracting edges; equivalently, H is a minor when G contains |V(H)| pairwise-disjoint nonempty connected vertex sets (branch sets), pairwise adjacent. A tree decomposition of G is a tree T whose nodes are labeled by subsets (bags) B_t ⊆ V(G), such that every vertex of G appears in a connected subtree, every edge of G lies in some bag, and the union covers V(G); its width is max |B_t| − 1 and the treewidth tw(G) is the minimum width over all decompositions. The elimination game: eliminate vertices in an order; when v goes, its remaining neighbors are filled pairwise; the maximum eliminated degree of an order, minimized over all orders, equals tw(G). G is k-choosable if every assignment of k-element lists to vertices admits a proper coloring from the lists; ch(G) is the least such k (similarly ch′ for edges). A nowhere-zero k-flow (integer) orients the edges and labels them with integers of absolute value in 1..k−1 so every vertex has inflow = outflow; a Z_k-flow uses values 1..k−1 with balance mod k; the flow number φ(G) is the least k for which a nowhere-zero k-flow exists. For a plane graph G, G* is its planar dual. G is perfect if every induced subgraph H satisfies χ(H) = ω(H); G∘v (replication) replaces v by two adjacent twins, each adjacent to exactly the old neighbors of v. α, ω, χ, Δ, d(v) are independence number, clique number, chromatic number, maximum degree, and the degree of v.

Lecture 1 · Treewidth: How Tree-like Is This Graph?

A path is easy. A tree is easy. A grid is harder — but only as hard as its width. The idea beneath treewidth is disarmingly simple: if your graph can be laid out as many small bags arranged along a tree, overlapping like roof tiles, then any global question can be answered by sweeping the tree, carrying only a little state per bag.

Tree decompositions, concretely. A tree decomposition of G consists of bags B_t (subsets of vertices, of size at most k+1) arranged as the nodes of a tree T, such that (i) every vertex of G appears in at least one bag; (ii) for every edge uv, some bag contains both u and v; and (iii) for every vertex v, the bags containing v form a connected subtree of T. The width is the largest bag size minus one, and tw(G) is the minimum width. The minus one flatters the singleton: one vertex per bag is width 0, and indeed tw(G) = 0 exactly when G has no edges. Forests are exactly the graphs of treewidth ≤ 1. A cycle needs width 2. A complete graph K_n refuses all compression: its bags must jointly witness every edge, and chasing the connectedness condition forces some bag to contain all n vertices — tw(K_n) = n − 1 (Exercise 2 makes you prove it).

The elimination game. There is a second, game-shaped way to compute the same number. Order the vertices v₁, v₂, …; eliminate them one by one, and each time v is eliminated, connect all its not-yet-eliminated neighbors to one another (the fill). The eliminated degree of v — its degree at the moment of elimination, fill included — depends on the order; the treewidth of G is the minimum, over all elimination orders, of the maximum eliminated degree. (That this equals the bag definition is a classical fact; the chapter's machine computes treewidth exactly this way, sweeping all orders by dynamic programming over subsets.) The two views discipline each other: an elimination order of eliminated-degree ≤ k is read off a width-k decomposition and back.

✎ Checkpoint. Subdivide each edge of K₄ once — every vertex now has degree 2 or 3, the graph is 2-degenerate (repeatedly remove a degree-≤2 vertex and it all comes apart). What is its treewidth? Predict before reading on.

The answer is 3, not 2: subdividing thins the graph's local structure but the K₄ minor survives untouched (contractions undo subdivisions), and treewidth cannot drop below any minor's clique number minus one — tw(K_k) = k − 1, tw(G) ≥ ω(G) − 1 always, and tw(H) ≤ tw(G) whenever H is a minor of G, because contracting and deleting only shrink bags. The machine agrees both ways: tw(subdivided K₄) = 3, degeneracy 2 — the chain runs K₄-minor-free ⟺ treewidth ≤ 2 ⟹ 2-degenerate ⟹ 3-colorable — the first arrow is an equivalence (the biconditional above), and the latter two are strict (the subdivided K₄ is 2-degenerate with treewidth 3; the Petersen graph is 3-colorable but 3-degenerate). The subdivided K₄ is the honest exhibit that the middle arrow cannot be reversed: 2-degenerate does not imply K₄-minor-free. (The census receipts record the same lesson from the other side: every 2-degenerate graph on at most 6 vertices that lacked a K₄ minor also had treewidth ≤ 2 — zero violations of the forward chain, all 33,867 graphs.)

What treewidth buys: dynamic programming. Fix a decomposition of width k and root it. For each bag, the subgraph below it hangs off the rest of the graph only through the ≤ k+1 bag vertices — a separator. So the answers to optimization problems can be assembled bottom-up, one bag at a time, tabulating how each interface state (a coloring of the bag, a membership pattern for the independent set) extends downward. Two Atlas-lab verifications make this concrete rather than folkloric: on nine named graphs the machine built optimal-order decompositions (nine valid: width = tw, every vertex covered, every edge covered, running intersection property holding, unique root) and computed, on those decompositions, the independence number α and the count of proper 3-colorings by pure bag-DP — identical, digit for digit, to brute force: α = 1, 2, 2, 2, 4, 3, 5, 3, 8 for K₄, C₅, octahedron, wheel W₅, Petersen, grid 2×3, grid 3×3, icosahedron, grid 4×4 respectively, and 3-coloring counts 30, 6, 120, 54, 246 for C₅, octahedron, Petersen, grid 2×3, grid 3×3 (K₄, W₅ and the icosahedron admit none — they are the 0 column). The point is not the nine numbers; it is that the method is mechanical and its correctness auditable on small cases where brute force is available as an oracle.

The grid theorem, honestly stated. Treewidth ≤ k is a promise of algorithmic tractability. The deep theorem of Robertson–Seymour graph structure theory says the promise has a structural converse: every graph of large treewidth contains a large grid minor — and modern quantitative forms (Chekuri–Chuzhoy; Chuzhoy) push the required treewidth down to a polynomial in the grid size k. So "algorithmically hard structure" and "contains a big grid" are the same statement up to polynomial overhead. Beneath it lies the discipline's crown: the graph minor theorem — in any infinite list of graphs, one is a minor of another; equivalently every minor-closed family is characterized by finitely many excluded minors (Robertson–Seymour, Graph Minors XX, 2004, proving Wagner's conjecture). Planarity is the ancestral example: by Wagner's 1937 theorem, the K₅/K₃,₃-excluded family and the plane embeddable family coincide. This chapter uses the theory's language (minors, branch sets, excluded families) but proves only what it prints; the big structure theorems are cited, with the census range as the small-scale laboratory.

The minor oracle, built honestly. "Does G contain a K_k minor?" is a search for k disjoint connected branch sets, pairwise adjacent. The chapter's machine does exactly that as a direct model search — and the receipt records why: the first-draft oracle (contract an edge, recurse) produced false negatives on the Petersen graph, whose K₅ minor is a book classic. The replacement search found the textbook witness, branch set by branch set: {0,1}, {2,3}, {4,9}, {5,7}, {6,8} — five disjoint connected pairs, every pair adjacent, hand-checked edge by edge. Another witness worth carrying: the 3×3 grid contains a K₄ minor with branch sets {0,1}, {2,5}, {3,6,7,8}, {4} — the center vertex 4 stands alone while its ring of eight vertices supplies three more branch sets. Contraction is violent and lossy; branch-set thinking is how humans and machines both should see minors.

Lecture 2 · Hadwiger's Conjecture: Coloring Forces Shadow-Cliques

The four-color theorem says a planar graph with chromatic number 4 exists. Hadwiger's 1943 conjecture says what any graph with χ ≥ k must contain: not a subgraph K_k — that is false (the wheel W₅ is 4-chromatic and planar with ω = 2: no K₄ subgraph, though its K₄ minor is machine-witnessed) — but a minor K_k: a clique you can recover by deletions and contractions, the way the 3×3 grid hides its K₄. Formally: χ(G) ≥ k ⟹ G contains K_k as a minor. Equivalently: Hadwiger k fails exactly when some K_k-minor-free graph needs k colors.

The small cases, proved in full.

k = 1: trivial. k = 2: a graph with no K₂ minor has no edges, and needs one color. k = 3: K₃-minor-free graphs are exactly the forests. Why: a cycle contracts (delete the rest of the graph, contract around the cycle) to a triangle — so any cycle yields a K₃ minor — and a forest, acyclic, contains no cycle to contract. So χ ≥ 3 means a non-forest, means a cycle, means a K₃ minor. (The census receipts carry a trap from this neighborhood: "K₃ minor ⟺ non-bipartite" is false — 88 bipartite graphs on ≤ 5 vertices contain a K₃ minor, because contraction manufactures odd cycles out of even ones. The correct biconditional is K₃-minor ⟺ not-a-forest, zero violations in 1,099 graphs.)

k = 4 — the case Hadwiger himself proved in the founding paper, and this chapter proves too: every K₄-minor-free graph is 3-colorable. The route: (1) a 2-connected K₄-minor-free graph has a vertex of degree 2. (2) Delete it, 3-color the rest by induction, restore it — its two neighbors use at most two colors, a third is always free. For (1), the honest route runs through Lecture 1's machinery: K₄-minor-free ⟺ treewidth ≤ 2 (the biconditional, census-verified in both directions), treewidth ≤ 2 ⟹ 2-degenerate (every subgraph of a width-2 chordal completion has a simplicial vertex of degree at most 2; degeneracy ≤ treewidth, zero census violations), and 2-connectivity excludes degree ≤ 1 — so a degree-2 vertex exists, and induction closes the theorem. (The fan-flavored sketch this paragraph first carried — an induced cycle plus a vertex welded on by two disjoint paths — was unsound: two attachments build only a theta, and a theta is K₄-minor-free, so the promised contradiction never arrives; the machine's trap exhibit, a C₅ with one extra vertex joined to two of its vertices, is 2-connected, K₄-minor-free and theta-shaped — the census says all 3,810 2-connected K₄-minor-free graphs on 6 vertices carry a degree-2 vertex, but for the reason above, not that one.) The lemma structure (find a low vertex, remove, extend the coloring) is exactly the five-color theorem's engine in Lecture 4 — Hadwiger k = 4 and Heawood's five-color theorem are the same machine pointed at different structure.

The frontier, stated honestly. Hadwiger k = 5 is equivalent to the four-color theorem — Wagner's 1937 result: K₅-minor-free graphs split into planar pieces glued along cliques of at most 2 vertices (the Wagner graph W being the one exotic piece), and the gluing preserves 4-colorability, so 4CT ⟹ Hadwiger 5, and the smallest counterexample to Hadwiger 5 would be a non-4-colorable K₅-minor-free graph, which by Wagner's decomposition would have to be... planar. So: Hadwiger 5 ⟺ 4CT (proved, both directions, modulo the 1976 computer proof and its 1997 RSST simplification). Hadwiger k = 6 is a theorem: Robertson–Seymour–Thomas (1993), relying on 4CT. Hadwiger k ≥ 7 is open — every k, at 7 and beyond, is conjecture. The chapter's machine can only patrol the bottom floor: it verified Hadwiger k = 4 on every labeled graph through n = 6 — at n = 6, all 32,768 graphs swept, 5,214 with χ ≥ 4, every single one carrying a K₄ minor (receipt-recorded; the same census at n ≤ 5 found 67 such graphs, again all clean). That is a floor, not evidence for the open cases, and the text says so.

Why minors, why conjecture, why believe it. Minors are the right notion because they are exactly what coloring cannot see past: contractions can raise chromatic number (contract a long even cycle's perfect matching: an odd cycle appears, χ jumps 2 → 3), but a K_k minor already needs k colors — χ(G) ≥ χ(minor) ≥ k — so the conjecture is tight in the direction it claims. It is believed because every precedent proved true and because structure theory keeps delivering the pieces: excluded-minor families have bounded, describable structure, and Hadwiger's statement is exactly the claim that K_k-minor-free structure is (k−1)-colorable. For k = 4 you just watched that be a theorem. Where it stops being one, this book stops proving and starts citing.

✎ Checkpoint. The Petersen graph has χ = 3. Hadwiger k = 3 predicts a K₃ minor; k = 4 predicts a K₄ minor (since χ = 3 < 4, it predicts nothing); what is the largest k with a guaranteed K_k minor — i.e., what does the conjecture not claim here? Predict the largest clique minor of the Petersen graph before reading the receipt's answer: it is K₅ (Lecture 1's witness) — Hadwiger is not sharp on every graph, and the Petersen graph is its classic exhibit of slack: 3 colors suffice, yet a 5-clique minor hides inside. Slack is the conjecture's mystery, in both directions.

Lecture 3 · List Coloring: When the Palettes Are Private

Ordinary coloring hands every vertex the same box of k crayons. List coloring hands vertex v a private list L(v) of k colors — maybe all different boxes at every vertex — and asks for a proper coloring with c(v) ∈ L(v). The graph is k-choosable if this is possible for every choice of lists; ch(G) is the least such k. Three facts anchor the theory, and the first two are one-liners.

ch(G) ≥ χ(G) — a proper coloring from identical lists is an ordinary coloring. ch(K_n) = n — assign the n vertices the same (n−1)-list and no color escapes; conversely greedy never needs more than the list size. So choosability refines chromatic number, and the refinement can be strict. The chapter's exhibit is the smallest one in the literature: K₂,₄ is not 2-choosable, even though χ(K₂,₄) = 2.

Here is the witness, exactly as the machine found it (the lexicographically-first failing assignment among all one million two-list assignments on the 5-color palette — 720 of the million fail, and this is the first): the two a-side vertices carry lists {1,2} and {3,4}; the four b-side vertices carry {1,3}, {1,4}, {2,3}, {2,4}. Try to color. Each a-vertex has two options; each b-vertex is forbidden its two list-mates' colors on the a-side... work the case analysis (Exercise 6 walks you through it): every choice collapses. Meanwhile K₂,₃ is 2-choosable — the machine checked all 100,000 two-list assignments, zero failures — and ch(K₂,₄) = 3 exactly: all 1,000,000 three-list assignments are choosable (two half-million sweeps). The complete bipartite graph is the standard laboratory of choosability because the two sides interact only through forbidden pairs — the problem becomes a hitting question about small sets, and the boundary between K₂,₃ and K₂,₄ is the boundary itself.

The degeneracy guarantee (and why order matters). If G is k-degenerate — every subgraph has a vertex of degree ≤ k — then G is (k+1)-choosable: color vertices in reverse peel order; when v is colored, at most k neighbors are already colored, so at least one of its k+1 list colors is free. No backtracking, ever. The lab receipts record a practical coda: the machine first colored in forward peel order and occasionally thrashed — hub vertices in stacked triangulations accumulated many colored neighbors before their turn, forcing search — while reverse-peel ran backtrack-free, 41 search nodes exactly, on all 300 stacked-40 samples. Correctness was never in question (search returns a coloring either way); the lesson is that a provable greedy order is also the engineering order.

The two theorems that define the planar frontier. Thomassen (1994): every planar graph is 5-choosable. The proof is a two-list induction on plane triangulations with precolored path boundaries — among the cleanest induction shapes in the field — and this chapter states it with proof cited, not given (the machine's contribution is the census floor: 1,500 seeded five-list samples across the octahedron, icosahedron, wheel W₅, 3×3 grid, and a 40-vertex stacked triangulation — 300 each, lists drawn uniformly from the 56 five-subsets of an 8-color palette, seed 777333 — all 1,500 choosable, zero failures). Galvin (1995): every bipartite multigraph has ch′(G) = Δ(G) — the list-coloring edge theorem that settles the 1979 Dinitz problem. Galvin's engine is worth naming because it is a pattern card of its own: orient the edges by a stable matching (hearts of the bipartite structure), so that every edge's out-neighborhood is kernel-perfect, and greedily color the kernels. The lab's small prints: exhaustive edge-list checks on C₄ (10,000 assignments) and C₆ (1,000,000 assignments) with Δ = 2 lists found zero failures, and 2,000 sampled three-list assignments on K₃,₃ (Δ = 3) all succeeded, seed 20261012.

The honest edge. Five lists suffice for every planar graph; four do not. Voigt (1993) constructed a planar graph on 238 vertices that is not 4-choosable — the counterexample that bounds Thomassen's theorem from below — and Voigt–Wirth (1997) showed even 3-colorable planar graphs can fail 4-choosability. (A 63-vertex example due to Mirzakhani is the smaller classic; the 238 is the publication that settled non-4-choosability first — both are cited, neither is reproduced here.) The gap between 4 and 5 — one color of daylight between theorem and counterexample — is the state of the art, and the chapter keeps it honest: choosability's planar story is not the four-color story wearing a hat; it is a different theory with a different (and still one-off-tight) frontier.

Lecture 4 · Discharging: Euler's Formula as Currency

Every planar graph obeys n − m + f = 2 (Euler). Read on the triangulation — every face a triangle, m = 3n − 6, f = 2n − 4 — the formula becomes a statement about degrees, and degrees are where the colorer lives.

The degree budget, derived and machine-audited. Sum the face-edge incidences on a triangulation: 3f = 2m, so with Euler, Σ_v (6 − d(v)) = 6n − 2m = 6n − (6n − 12) = 12. Twelve units of "credit" are minted, no more, no less, on every triangulation of every order. The chapter's lab verified this the only trustworthy way — not once, but step by step on growing stacked triangulations (start from a triangle; each step splits a face by a new degree-3 vertex): at n = 300, three seeds, every intermediate n carried Σ(6 − d(v)) = 12 exactly, with m = 3n − 6 and f = 2n − 4 holding at every step and min degree exactly 3 (each new vertex arrives with degree 3 and old degrees only grow — the identity is a telescope in disguise, and the machine watched it not wobble).

The five-color theorem (Heawood 1890), proved in full. Every planar graph is 5-colorable.

Proof. Induct on n. A minimal counterexample G is 6-critical, so we may take G a near-triangulation (add edges to triangulate; more edges only harden coloring). The budget forces a vertex v with d(v) ≤ 5: if all degrees were ≥ 6, Σ(6 − d(v)) ≤ 0 < 12. If d(v) ≤ 4, delete v, color by induction, extend — at most 4 neighbors, 5 colors, one is free. So the load-bearing case is d(v) = 5 with neighbors v₁, …, v₅ in cyclic order, using all five colors (else a free color extends). Consider the two colors on v₁ and v₃; if no v₁–v₃ Kempe chain — no two-color path joining v₁ to v₃ through the subgraph of those two colors — swap the two colors on v₁'s chain component and free v₁'s color for v. The chain must be absent in one of the two directions: a v₁–v₃ chain of colors {1,3} and a v₂–v₄ chain of colors {2,4} would have to cross, and planarity forbids it (one chain lives inside the v-cycle wedge v₁v₂v₃, the other inside v₃v₄v₁, and they cannot touch). So one swap always frees a color. ∎

Every ingredient of the four-color proof is visible in miniature: the degree budget producing a reducible configuration (d ≤ 5), and Kempe chains trading colors at a distance. What fails at four colors is precisely the Kempe argument's bookkeeping: with a degree-5 vertex and only 4 colors, two chains can both be present and an innocent-looking swap can corrupt a distant region — Heawood himself found the flaw in Kempe's 1879 "proof" this way, eleven years after the journal published it. The four-color theorem, when Appel and Haken closed it in 1976 (simplified by Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas 1997), is this same machine at industrial scale: a discharging argument proves every minimal counterexample must contain one of an unavoidable set of 1,936 configurations (RSST: 633), and a computer case-checks each configuration reducible. The proof shape is in this lecture; the execution is a cited citation.

The light edge, the engine of the modern proof. The budget mints 12 credits; a vertex of degree d "consumes" 6 − d of them, and d ≥ 7 vertices are net payers (a 6-vertex breaks even). Discharging formalizes this: assign charges, move them along edges by local rules, and conclude some local configuration must exist — in a triangulation of minimum degree 5, some edge uv has d(u) + d(v) ≤ 11. Proof, by discharging. Suppose every edge had d(u) + d(v) ≥ 12. Then no edge joins two 5-vertices (weight 10) and none joins a 5-vertex to a 6-vertex (weight 11); the budget Σ(6 − d(v)) = 12 > 0 still forces a 5-vertex to exist, and every one of its neighbors has degree ≥ 7. Discharge: each 5-vertex sends 1/5 to every neighbor. A vertex w of degree d ≥ 7 receives at most ⌊d/2⌋/5 ≤ d/10 in total — its 5-neighbors are pairwise non-adjacent (a 5–5 edge is forbidden) and they sit on w's neighbor cycle, so there are at most ⌊d/2⌋ of them — and ends at no more than (6 − d) + d/10 = 6 − 0.9d < 0; a 6-vertex receives nothing (5–6 edges are forbidden) and stays at 0; a 5-vertex gives away exactly its +1 and receives nothing (its neighbors are not 5-vertices), ending at exactly 0. Every vertex ends ≤ 0 and the forced 5-vertex's neighbors end strictly negative, so the total is < 0 — but charge is conserved, so the total is Σ(6 − d(v)) = 12. Contradiction. ∎ The receipt's samples show the bound is met, not beaten: light edges of weight exactly 11 occur — the pentakis dodecahedron (the C₆₀ fullerene's dual: twelve degree-5 apexes over the dodecahedron's twenty faces, plus twenty degree-6 rim vertices) carries all sixty apex–rim edges at weight exactly 11 and thirty rim edges at 12, none lighter (machine-verified) — wheels carry rim edges of weight 6 (W₃ = K₄, all edges 6; spokes run heavier: W₄ 7, W₅ 8), stacked triangulations 7, the octahedron 8, the icosahedron 10. The light edge is the 4CT engine's piston: reducibility proofs attach to light configurations, unavoidability comes from the charge — and the proof above is that unavoidability engine in miniature; the lab holds the exhibits.

Lecture 5 · Nowhere-Zero Flows: Coloring's Mirror

Turn the picture around. Instead of coloring vertices, orient the edges and write a nonzero integer on each, demanding that flow conserves at every vertex: inflow = outflow. The labels must come from ±1, …, ±(k−1) — nowhere zero is the point, a zero label is an edge you might as well delete. If such a labeling exists, G carries a nowhere-zero k-flow, and the flow number φ(G) is the least such k. A loopless graph with a bridge can never carry one — the bridge must carry the entire flow of its component, forcing zero — so the theory's home is the bridgeless (2-edge-connected) graphs.

First equivalences, by hand. φ(G) = 2 happens exactly when every degree is even: a nowhere-zero 2-flow uses only ±1, so at each vertex the number of in-edges must equal the number of out-edges — the graph is Eulerian, and an Eulerian orientation (split each vertex's incident edges half-in, half-out along an Euler tour) is the flow. This is why the flow table's strangest-looking entry is K₅ → 2: five vertices, ten edges, every degree 4 — Eulerian — a 2-flow with no effort at all, the same number as a triangle's... no, a triangle is degree 2 throughout and also Eulerian: φ(C₃) = 2 as well. Flow number ignores cliquishness entirely; it tracks parity structure. The other extreme: the Petersen graph has φ = 5, and no nowhere-zero 4-flow exists — the machine proved the negative by exhaustive search over all assignments — and the classic witness 5-flow is printed edge by edge in the lab (outer pentagon and spokes labeled [1, −1, 1, −1, 2, 1, 2, −2, 2, −3] and the inner star [4, 1, 2, 3, −1] in the recorded edge order — Exercise 10's balance table; every vertex balances, every label in ±1..±4; the arrays this paragraph first carried were compacted-session garble and died in the F6 scar, the receipt's correction class). The Petersen graph is to flows what the four-critical planar graphs are to coloring: the boundary case at the bottom of the conjecture ladder.

Modular flows and Tutte's lift. A Z_k-flow is the same demand with balance checked mod k, labels in 1..k−1; a mod-k orientation orients edges so every vertex has indegree ≡ outdegree (mod k). Tutte proved these are the same theory: Z_k-flow ⟺ integer nowhere-zero k-flow, and (for k = 3, the case that matters in applications) Z₃-flow ⟺ mod-3 orientability. The lift's hard direction (integer ⟸ modular) is a tightening argument that rounds a fractional residue into an exact one; it is this chapter's ◆ Problem 2, with the lab receipts as its empirical floor: on every named graph of this chapter and k ∈ {3, 4, 5}, the machine verified the equivalence directly (Z_k-flow found ⟺ integer flow found), and mod-3-orientation ⟺ Z₃-flow on all the named graphs with at most 16 edges.

Planar duality: one object, two theories. For a plane graph G with dual G*, every edge of G crosses exactly one edge of G*, and a moment's staring at the picture shows: a proper k-coloring of G*'s vertices is a nowhere-zero k-flow on G, and back — Tait's observation, in modern dress: φ(G) = χ(G*) for every bridgeless plane G. A k-flow on G is a k-color-compatible tension on the dual and vice versa: color the dual's vertices so adjacent vertices differ, subtract along crossed edges (properness is nowhere-zeroness; telescoping around each face of G* is conservation at the vertex of G). The lab's seven embedded samples verify the identity on both self-duals and dual pairs: K₄ (self-dual, 4 = 4), the wheel W₅ (self-dual, 4 = 4), C₄ (2 = 2), the octahedron/cube pair (2 = 2 — the octahedron is Eulerian and its dual the cube is bipartite: even degrees dualize to even face sizes), the prism/bipyramid pair (4 = 4), the icosahedron/dodecahedron pair (3 = 3), and a 6-vertex stacked triangulation (3 = 3) — with Euler n − m + f = 2 re-verified on every embedding. Two consequences worth pausing on: the four-color theorem is a flow statement — χ(G*) ≤ 4 for every plane G* is φ(G) ≤ 4 for every bridgeless plane G, Tait's 1880 route; and flow numbers are the right invariant for nonplanar generalization, because duals stop existing but flows do not.

The honest ledger of the flow conjectures. This is where the chapter refuses to decorate. Tutte's 5-flow conjecture (1954): every bridgeless graph has a nowhere-zero 5-flow — OPEN. The chapter's census receipts patrol the small world: all 264 connected bridgeless graphs on ≤ 5 vertices have φ ∈ {2, 3, 4} (histogram 42 / 191 / 31), and all 11,968 bridgeless graphs on 6 vertices likewise (720 / 9,961 / 1,287) — zero need k = 5, and the Petersen graph at n = 10 shows the conjecture's bound is tight, since its flow number is exactly 5. What is proved: Seymour's 6-flow theorem (1981) — every bridgeless graph carries a nowhere-zero 6-flow — the general result the field actually owns, proved by a clever three-edge-case induction this chapter cites without proof. The 4-flow conjecture (Tutte 1966): every graph with no Petersen minor carries a nowhere-zero 4-flow — a statement strictly generalizing the four-color theorem (a planar graph has no Petersen minor) — still open in general, with major settled cases (the cubic case is known). The 3-flow conjecture (Tutte): every 4-edge-connected graph has a nowhere-zero 3-flow — still open; the 6-edge-connected case is a theorem (Lovász–Thomassen–Wu–Zhang, J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 103 (2013), 587–598). The lecture's table for small named graphs (K₄ → 4, K₃,₃ → 3, octahedron → 2, cube → 3, triangular prism → 4, wheel W₅ → 4, icosahedron → 3) is machine-computed; every statement about k ≥ 5 in the unbounded world is conjecture, and says so, in those words.

Lecture 6 · Perfect Graphs: Where Coloring and Cliques Agree

Coloring asks for partitions; cliques ask for witnesses. On every graph, ω ≤ χ — a clique needs its own colors. The gap between them is the graph's structural debt, and most of this book (Hadwiger, flows, discharging) studies graphs where the debt is real. Perfect graphs are the class with none: G is perfect if every induced subgraph H satisfies χ(H) = ω(H) — the induced-subgraph demand is what makes the class hereditary and the theory deep.

First examples, first counterexample. Bipartite graphs are perfect (χ = ω = 2 on every induced subgraph, which stays bipartite). Cycles of even length: perfect. Interval graphs, comparability graphs: perfect (both cited to their standard proofs). And then the smallest failure, the hole: C₅ has χ = 3 > 2 = ω. The induced-subgraph clause now pays: C₅'s proper induced subgraphs are paths and singletons — all perfect — so C₅ is minimal imperfect: every proper induced subgraph is honest, the whole is not. The machine certified exactly this (χ = 3, ω = 2, α = 2 on C₅; all 30 proper induced subgraphs perfect), and counted the damage at census scale: among all 1,024 labeled graphs on 5 vertices, precisely 12 are imperfect — the 12 labelings of C₅, no others — and among all 32,768 on 6 vertices, 1,944, every one containing an induced C₅ on five of its vertices. Imperfection, at this scale, is exactly the hole.

Lovász's characterization: α·ω ≥ n. What does the C₅ failure look like numerically? α = 2, ω = 2, and α·ω = 4 < 5 = n. Lovász's theorem (1972) says that inequality is imperfection: G is perfect ⟺ every induced subgraph H of G satisfies α(H)·ω(H) ≥ |V(H)|. Reading it forward: if H is perfect, χ(H) = ω(H) colors partition H into χ(H) = ω(H) independent sets, and the largest has size ≥ n/ω; but any independent set has size ≤ α, so α·ω ≥ n. (The proof is Exercise 12's route for the replication lemma; the converse — the inequality on every induced subgraph forcing perfection — is the hard direction.) The converse — α·ω ≥ n on every induced subgraph forces perfection — is the hard direction, and it is where replication enters. The census receipts patrol the whole small world: on all 33,867 graphs through n = 6, the biconditional held with zero violations — every imperfect graph has an induced subgraph with α·ω < n (C₅ and its kin), every perfect graph has none.

Replication, the engine of the theory. Given G and a vertex v, form G∘v: replace v by two adjacent twins v₁, v₂, each carrying exactly v's old neighborhood. Two facts, one true and one false, and the receipts keep them straight. True — the replication lemma (Lovász): G is perfect ⟺ G∘v is perfect. The proof runs the α·ω ≥ n characterization through the duplication: twins let you weight, and the characterization is exactly what weights respect (Exercise 12). False — the tempting side-formula χ(G∘v) = max(χ(G), d(v)+1): the census tested all 285 replications of every graph on ≤ 4 vertices and it fails on 167 of them; the smallest witness is K₂ (replicate either endpoint: a triangle, χ = 3, while max(χ, d+1) = 2). The one-schoolbook case that does satisfy it — P₃ with its middle vertex replicated, which yields K₄ minus an edge with χ = 3 = max(2, 3) — is a coincidence of that one shape, not a theorem. The machine's certified true bounds: χ(G∘v) ∈ [χ(G), χ(G)+1] always (color the twin with a fresh color), and perfection is exactly preserved.

The weak perfect graph theorem, proved by replication. WPGT (Lovász 1972): G is perfect ⟺ the complement of G is perfect. The proof route this chapter follows is Lovász's own, and it starts from Fulkerson's 1971 observation that the statement would follow if replication preserved anti-perfection — the details are ◆ Problem 3, and the answer walks the full route: the α·ω characterization is self-complementary in just the right way (α and ω swap under complementation, so the inequality is complement-invariant), replication substitutes weights, and WPGT falls out. The machine's census floor: perfect(G) ⟺ perfect(complement) verified on all 33,867 graphs through n = 6, zero violations — and the small world's complement exhibits are printed in the lab (C₅'s complement is C₅ again — the pentagram; and the octahedron's complement is a perfect matching on six vertices — both sides of each mirror perfect, as the theorem demands).

✎ Checkpoint. Predict before reading: is the complement of C₇ another C₇? The pentagon taught you holes and antiholes rhyme. The answer is no, and the machine caught the working assumption before it reached the page: C̄₇ is 4-regular (each vertex non-adjacent to itself and two cycle-neighbors, adjacent to the other four), while C₇ is 2-regular — they are not isomorphic at a glance at any degree sequence. In fact C̄ₙ ≅ Cₙ holds only at n = 5. But C̄₇ is still imperfect — χ(C̄₇) = 4 > 3 = ω — and still minimal imperfect (all proper induced subgraphs perfect, α·ω = 6 < 7). The antihole is the complement's own boundary case, and it is a genuinely different graph from the hole except in the one dimension where C₅ is self-complementary.

The strong perfect graph theorem, stated. Four decades of conjecture, closed by Chudnovsky–Robertson–Seymour–Thomas (Annals, 2006): G is perfect ⟺ G contains no induced odd hole and no induced odd antihole (no induced C_{2k+1}, k ≥ 2, and no induced complement of one). The census receipts are the theorem's small-scale shadow: through n = 6, "imperfect" and "contains an induced C₅" are equivalent (1,944 + 12 graphs, zero exceptions either way) — because the smallest odd hole is C₅ and the smallest odd antihole beyond it is C̄₇ on 7 vertices, both out of census range, and C̄₅ ≅ C₅. At n = 7 the world would start distinguishing holes from antiholes (the lab Part 6's antihole unit) — the census stops honestly at 6 and the theorem is cited, not proved. Recognition is polynomial (Chudnovsky et al. 2005), closing the class as algorithmically tame; the theta graphs you met in Chapter 8 as spectral exhibits live here as the classic family the SPGT had to account for.

Why perfect graphs close this chapter. Hadwiger says χ's growth forces minors; flows say edge-labeling balances mirror coloring; discharging says planarity taxes degrees; perfection says: on a class defined by forbidden induced subgraphs — holes and antiholes — coloring never lies. The induced-subgraph world (perfection) and the minor world (Hadwiger, treewidth) are the two honest regimes where structure and chromatic number meet exactly, and the gap between them — graphs that are neither — is where the open problems all live.

Answer 2. (a) Upper: the single bag V(K_n) is a decomposition of width n − 1. Lower: in any decomposition, each clique vertex v owns a connected subtree T_v of bags containing v; for every pair u, v of clique vertices some bag contains both, so the subtrees pairwise intersect; by subtree Helly (prove it: root the tree, each pairwise-intersecting subtree family has the bag of minimum depth common to all — a one-paragraph induction), all n subtrees share one bag, which contains all of K_n: width ≥ n − 1. (b) Bags of size ≤ 2: contract each bag that is an edge to a tree edge; G's every edge lies in some bag, so E(G) is a subset of the decomposition tree's edge set — G is a forest. Conversely, for a path v₁…v_k take bags {v₁}, {v₁,v₂}, {v₂}, {v₂,v₃}, {v₃}, … (alternating singletons and edges) — width 1; a general forest decomposes component by component, joined into one tree at arbitrary bag nodes.

Answer 3. k = 2: χ ≥ 2 means G has an edge, and an edge is a K₂ minor. For the biconditional: if G contains a cycle C, delete every vertex and edge outside C, then contract all but three of C's edges — three vertices remain, joined in a triangle: a K₃ minor. If G is a forest, every minor of a forest is a forest (deletion and contraction preserve acyclicity: contracting an edge of a forest cannot create a cycle — a new cycle would lift to a closed walk in the original), and K₃ is not a forest — no K₃ minor. So K₃-minor ⟺ non-forest, and χ ≥ 3 ⟺ non-bipartite... no: χ ≥ 3 means not 2-colorable, which for forests fails — every forest is 2-colorable — so χ ≥ 3 forces a cycle, forces a K₃ minor. (The trap the census caught: bipartite ≠ forest. K₂,₃ is bipartite and carries a K₃ minor.)

Answer 4. Connectivity of the branch sets: 0–1 is an outer edge, 2–3 outer, 4–9 spoke, 5–7 star, 6–8 star — all five connected. The ten adjacency checks: {0,1}–{2,3} via 1–2 (outer); {0,1}–{4,9} via 0–4 (outer); {0,1}–{5,7} via 0–5 (spoke); {0,1}–{6,8} via 1–6 (spoke); {2,3}–{4,9} via 3–4 (outer); {2,3}–{5,7} via 2–7 (spoke); {2,3}–{6,8} via 3–8 (spoke); {4,9}–{5,7} via 7–9 (star); {4,9}–{6,8} via 6–9 (star); {5,7}–{6,8} via 5–8 (star). The Petersen graph, chromatic number 3, carries a 5-clique minor — Hadwiger's conjecture is not tight, and this is its classic exhibit of slack.

Answer 5. ch(G) ≥ χ(G): identical lists are a legal list assignment, and a proper coloring from them is an ordinary coloring — so if every k-list assignment were colorable, G would be k-colorable. Contrapositively: the identical lists of any χ(G) − 1 colors admit no proper coloring at all, so no list size below χ(G) suffices. ch(K_n) = n: the same (n−1)-list everywhere forces two adjacent vertices onto the same color by pigeonhole (n vertices, n−1 colors, adjacency complete), so ch ≥ n; conversely, color vertices in any order — each vertex's list has n colors, at most n−1 are blocked by earlier neighbors: ch ≤ n.

Answer 6. (a) If L(a₁) ∩ L(a₂) ∋ c: color both a-vertices c (they are not adjacent — the a-side of a complete bipartite graph is independent); every b-vertex loses at most one color, keeping at least one — done. If the lists are disjoint, L(a₁) = {p, q}, L(a₂) = {r, s}: there are four candidate pairs (c₁, c₂); a b-vertex with list {x, y} is stuck only if {c₁, c₂} = {x, y} — each b-list rules out exactly one candidate pair. Three b-vertices rule out at most three of the four pairs: some pair survives, colors the a-side, and leaves every b-vertex a free color. K₂,₃ is 2-choosable. (b) The machine's witness: b-lists {1,3}, {1,4}, {2,3}, {2,4} with a-lists {1,2} and {3,4} — the four pairs (1,3), (1,4), (2,3), (2,4) are ruled out one per b-vertex, all four gone: no coloring exists, ch(K₂,₄) ≥ 3. And ch(K₂,₄) = 3: with 3-lists, the a-side (any pair) rules out at most... each a-color choice kills b-lists contained in it; a 2-subset of a 3-list leaves one color, so a b-vertex is stuck only if its entire 3-list is contained in the two a-colors — impossible for a 3-list against 2 colors. Every assignment colors. // f(S) = min over v not in S of max(cost(S,v), f(S ∪ {v})); tw = f(∅).
// cost(S,v) = the number of present vertices reachable from v through the eliminated cloud
// — the eliminated degree WITH FILL: neighbors reachable only through already-eliminated
// vertices join the bag.
function tw(n:number, adj:number[]):number{
  const N=1<<n, memo=new Float64Array(N).fill(-1); memo[N-1]=0;
  const cost=(S:number,v:number)=>{ // BFS from v through ELIMINATED vertices, collecting present ones
    const stack=[v]; let vis=1<<v, bag=1<<v;
    while(stack.length){const u=stack.pop()!;
      for(let w=0;w<n;w++) if((adj[u]>>w)&1){
        if((S&(1<<w))===0) bag|=1<<w;                         // present neighbor: joins the bag
        else if((vis&(1<<w))===0){vis|=1<<w; stack.push(w);}  // eliminated: traverse through it
      }}
    // eliminated degree = present vertices in the bag (fill included) minus v
    let deg=0; for(let w=0;w<n;w++) if(w!==v&&(bag&(1<<w)))deg++; return deg;};
  const f=(S:number):number=>{ if(memo[S]>=0)return memo[S]; let best=Infinity;
    for(let v=0;v<n;v++) if((S&(1<<v))===0) best=Math.min(best,Math.max(cost(S,v),f(S|1<<v)));
    return memo[S]=best; };
  return f(0);
}
