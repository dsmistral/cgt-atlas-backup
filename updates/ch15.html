Chapter 15 · Graph Structure: Minors, Perfect Graphs, and the Limits of Coloring

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

The four-color theorem (Chapter 3) is the answer to one question. This chapter is about the questions underneath it. How much structure must a graph have before its chromatic number climbs? Hadwiger's conjecture says: a full clique minor — $\chi(G) \ge k$ forces a $K_k$ you can reach by deleting and contracting edges, a shadow-clique living in the graph's connectivity rather than its edges. Treewidth (Lecture 1) measures how tree-like a graph is, and is the parameter that makes hard problems easy: DP on a decomposition solves coloring, independent set, and a hundred others in linear time on bounded width. List coloring (Lecture 3) asks more of the colorer than the four-color theorem ever did — each vertex brings its own palette — and the guarantees thin out in a way the ordinary theory never signaled: five-list planar is a theorem (Thomassen), four-list planar is false (Voigt's $238$-vertex graph). Discharging (Lecture 4) is the technique that tamed planarity: Euler's formula as a currency, degrees as accounts, and the five-color theorem proved in full as the working example, the four-color proof honestly sketched as an industry. Nowhere-zero flows (Lecture 5) are coloring's mirror image — assign values to edges so every vertex balances — and for planar graphs the two theories are literally dual: the flow number of a plane graph is the chromatic number of its dual. Here the chapter is deliberately honest about the frontier: Tutte's $5$-flow conjecture (1954) is still a conjecture, machine-verified to $n = 6$ in this chapter's censuses, while Seymour's $6$-flow theorem (1981) is the proved general result. Perfect graphs (Lecture 6) close the book: the class where $\chi = \omega$ on every induced subgraph, characterized by Lovász's replication argument and the weak perfect graph theorem (both proved here), with the strong perfect graph theorem stated and machine-witnessed in census range.

The deal with the reader is the one the Atlas has enforced since Chapter 1: every number printed below was machine-verified before printing — exhaustive censuses over all nonempty labeled graphs to $n = 6$, exact searches, seeded samplers with recorded seeds — and where a statement is a conjecture, a proof is cited rather than given, or an optimality is beyond the chapter's computational reach, the text says so in those words.

Notation. A graph minor of $G$ is any graph obtained by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and contracting edges; equivalently, $H$ is a minor when $G$ contains $|V(H)|$ pairwise-disjoint nonempty connected vertex sets (branch sets), pairwise adjacent. A tree decomposition of $G$ is a tree $T$ whose nodes are labeled by subsets (bags) $B_t \subseteq V(G)$, such that every vertex of $G$ appears in a connected subtree, every edge of $G$ lies in some bag, and the union covers $V(G)$; its width is $\max_t |B_t| - 1$ and the treewidth $\operatorname{tw}(G)$ is the minimum width over all decompositions. The elimination game: eliminate vertices in an order; when $v$ goes, its remaining neighbors are filled pairwise; the maximum eliminated degree of an order, minimized over all orders, equals $\operatorname{tw}(G)$. $G$ is $k$-choosable if every assignment of $k$-element lists to vertices admits a proper coloring from the lists; $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ is the least such $k$ (similarly $\operatorname{ch}'$ for edges). A nowhere-zero $k$-flow (integer) orients the edges and labels them with integers of absolute value in $\{1, \dots, k-1\}$ so every vertex has $\operatorname{inflow} = \operatorname{outflow}$; a $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow uses values in $\{1, \dots, k-1\}$ with balance $\bmod k$; the flow number $\varphi(G)$ is the least $k$ for which a nowhere-zero $k$-flow exists. For a plane graph $G$, $G^*$ is its planar dual. $G$ is perfect if every induced subgraph $H$ satisfies $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$; $G \circ v$ (replication) replaces $v$ by two adjacent twins, each adjacent to exactly the old neighbors of $v$. $\alpha$, $\omega$, $\chi$, $\Delta$, $d(v)$ are independence number, clique number, chromatic number, maximum degree, and the degree of $v$.

The chapter's machine runs follow the Atlas's standing rule: exhaustive where feasible (full censuses over all nonempty labeled graphs on $1 \le n \le 6$ vertices — $33{,}867$ of them), and where randomness is used, a recorded seed, so every sample is reproducible bit for bit.

Lecture 1 · Treewidth: How Tree-like Is This Graph?

A path is easy. A tree is easy. A grid is harder — but only as hard as its width. The idea beneath treewidth is disarmingly simple: if your graph can be laid out as many small bags arranged along a tree, overlapping like roof tiles, then any global question can be answered by sweeping the tree, carrying only a little state per bag.

Tree decompositions, concretely. A tree decomposition of $G$ consists of bags $B_t$ (subsets of vertices, of size at most $k+1$) arranged as the nodes of a tree $T$, such that (i) every vertex of $G$ appears in at least one bag; (ii) for every edge $uv$, some bag contains both $u$ and $v$; and (iii) for every vertex $v$, the bags containing $v$ form a connected subtree of $T$. The width is the largest bag size minus one, and $\operatorname{tw}(G)$ is the minimum width. The minus one flatters the singleton: one vertex per bag is width $0$, and indeed $\operatorname{tw}(G) = 0$ exactly when $G$ has no edges. Forests with at least one edge are exactly the graphs of treewidth $1$ (and forests overall are the graphs of treewidth $\le 1$). A cycle needs width $2$. A complete graph $K_n$ refuses all compression: its bags must jointly witness every edge, and chasing the connectedness condition forces some bag to contain all $n$ vertices — $\operatorname{tw}(K_n) = n - 1$ (Exercise 2 makes you prove it).

The elimination game. There is a second, game-shaped way to compute the same number. Order the vertices $v_1, v_2, \dots$; eliminate them one by one, and each time $v$ is eliminated, connect all its not-yet-eliminated neighbors to one another (the fill). The eliminated degree of $v$ — its degree at the moment of elimination, fill included — depends on the order; the treewidth of $G$ is the minimum, over all elimination orders, of the maximum eliminated degree. (That this equals the bag definition is a classical fact; the chapter's machine computes treewidth exactly this way, sweeping all orders by dynamic programming over subsets.) The two views discipline each other: an elimination order of eliminated-degree $\le k$ is read off a width-$k$ decomposition and back.

✎ Checkpoint. Subdivide each edge of $K_4$ once — every vertex now has degree $2$ or $3$, the graph is $2$-degenerate (repeatedly remove a degree-$\le 2$ vertex and it all comes apart). What is its treewidth? Predict before reading on.

The answer is $3$, not $2$: subdividing thins the graph's local structure but the $K_4$ minor survives untouched (contractions undo subdivisions), and treewidth cannot drop below any minor's clique number minus one — $\operatorname{tw}(K_k) = k - 1$, $\operatorname{tw}(G) \ge \omega(G) - 1$ always, and $\operatorname{tw}(H) \le \operatorname{tw}(G)$ whenever $H$ is a minor of $G$, because contracting and deleting only shrink bags. The machine agrees both ways: $\operatorname{tw}(\text{subdivided } K_4) = 3$, degeneracy $2$ — the chain runs $K_4\text{-minor-free} \iff \operatorname{tw} \le 2 \implies 2\text{-degenerate} \implies 3\text{-colorable}$ — the first arrow is an equivalence (the biconditional above), and the latter two are strict (the subdivided $K_4$ is $2$-degenerate with treewidth $3$; the Petersen graph is $3$-colorable but $3$-degenerate). The subdivided $K_4$ is the honest exhibit that the middle arrow cannot be reversed: $2$-degenerate does not imply $K_4$-minor-free. (The census receipts record the same lesson from the other side: every $2$-degenerate graph on $1 \le n \le 6$ vertices that lacked a $K_4$ minor also had treewidth $\le 2$ — zero violations of the forward chain, all $33{,}867$ nonempty labeled graphs.)

What treewidth buys: dynamic programming. Fix a decomposition of width $k$ and root it. For each bag, the subgraph below it hangs off the rest of the graph only through the $\le k+1$ bag vertices — a separator. So the answers to optimization problems can be assembled bottom-up, one bag at a time, tabulating how each interface state (a coloring of the bag, a membership pattern for the independent set) extends downward. Two Atlas-lab verifications make this concrete rather than folkloric: on nine named graphs the machine built optimal-order decompositions (nine valid: $\text{width} = \operatorname{tw}$, every vertex covered, every edge covered, running intersection property holding, unique root) and computed, on those decompositions, the independence number $\alpha$ and the count of proper $3$-colorings by pure bag-DP — identical, digit for digit, to brute force: $\alpha = 1, 2, 2, 2, 4, 3, 5, 3, 8$ for $K_4$, $C_5$, octahedron, wheel $W_5$, Petersen, grid $2\times 3$, grid $3\times 3$, icosahedron, grid $4\times 4$ respectively, and $3$-coloring counts $30, 6, 120, 54, 246$ for $C_5$, octahedron, Petersen, grid $2\times 3$, grid $3\times 3$ ($K_4$, $W_5$ and the icosahedron admit none — they are the $0$ column). The point is not the nine numbers; it is that the method is mechanical and its correctness auditable on small cases where brute force is available as an oracle.

The grid theorem, honestly stated. Treewidth $\le k$ is a promise of algorithmic tractability. The deep theorem of Robertson–Seymour graph structure theory says the promise has a structural converse: every graph of large treewidth contains a large grid minor — and modern quantitative forms (Chekuri–Chuzhoy; Chuzhoy) push the required treewidth down to a polynomial in the grid size $k$. So “algorithmically hard structure” and “contains a big grid” are the same statement up to polynomial overhead. Beneath it lies the discipline's crown: the graph minor theorem — in any infinite list of graphs, one is a minor of another; equivalently every minor-closed family is characterized by finitely many excluded minors (Robertson–Seymour, Graph Minors XX, 2004, proving Wagner's conjecture). Planarity is the ancestral example: by Wagner's 1937 theorem, the $K_5 / K_{3,3}$-excluded family and the plane embeddable family coincide. This chapter uses the theory's language (minors, branch sets, excluded families) but proves only what it prints; the big structure theorems are cited, with the census range as the small-scale laboratory.

The minor oracle, built honestly. “Does $G$ contain a $K_k$ minor?” is a search for $k$ disjoint connected branch sets, pairwise adjacent. The chapter's machine does exactly that as a direct model search — and the receipt records why: the first-draft oracle (contract an edge, recurse) produced false negatives on the Petersen graph, whose $K_5$ minor is a book classic. The replacement search found the textbook witness, branch set by branch set: $\{0,1\}$, $\{2,3\}$, $\{4,9\}$, $\{5,7\}$, $\{6,8\}$ — five disjoint connected pairs, every pair adjacent, hand-checked edge by edge. Another witness worth carrying: the $3\times 3$ grid contains a $K_4$ minor with branch sets $\{0,1\}$, $\{2,5\}$, $\{3,6,7,8\}$, $\{4\}$ — the center vertex $4$ stands alone while its ring of eight vertices supplies three more branch sets. Contraction is violent and lossy; branch-set thinking is how humans and machines both should see minors.

Lecture 2 · Hadwiger's Conjecture: Coloring Forces Shadow-Cliques

The four-color theorem says a planar graph with chromatic number $4$ exists. Hadwiger's 1943 conjecture says what any graph with $\chi \ge k$ must contain: not a subgraph $K_k$ — that is false (the wheel $W_5$ is $4$-chromatic and planar with $\omega = 2$: no $K_4$ subgraph, though its $K_4$ minor is machine-witnessed) — but a minor $K_k$: a clique you can recover by deletions and contractions, the way the $3\times 3$ grid hides its $K_4$. Formally: $\chi(G) \ge k \implies G$ contains $K_k$ as a minor. Equivalently: Hadwiger $k$ fails exactly when some $K_k$-minor-free graph needs $k$ colors.

The small cases, proved in full.

$k = 1$: trivial. $k = 2$: a graph with no $K_2$ minor has no edges, and needs one color. $k = 3$: $K_3$-minor-free graphs are exactly the forests. Why: a cycle contracts (delete the rest of the graph, contract around the cycle) to a triangle — so any cycle yields a $K_3$ minor — and a forest, acyclic, contains no cycle to contract. So $\chi \ge 3$ means a non-forest, means a cycle, means a $K_3$ minor. (The census receipts carry a trap from this neighborhood: “$K_3$ minor $\iff$ non-bipartite” is false — $88$ bipartite graphs on $1 \le n \le 5$ vertices contain a $K_3$ minor, because contraction manufactures odd cycles out of even ones. The correct biconditional is $K_3$-minor $\iff$ not-a-forest, zero violations in all $1{,}099$ nonempty labeled graphs on $1 \le n \le 5$ vertices.)

$k = 4$ — the case Hadwiger himself proved in the founding paper, and this chapter proves too: every $K_4$-minor-free graph $G$ is $3$-colorable. By induction on $|V(G)|$, with base cases $|V(G)| \le 3$ trivial. Suppose $|V(G)| \ge 4$. We claim every nonempty $K_4$-minor-free graph $G$ has a vertex of degree at most $2$ (i.e., $G$ is $2$-degenerate); deleting that vertex $v$, $3$-coloring $G - v$ by induction, and restoring $v$ with a color missing from its at most two neighbors completes the induction. To prove the claim, take a minimal non-redundant tree decomposition $T$ of $G$ of width $\operatorname{tw}(G) \le 2$ (where $B_s \not\subseteq B_t$ for all adjacent tree nodes $s, t$). If $T$ has a single bag $B_1 = V(G)$, then $|V(G)| \le 3$ and every vertex has degree at most $2$. If $T$ has at least two bags, pick any leaf bag $B_\ell$ of $T$ with unique neighbor bag $B_p$; by non-redundancy, there is a vertex $u \in B_\ell \setminus B_p$. By the running intersection property, $B_\ell$ is the only bag containing $u$; since every edge incident to $u$ must lie in some bag containing $u$, all neighbors of $u$ in $G$ belong to $B_\ell \setminus \{u\}$, so $d_G(u) \le |B_\ell| - 1 \le 2$. (No reduction to $2$-connected blocks is even needed once $\operatorname{tw}(G) \le 2$ is invoked directly; if one instead states the lemma for a $2$-connected $K_4$-minor-free graph having a vertex of degree equal to $2$, $2$-connectivity rules out degrees $0$ and $1$, while the same leaf-bag vertex $u$ provides degree at most $2$. The fan-flavored sketch an earlier draft carried — an induced cycle plus a vertex welded on by two disjoint paths — was unsound: two attachments build only a theta, and a theta is $K_4$-minor-free, so the promised contradiction never arrives; the machine's trap exhibit, a $C_5$ with one extra vertex joined to two of its vertices, is $2$-connected, $K_4$-minor-free and theta-shaped — the census says all $3{,}810$ $2$-connected $K_4$-minor-free graphs on $6$ vertices carry a degree-$2$ vertex, for the tree-decomposition reason above.) The lemma structure (find a low vertex, remove, extend the coloring) is exactly the five-color theorem's engine in Lecture 4 — Hadwiger $k = 4$ and Heawood's five-color theorem are the same machine pointed at different structure.

The frontier, stated honestly. Hadwiger $k = 5$ is equivalent to the four-color theorem — Wagner's 1937 result: $K_5$-minor-free graphs split into planar pieces glued along cliques of at most $2$ vertices (the Wagner graph $W$ being the one exotic piece), and the gluing preserves $4$-colorability, so 4CT $\implies$ Hadwiger $5$, and the smallest counterexample to Hadwiger $5$ would be a non-$4$-colorable $K_5$-minor-free graph, which by Wagner's decomposition would have to be... planar. So: Hadwiger $5 \iff$ 4CT (proved, both directions, modulo the 1976 computer proof and its 1997 RSST simplification). Hadwiger $k = 6$ is a theorem: Robertson–Seymour–Thomas (1993), relying on 4CT. Hadwiger $k \ge 7$ is open — every $k$, at $7$ and beyond, is conjecture. The chapter's machine can only patrol the bottom floor: it verified Hadwiger $k = 4$ on every nonempty labeled graph through $n = 6$ — at $n = 6$, all $32{,}768$ graphs swept, $5{,}214$ with $\chi \ge 4$, every single one carrying a $K_4$ minor (receipt-recorded; the same census at $1 \le n \le 5$ found $67$ such graphs, again all clean). That is a floor, not evidence for the open cases, and the text says so.

Why minors, why conjecture, why believe it. Minors are the right notion because they are exactly what coloring cannot see past: contractions can raise chromatic number (contract a long even cycle's perfect matching: an odd cycle appears, $\chi$ jumps $2 \to 3$), but a $K_k$ minor already needs $k$ colors — $\chi(G) \ge \chi(\text{minor}) \ge k$ — so the conjecture is tight in the direction it claims. It is believed because every precedent proved true and because structure theory keeps delivering the pieces: excluded-minor families have bounded, describable structure, and Hadwiger's statement is exactly the claim that $K_k$-minor-free structure is $(k-1)$-colorable. For $k = 4$ you just watched that be a theorem. Where it stops being one, this book stops proving and starts citing.

✎ Checkpoint. The Petersen graph has $\chi = 3$. Hadwiger $k = 3$ predicts a $K_3$ minor; $k = 4$ predicts a $K_4$ minor (since $\chi = 3 < 4$, it predicts nothing); what is the largest $k$ with a guaranteed $K_k$ minor — i.e., what does the conjecture not claim here? Predict the largest clique minor of the Petersen graph before reading the receipt's answer: it is $K_5$ (Lecture 1's witness) — Hadwiger is not sharp on every graph, and the Petersen graph is its classic exhibit of slack: $3$ colors suffice, yet a $5$-clique minor hides inside. Slack is the conjecture's mystery, in both directions.

Lecture 3 · List Coloring: When the Palettes Are Private

Ordinary coloring hands every vertex the same box of $k$ crayons. List coloring hands vertex $v$ a private list $L(v)$ of $k$ colors — maybe all different boxes at every vertex — and asks for a proper coloring with $c(v) \in L(v)$. The graph is $k$-choosable if this is possible for every choice of lists; $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ is the least such $k$. Three facts anchor the theory, and the first two are one-liners.

$\operatorname{ch}(G) \ge \chi(G)$ — a proper coloring from identical lists is an ordinary coloring. $\operatorname{ch}(K_n) = n$ — assign the $n$ vertices the same $(n-1)$-list and no color escapes; conversely greedy never needs more than the list size. So choosability refines chromatic number, and the refinement can be strict. The chapter's exhibit is the smallest one in the literature: $K_{2,4}$ is not $2$-choosable, even though $\chi(K_{2,4}) = 2$.

Here is the witness, exactly as the machine found it (the lexicographically-first failing assignment among all one million two-list assignments on the $5$-color palette — $720$ of the million fail, and this is the first): the two $a$-side vertices carry lists $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$; the four $b$-side vertices carry $\{1,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$, $\{2,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$. Try to color. Each $a$-vertex has two options ($c(a_1) \in \{1,2\}$ and $c(a_2) \in \{3,4\}$), producing four possible color pairs $(c(a_1), c(a_2)) \in \{1,2\} \times \{3,4\} = \{(1,3), (1,4), (2,3), (2,4)\}$; each of the four $b$-vertices has one of those exact two-element sets as its list $L(b_j)$, so whichever pair is chosen on the $a$-side completely exhausts the list of the corresponding $b$-vertex, leaving it uncolorable (Exercise 6 walks through the full comparison with $K_{2,3}$). Meanwhile $K_{2,3}$ is $2$-choosable — the machine checked all $100{,}000$ two-list assignments, zero failures — and $\operatorname{ch}(K_{2,4}) = 3$ exactly: all $1{,}000{,}000$ three-list assignments are choosable (two half-million sweeps). The complete bipartite graph is the standard laboratory of choosability because the two sides interact only through forbidden pairs — the problem becomes a hitting question about small sets, and the boundary between $K_{2,3}$ and $K_{2,4}$ is the boundary itself.

The degeneracy guarantee (and why order matters). If $G$ is $k$-degenerate — every subgraph has a vertex of degree $\le k$ — then $G$ is $(k+1)$-choosable: color vertices in reverse peel order; when $v$ is colored, at most $k$ neighbors are already colored, so at least one of its $k+1$ list colors is free. No backtracking, ever. The lab receipts record a practical coda: the machine first colored in forward peel order and occasionally thrashed — hub vertices in stacked triangulations accumulated many colored neighbors before their turn, forcing search — while reverse-peel ran backtrack-free, $41$ search nodes exactly, on all $300$ stacked-$40$ samples. Correctness was never in question (search returns a coloring either way); the lesson is that a provable greedy order is also the engineering order.

The two theorems that define the planar frontier. Thomassen (1994): every planar graph is $5$-choosable. The proof is a two-list induction on plane triangulations with precolored path boundaries — among the cleanest induction shapes in the field — and this chapter states it with proof cited, not given (the machine's contribution is the census floor: $1{,}500$ seeded five-list samples across the octahedron, icosahedron, wheel $W_5$, $3\times 3$ grid, and a $40$-vertex stacked triangulation — $300$ each, lists drawn uniformly from the $56$ five-subsets of an $8$-color palette, seed $777333$ — all $1{,}500$ choosable, zero failures). Galvin (1995): every bipartite multigraph has $\operatorname{ch}'(G) = \Delta(G)$ — the list-coloring edge theorem that settles the 1979 Dinitz problem. Galvin's engine is worth naming because it is a pattern card of its own: orient the edges by a stable matching (hearts of the bipartite structure), so that every edge's out-neighborhood is kernel-perfect, and greedily color the kernels. The lab's small prints: exhaustive edge-list checks on $C_4$ ($10{,}000$ assignments) and $C_6$ ($1{,}000{,}000$ assignments) with $\Delta = 2$ lists found zero failures, and $2{,}000$ sampled three-list assignments on $K_{3,3}$ ($\Delta = 3$) all succeeded, seed $20261012$.

The honest edge. Five lists suffice for every planar graph; four do not. Voigt (1993) constructed a planar graph on $238$ vertices that is not $4$-choosable — the counterexample that bounds Thomassen's theorem from below — and Voigt–Wirth (1997) showed even $3$-colorable planar graphs can fail $4$-choosability. (A $63$-vertex example due to Mirzakhani is the smaller classic; the $238$ is the publication that settled non-$4$-choosability first — both are cited, neither is reproduced here.) The gap between $4$ and $5$ — one color of daylight between theorem and counterexample — is the state of the art, and the chapter keeps it honest: choosability's planar story is not the four-color story wearing a hat; it is a different theory with a different (and still one-off-tight) frontier.

Lecture 4 · Discharging: Euler's Formula as Currency

Every connected planar graph obeys $n - m + f = 2$ (Euler). Read on a simple plane triangulation on $n \ge 3$ vertices — every face a triangle, $m = 3n - 6$, $f = 2n - 4$ — the formula becomes a statement about degrees, and degrees are where the colorer lives.

The degree budget, derived and machine-audited. Sum the face-edge incidences on a plane triangulation ($n \ge 3$): $3f = 2m$, so with Euler, $\sum_v (6 - d(v)) = 6n - 2m = 6n - (6n - 12) = \mathbf{12}$. Twelve units of “credit” are minted, no more, no less, on every plane triangulation of every order $n \ge 3$. The chapter's lab verified this the only trustworthy way — not once, but step by step on growing stacked triangulations (start from a triangle; each step splits a face by a new degree-$3$ vertex): at $n = 300$, three seeds, every intermediate $n$ carried $\sum_v (6 - d(v)) = 12$ exactly, with $m = 3n - 6$ and $f = 2n - 4$ holding at every step and min degree exactly $3$ (each new vertex arrives with degree $3$ and old degrees only grow — the identity is a telescope in disguise, and the machine watched it not wobble).

The five-color theorem (Heawood 1890), proved in full. Every planar graph is $5$-colorable.

Proof. Induct on $n$. A minimal counterexample $G$ is $6$-critical, so we may take $G$ a plane triangulation on $n \ge 6$ vertices (adding edges to triangulate only hardens coloring). The budget forces a vertex $v$ with $d(v) \le 5$: if all degrees were $\ge 6$, $\sum_v (6 - d(v)) \le 0 < 12$. If $d(v) \le 4$, delete $v$, color by induction, extend — at most $4$ neighbors, $5$ colors, one is free. So the load-bearing case is $d(v) = 5$ with neighbors $v_1, \dots, v_5$ in cyclic order, using all five colors (else a free color extends). Consider the two colors on $v_1$ and $v_3$; if no $v_1$–$v_3$ Kempe chain — no two-color path joining $v_1$ to $v_3$ through the subgraph of those two colors — swap the two colors on $v_1$'s chain component and free $v_1$'s color for $v$. The chain must be absent in one of the two directions: a $v_1$–$v_3$ chain of colors $\{1,3\}$ and a $v_2$–$v_4$ chain of colors $\{2,4\}$ would have to cross, and planarity forbids it (one chain lives inside the $v$-cycle wedge $v_1 v_2 v_3$, the other inside $v_3 v_4 v_1$, and they cannot touch). So one swap always frees a color. $\blacksquare$

Every ingredient of the four-color proof is visible in miniature: the degree budget producing a reducible configuration ($d \le 5$), and Kempe chains trading colors at a distance. What fails at four colors is precisely the Kempe argument's bookkeeping: with a degree-$5$ vertex and only $4$ colors, two chains can both be present and an innocent-looking swap can corrupt a distant region — Heawood himself found the flaw in Kempe's 1879 “proof” this way, eleven years after the journal published it. The four-color theorem, when Appel and Haken closed it in 1976 (simplified by Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas 1997), is this same machine at industrial scale: a discharging argument proves every minimal counterexample must contain one of an unavoidable set of $1{,}936$ configurations (RSST: $633$), and a computer case-checks each configuration reducible. The proof shape is in this lecture; the execution is a cited citation.

The light edge, the engine of the modern proof. The budget mints $12$ credits; a vertex of degree $d$ “consumes” $6 - d$ of them, and $d \ge 7$ vertices are net payers (a $6$-vertex breaks even). Discharging formalizes this: assign charges, move them along edges by local rules, and conclude some local configuration must exist — in a plane triangulation of minimum degree $5$, some edge $uv$ has $d(u) + d(v) \le 11$. Proof, by discharging. Suppose every edge had $d(u) + d(v) \ge 12$. Then no edge joins two $5$-vertices (weight $10$) and none joins a $5$-vertex to a $6$-vertex (weight $11$); the budget $\sum_v (6 - d(v)) = 12 > 0$ still forces a $5$-vertex to exist, and every one of its neighbors has degree $\ge 7$. Discharge: each $5$-vertex sends $1/5$ to every neighbor. A vertex $w$ of degree $d \ge 7$ receives at most $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor / 5 \le d/10$ in total — its $5$-neighbors are pairwise non-adjacent (a $5$–$5$ edge is forbidden) and they sit on $w$'s neighbor cycle, so there are at most $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ of them — and ends at no more than $(6 - d) + d/10 = 6 - 0.9d < 0$; a $6$-vertex receives nothing ($5$–$6$ edges are forbidden) and stays at $0$; a $5$-vertex gives away exactly its $+1$ and receives nothing (its neighbors are not $5$-vertices), ending at exactly $0$. Every vertex ends $\le 0$ and the forced $5$-vertex's neighbors end strictly negative, so the total is $< 0$ — but charge is conserved, so the total is $\sum_v (6 - d(v)) = 12$. Contradiction. $\blacksquare$ The receipt's samples show the bound is met, not beaten: light edges of weight exactly $11$ occur — the pentakis dodecahedron (the $C_{60}$ fullerene's dual: twelve degree-$5$ apexes over the dodecahedron's twenty faces, plus twenty degree-$6$ rim vertices) carries all sixty apex–rim edges at weight exactly $11$ and thirty rim edges at $12$, none lighter (machine-verified) — wheels carry rim edges of weight $6$ ($W_3 = K_4$, all edges $6$; spokes run heavier: $W_4$ $7$, $W_5$ $8$), stacked triangulations $7$, the octahedron $8$, the icosahedron $10$. The light edge is the 4CT engine's piston: reducibility proofs attach to light configurations, unavoidability comes from the charge — and the proof above is that unavoidability engine in miniature; the lab holds the exhibits.

Lecture 5 · Nowhere-Zero Flows: Coloring's Mirror

Turn the picture around. Instead of coloring vertices, orient the edges and write a nonzero integer on each, demanding that flow conserves at every vertex: $\operatorname{inflow} = \operatorname{outflow}$. The labels must come from $\{\pm 1, \dots, \pm(k-1)\}$ — nowhere zero is the point, a zero label is an edge you might as well delete. If such a labeling exists, $G$ carries a nowhere-zero $k$-flow, and the flow number $\varphi(G)$ is the least such $k$. A loopless graph with a bridge can never carry one — the bridge must carry the entire flow of its component, forcing zero — so the theory's home is the bridgeless ($2$-edge-connected) graphs.

First equivalences, by hand. For a graph $G$ with at least one edge, $\varphi(G) = 2$ happens exactly when every vertex degree is even: a nowhere-zero $2$-flow uses only $\pm 1$, so at each vertex the number of in-edges must equal the number of out-edges — every connected component is Eulerian, and an Eulerian orientation (split each vertex's incident edges half-in, half-out along an Euler tour) is the flow. This is why the flow table's strangest-looking entry is $K_5 \to 2$: five vertices, ten edges, every vertex of degree $4$ — hence Eulerian — admitting a nowhere-zero $2$-flow with no effort at all, the exact same flow number $\varphi(C_3) = 2$ as a single triangle (which is $2$-regular and also Eulerian). Flow number ignores cliquishness entirely; it tracks parity structure. The other extreme: the Petersen graph has $\varphi = 5$, and no nowhere-zero $4$-flow exists — the machine proved the negative by exhaustive search over all assignments — and the classic witness $5$-flow is printed edge by edge in the lab (outer pentagon and spokes labeled $[1, -1, 1, -1, 2, 1, 2, -2, 2, -3]$ and the inner star $[4, 1, 2, 3, -1]$ in the recorded edge order — Exercise 10's balance table; every vertex balances, every label in $\{\pm 1, \dots, \pm 4\}$; the arrays this paragraph first carried were compacted-session garble and died in the F6 scar, the receipt's correction class). The Petersen graph is to flows what the four-critical planar graphs are to coloring: the boundary case at the bottom of the conjecture ladder.

Modular flows and Tutte's lift. A $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow is the same demand with balance checked $\bmod k$, labels in $\{1, \dots, k-1\}$; a $\bmod\text{-}k$ orientation orients edges so every vertex has $\operatorname{indegree} \equiv \operatorname{outdegree} \pmod{k}$. Tutte proved these are the same theory: $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow $\iff$ integer nowhere-zero $k$-flow, and (for $k = 3$, the case that matters in applications) $\mathbb{Z}_3$-flow $\iff$ $\bmod\text{-}3$ orientability. The lift's hard direction (integer $\impliedby$ modular) is a tightening argument that rounds a fractional residue into an exact one; it is this chapter's ◆ Problem 2, with the lab receipts as its empirical floor: on every named graph of this chapter and $k \in \{3, 4, 5\}$, the machine verified the equivalence directly ($\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow found $\iff$ integer flow found), and $\bmod\text{-}3$-orientation $\iff \mathbb{Z}_3$-flow on all the named graphs with at most $16$ edges.

Planar duality: one object, two theories. For a plane graph $G$ with dual $G^*$, every edge of $G$ crosses exactly one edge of $G^*$, and a proper $k$-coloring of the vertices of $G^*$ corresponds bijectively (up to uniform color shifts $\bmod k$) to a nowhere-zero $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow on $G$, establishing Tait's identity $\varphi(G) = \chi(G^*)$ for every connected bridgeless plane graph $G$ with at least one edge. Specifically, fix a reference orientation for each edge $e = u \to v$ of $G$, and orient the dual edge $e^* = f_L \to f_R$ in $G^*$ from the face $f_L$ on the left of $e$ to the face $f_R$ on the right of $e$. Given a proper vertex coloring $c : V(G^*) \to \mathbb{Z}_k$, assign to each edge $e$ of $G$ the modular flow value $\phi(e) \equiv c(f_R) - c(f_L) \pmod{k}$. Since $c$ is proper, $\phi(e) \not\equiv 0 \pmod{k}$ on every edge (the bridge-free hypothesis ensures $f_L \ne f_R$ on every edge). Around any vertex $v \in V(G)$, the incident edges $e_1, \dots, e_d$ in cyclic clockwise order separate a cyclic sequence of dual faces $f_1, f_2, \dots, f_d, f_1$; the net outflow from $v$ sums the telescoping differences $\sum_{i=1}^d (c(f_{i+1}) - c(f_i)) \equiv 0 \pmod{k}$, yielding a nowhere-zero $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow (and hence an integer nowhere-zero $k$-flow by Tutte's lift). Conversely, given any nowhere-zero $\mathbb{Z}_k$-flow $\phi$ on $G$, the circulation of the dual difference around every facial cycle of $G^*$ vanishes because each face $v^*$ of $G^*$ corresponds to a vertex $v$ of $G$ where flow is conserved; since the plane is simply connected, every closed walk in $G^*$ is a sum of facial cycles, so accumulating $\phi$ along paths in $G^*$ from a fixed root face $f_0$ (with $c(f_0) = 0$) defines a path-independent, proper $\mathbb{Z}_k$-coloring $c$ of $V(G^*)$. The lab's seven embedded samples verify the identity on both self-duals and dual pairs: $K_4$ (self-dual, $4 = 4$), the wheel $W_5$ (self-dual, $4 = 4$), $C_4$ ($2 = 2$), the octahedron/cube pair ($2 = 2$ — the octahedron is Eulerian and its dual the cube is bipartite: even degrees dualize to even face sizes), the prism/bipyramid pair ($4 = 4$), the icosahedron/dodecahedron pair ($3 = 3$), and a $6$-vertex stacked triangulation ($3 = 3$) — with Euler $n - m + f = 2$ re-verified on every embedding. Two consequences worth pausing on: the four-color theorem is a flow statement — $\chi(G^*) \le 4$ for every loopless plane $G^*$ is $\varphi(G) \le 4$ for every bridgeless plane $G$, Tait's 1880 route; and flow numbers are the right invariant for nonplanar generalization, because duals stop existing but flows do not.

The honest ledger of the flow conjectures. This is where the chapter refuses to decorate. Tutte's $5$-flow conjecture (1954): every bridgeless graph has a nowhere-zero $5$-flow — OPEN. The chapter's census receipts patrol the small world: all $264$ connected bridgeless graphs on $2 \le n \le 5$ vertices have $\varphi \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ (histogram $42 / 191 / 31$), and all $11{,}968$ bridgeless graphs on $6$ vertices likewise ($720 / 9{,}961 / 1{,}287$) — zero need $k = 5$, and the Petersen graph at $n = 10$ shows the conjecture's bound is tight, since its flow number is exactly $5$. What is proved: Seymour's $6$-flow theorem (1981) — every bridgeless graph carries a nowhere-zero $6$-flow — the general result the field actually owns, proved by a clever three-edge-case induction this chapter cites without proof. The $4$-flow conjecture (Tutte 1966): every bridgeless graph with no Petersen minor carries a nowhere-zero $4$-flow — a statement strictly generalizing the four-color theorem (a planar graph has no Petersen minor) — still open in general, with major settled cases (the cubic case is known). The $3$-flow conjecture (Tutte): every $4$-edge-connected graph has a nowhere-zero $3$-flow — still open; the $6$-edge-connected case is a theorem (Lovász–Thomassen–Wu–Zhang, J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 103 (2013), 587–598). The lecture's table for small named graphs ($K_4 \to 4$, $K_{3,3} \to 3$, octahedron $\to 2$, cube $\to 3$, triangular prism $\to 4$, wheel $W_5 \to 4$, icosahedron $\to 3$) is machine-computed; every statement about $k \ge 5$ in the unbounded world is conjecture, and says so, in those words.

Lecture 6 · Perfect Graphs: Where Coloring and Cliques Agree

Coloring asks for partitions; cliques ask for witnesses. On every graph, $\omega \le \chi$ — a clique needs its own colors. The gap between them is the graph's structural debt, and most of this book (Hadwiger, flows, discharging) studies graphs where the debt is real. Perfect graphs are the class with none: $G$ is perfect if every induced subgraph $H$ satisfies $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$ — the induced-subgraph demand is what makes the class hereditary and the theory deep.

First examples, first counterexample. Bipartite graphs are perfect ($\chi(H) = \omega(H) \in \{1, 2\}$ on every nonempty induced subgraph $H$, which stays bipartite: $\chi = \omega = 2$ if $H$ has an edge, and $\chi = \omega = 1$ if $H$ is edgeless). Cycles of even length: perfect. Interval graphs, comparability graphs: perfect (both cited to their standard proofs). And then the smallest failure, the hole: $C_5$ has $\chi = 3 > 2 = \omega$. The induced-subgraph clause now pays: $C_5$'s proper induced subgraphs are paths and singletons — all perfect — so $C_5$ is minimal imperfect: every proper induced subgraph is honest, the whole is not. The machine certified exactly this ($\chi = 3$, $\omega = 2$, $\alpha = 2$ on $C_5$; all $30$ nonempty proper induced subgraphs perfect), and counted the damage at census scale: among all $1{,}024$ labeled graphs on $5$ vertices, precisely $12$ are imperfect — the $12$ labelings of $C_5$, no others — and among all $32{,}768$ on $6$ vertices, $1{,}944$, every one containing an induced $C_5$ on five of its vertices. Imperfection, at this scale, is exactly the hole.

Lovász's characterization: $\alpha\cdot\omega \ge n$. What does the $C_5$ failure look like numerically? $\alpha = 2$, $\omega = 2$, and $\alpha\cdot\omega = 4 < 5 = n$. Lovász's theorem (1972) says that inequality is imperfection: $G$ is perfect $\iff$ every induced subgraph $H$ of $G$ satisfies $\alpha(H)\cdot\omega(H) \ge |V(H)|$. Reading it forward: if $H$ is perfect, $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$ colors partition $H$ into $\chi(H) = \omega(H)$ independent sets, and the largest has size $\ge |V(H)|/\omega(H)$; but any independent set has size $\le \alpha(H)$, so $\alpha(H)\cdot\omega(H) \ge |V(H)|$. (The proof is Exercise 12's route for the replication lemma; the converse — the inequality on every induced subgraph forcing perfection — is the hard direction, and it is where replication enters.) The census receipts patrol the whole small world: on all $33{,}867$ nonempty labeled graphs through $n = 6$, the biconditional held with zero violations — every imperfect graph has an induced subgraph with $\alpha\cdot\omega < n$ ($C_5$ and its kin), every perfect graph has none.

Replication, the engine of the theory. Given $G$ and a vertex $v$, form $G \circ v$: replace $v$ by two adjacent twins $v_1, v_2$, each carrying exactly $v$'s old neighborhood. Two facts, one true and one false, and the receipts keep them straight. True — the replication lemma (Lovász): $G$ is perfect $\iff G \circ v$ is perfect. The proof runs the $\alpha\cdot\omega \ge n$ characterization through the duplication: twins let you weight, and the characterization is exactly what weights respect (Exercise 12). False — the tempting side-formula $\chi(G \circ v) = \max(\chi(G), d(v)+1)$: the census tested all $285$ replications of every nonempty labeled graph on $1 \le n \le 4$ vertices and it fails on $167$ of them; the smallest witness is $K_2$ (replicate either endpoint: a triangle, $\chi = 3$, while $\max(\chi, d+1) = 2$). The one-schoolbook case that does satisfy it — $P_3$ with its middle vertex replicated, which yields $K_4$ minus an edge with $\chi = 3 = \max(2, 3)$ — is a coincidence of that one shape, not a theorem. The machine's certified true bounds: $\chi(G \circ v) \in [\chi(G), \chi(G)+1]$ always (color the twin with a fresh color), and perfection is exactly preserved.

The weak perfect graph theorem, proved by replication. WPGT (Lovász 1972): $G$ is perfect $\iff$ the complement $\overline{G}$ is perfect. The proof route this chapter follows is Lovász's own, and it starts from Fulkerson's 1971 observation that the statement would follow if replication preserved anti-perfection — the details are ◆ Problem 3, and the answer walks the full route: the $\alpha\cdot\omega$ characterization is self-complementary in just the right way ($\alpha$ and $\omega$ swap under complementation, so the inequality is complement-invariant), replication substitutes weights, and WPGT falls out. The machine's census floor: $\operatorname{perfect}(G) \iff \operatorname{perfect}(\overline{G})$ verified on all $33{,}867$ nonempty labeled graphs through $n = 6$, zero violations — and the small world's complement exhibits are printed in the lab ($C_5$'s complement is $C_5$ again — the pentagram; and the octahedron's complement is a perfect matching on six vertices — both sides of each mirror perfect, as the theorem demands).

✎ Checkpoint. Predict before reading: is the complement of $C_7$ another $C_7$? The pentagon taught you holes and antiholes rhyme. The answer is no, and the machine caught the working assumption before it reached the page: $\overline{C_7}$ is $4$-regular (each vertex non-adjacent to itself and two cycle-neighbors, adjacent to the other four), while $C_7$ is $2$-regular — they are not isomorphic at a glance at any degree sequence. In fact $\overline{C_n} \cong C_n$ holds only at $n = 5$. But $\overline{C_7}$ is still imperfect — $\chi(\overline{C_7}) = 4 > 3 = \omega$ — and still minimal imperfect (all proper induced subgraphs perfect, $\alpha\cdot\omega = 6 < 7$). The antihole is the complement's own boundary case, and it is a genuinely different graph from the hole except in the one dimension where $C_5$ is self-complementary.

The strong perfect graph theorem, stated. Four decades of conjecture, closed by Chudnovsky–Robertson–Seymour–Thomas (Annals, 2006): $G$ is perfect $\iff G$ contains no induced odd hole and no induced odd antihole (no induced $C_{2k+1}$, $k \ge 2$, and no induced complement of one). The census receipts are the theorem's small-scale shadow: through $n = 6$, “imperfect” and “contains an induced $C_5$” are equivalent ($1{,}944 + 12$ graphs, zero exceptions either way) — because the smallest odd hole is $C_5$ and the smallest odd antihole beyond it is $\overline{C_7}$ on $7$ vertices, both out of census range, and $\overline{C_5} \cong C_5$. At $n = 7$ the world would start distinguishing holes from antiholes (the lab Part 6's antihole unit) — the census stops honestly at $6$ and the theorem is cited, not proved. Recognition is polynomial (Chudnovsky et al. 2005), closing the class as algorithmically tame; the theta graphs you met in Chapter 8 as spectral exhibits live here as the classic family the SPGT had to account for.

Why perfect graphs close this chapter. Hadwiger says $\chi$'s growth forces minors; flows say edge-labeling balances mirror coloring; discharging says planarity taxes degrees; perfection says: on a class defined by forbidden induced subgraphs — holes and antiholes — coloring never lies. The induced-subgraph world (perfection) and the minor world (Hadwiger, treewidth) are the two honest regimes where structure and chromatic number meet exactly, and the gap between them — graphs that are neither — is where the open problems all live.

def has_kk_minor(n: int, adj: list[int], k: int) -> bool:
    """Exact K_k minor test via k disjoint connected pairwise-adjacent branch sets."""
    if k <= 0:
        return True
    if n < k:
        return False

    def is_conn(mask: int) -> bool:
        start = (mask & -mask).bit_length() - 1
        vis, stack = 1 << start, [start]
        while stack:
            u = stack.pop()
            nbrs = adj[u] & mask & ~vis
            while nbrs:
                lsb = nbrs & -nbrs
                vis |= lsb
                stack.append(lsb.bit_length() - 1)
                nbrs ^= lsb
        return vis == mask

    by_min = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    nbr_mask = {}
    for m in range(1, 1 << n):
        if is_conn(m):
            by_min[(m & -m).bit_length() - 1].append(m)
            nm, tmp = 0, m
            while tmp:
                lsb = tmp & -tmp
                nm |= adj[lsb.bit_length() - 1]
                tmp ^= lsb
            nbr_mask[m] = nm

    chosen: list[int] = []
    def dfs(start_v: int, used: int) -> bool:
        if len(chosen) == k:
            return True
        if n - start_v < k - len(chosen):
            return False
        for v in range(start_v, n):
            if (used >> v) & 1:
                continue
            for m in by_min[v]:
                if not (m & used) and all((nbr_mask[m] & prev) != 0 for prev in chosen):
                    chosen.append(m)
                    if dfs(v + 1, used | m):
                        return True
                    chosen.pop()
        return False

    return dfs(0, 0)

def is_perfect_graph(n: int, adj: list[int]) -> tuple[bool, int, int, int]:
    """Checks chi(H) == omega(H) and alpha(H)*omega(H) >= |H| on all 2^n - 1 induced subgraphs."""
    if n == 0:
        return True, 0, 0, 0
    N = 1 << n
    indep, omega, alpha, chi = [True] * N, [0] * N, [0] * N, [n] * N
    chi[0] = 0
    for mask in range(1, N):
        lsb = mask & -mask
        v = lsb.bit_length() - 1
        rest = mask ^ lsb
        indep[mask] = indep[rest] and ((adj[v] & rest) == 0)
        omega[mask] = max(omega[rest], 1 + omega[rest & adj[v]])
        alpha[mask] = max(alpha[rest], 1 + alpha[rest & ~adj[v]])
        sub, best = mask, n
        while sub:
            if (sub & lsb) and indep[sub]:
                best = min(best, 1 + chi[mask ^ sub])
            sub = (sub - 1) & mask
        chi[mask] = best
    full = N - 1
    perfect = all(chi[m] == omega[m] for m in range(1, N))
    lovasz = all(alpha[m] * omega[m] >= m.bit_count() for m in range(1, N))
    assert perfect == lovasz
    return perfect, chi[full], omega[full], alpha[full]
