Chapter 1 · Counting

A lecture course, in the spirit of Feynman's physics lectures: the idea before the formula, the story before the proof, and the proof as a conversation. A compact reference card follows the lectures; graded exercises, a computational lab and worked answers close the chapter.

How to read this chapter

Read it slowly, with a pencil. Whenever a small number appears in front of you — compute it yourself before reading on. This chapter is a conversation, and you must do your half. Nothing here requires genius; it requires only the patience to count carefully, which is the entire secret of combinatorics. Lines marked ✎ Checkpoint are the places to stop and predict: commit to a guess — in ink — before reading on. A checked guess teaches; a passively read reveal only feels like learning.

The exercises are graded: easy (a direct application of one lecture idea), moderate (two ideas, or one tricky step), and tough (several chapter ideas combined), followed by a Pattern unknown diagnostic set where the hints and difficulty labels are removed so you must choose the tool yourself. For the graded tiers, hints travel with the problems and worked answers sit at the very end of the chapter. Try honestly before reading a hint — the struggle is the part of learning that cannot be outsourced. Beyond the problem sets, Explorations at the end are open-ended investigations with no single right answer — research training, not test preparation.

Lecture 1 · Ten Letters, Ten Wrong Envelopes

Here is a problem posed around 1708 by Pierre Rémond de Montmort, and it caused a surprising amount of trouble: You have $n$ letters and $n$ addressed envelopes. In how many ways can you put every letter into a wrong envelope?

A permutation with no fixed point is called a derangement, and the count is written $D_n$. Before we know anything, let us feel the numbers. Take $n = 2$: the only way to get both wrong is to swap them — $D_2 = 1$. For $n = 3$, try it: the two $3$-cycles $(2,3,1)$ and $(3,1,2)$ — each list says where letters $1, 2, 3$ go — put every letter in a wrong envelope, and everything else leaves at least one letter at home, so $D_3 = 2$. For $n = 4$ you can grind it out: $D_4 = 9$. From $n = 2$ the sequence runs $1, 2, 9, 44, 265, 1854, \dots$ and it grows almost as fast as the factorial. Already something is strange: "get everything wrong" turns out to be far from rare — a sizeable fraction of all the arrangements there are.

Now — how would you attack this? Let me show you three attacks, and I promise each one is something you could have invented.

1.1 First attack: count the complement — but carefully

There are $n!$ arrangements in all. The ones we want are the complement of "at least one letter lands home." Fine: count the arrangements where letter $1$ is home, letter $2$ is home, and so on, and subtract. Let's do $n = 4$ by hand, because the danger shows up immediately.

"Letter $i$ is home" happens in $3!$ ways for each fixed $i$ — that's $4 \times 6 = 24$. Subtract from $24$ total: $24 - 24 = 0$?? Already nonsense — the answer is $9$, not $0$. What went wrong? A permutation with two letters home was counted twice, once for each letter. The events overlap, and overlapping events must not be counted with a plain subtraction.

So we repair it, and the repair is the whole method: add back the double overlaps, subtract the triple overlaps, add back the quadruple… For $n = 4$: $(4 \times 3!) - \left(\binom{4}{2} \times 2!\right) + \left(\binom{4}{3} \times 1!\right) - (1 \times 0!) = 24 - 12 + 4 - 1 = 15$ arrangements with at least one letter home. The complement: $24 - 15 = \mathbf{9}$ ✓.

Do you see the mechanism? Add, subtract, add, subtract — alternate. Let us prove that this zigzag gives the exact count $D_n$ for every $n$, not just $n = 4$. For each subset $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$ of size $k$, the number of arrangements that place at least the letters of $S$ into their own envelopes is $(n-k)!$, because the $k$ letters in $S$ are fixed and the remaining $n-k$ letters may be arranged in any order. Since there are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways to choose the $k$-element subset $S$, the $k$-th level sum across all $k$-tuples of letters is $\binom{n}{k}(n-k)!$. Now follow any single arrangement $\pi$ through the alternating sum

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n-k)!.$$

Suppose $\pi$ puts exactly $m$ letters into their correct envelopes. At level $k$, the arrangement $\pi$ is counted once for every $k$-element subset $S$ chosen from its $m$ fixed letters — that is, $\binom{m}{k}$ times for $k \le m$, and $0$ times for $k > m$. The total net weight contributed by $\pi$ to the alternating sum is therefore

$$\sum_{k=0}^{m} (-1)^k \binom{m}{k}.$$

If $m = 0$ (so $\pi$ is a derangement), this sum consists of the single term $(-1)^0 \binom{0}{0} = 1$, so every derangement is counted with weight $1$. If $m \ge 1$ (so $\pi$ puts at least one letter home), the binomial theorem $(1 + x)^m = \sum_{k=0}^{m} \binom{m}{k} x^k$ evaluated at $x = -1$ gives

$$\sum_{k=0}^{m} (-1)^k \binom{m}{k} = (1 - 1)^m = 0,$$

so every arrangement with one or more fixed letters cancels out completely. Finally, expanding $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ cancels the $(n-k)!$ factors: $\binom{n}{k}(n-k)! = \frac{n!}{k!}$. We have proved:

$$D_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n-k)! = n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$$ — inclusion–exclusion (pattern P3).

✎ Checkpoint. Own the zigzag before moving on. Compute $D_5$ by hand: $120 - 5\cdot 24 + 10\cdot 6 - 10\cdot 2 + 5\cdot 1 - 1$. Then compute the two fractions $D_5/5! = 44/120$ and $D_4/4! = 9/24$ — and notice how close together they already sit. Pocket that observation; §1.4 will explain it.

1.2 Second attack: explain the objects to themselves

The formula above is an answer. But a recurrence is an explanation, and explanations are worth more. Follow letter number $1$. It must land in some envelope $i \ne 1$: there are $n - 1$ choices.

✎ Checkpoint. Try the rest yourself before reading on: what can you say about where letter $i$ goes? Find the two possible situations — one is a plain swap, and one hides a relabeling that is the entire trick.

Now there are exactly two situations:

  1. Envelope $1$ gets letter $i$ back — a swap. The remaining $n - 2$ letters must all go wrong among themselves: $D_{n-2}$ ways.
  2. Envelope $1$ does not get letter $i$. Then remove letter $1$ and envelope $i$ — both are spent — and relabel envelope $1$ as $i$. Now letter $i$ is forbidden the relabelled envelope $i$, and every other remaining letter is forbidden its own envelope: exactly the derangement problem one size down, $D_{n-1}$ ways. (Check the bookkeeping on $n = 3$: letter $1 \to$ envelope $3$, and letter $3$ avoids envelope $1$. Remove letter $1$ and envelope $3$; relabel envelope $1$ as "$3$". Now letters $\{2, 3\}$ must derange on envelopes $\{2, 3^*\}$ — $D_2 = 1$ way ✓, namely letter $2 \to$ envelope $1$ and letter $3 \to$ envelope $2$.)

$$D_n = (n - 1)(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2}) \quad \text{for all } n \ge 2, \quad \text{with } D_0 = 1, \; D_1 = 0.$$

Check: $D_4 = 3 \times (2 + 1) = 9$ ✓. (Two seeds power the machine: $D_0 = 1$ — the empty arrangement — and $D_1 = 0$ — a single letter cannot go wrong alone. The recurrence then produces $D_2 = 1\cdot(0 + 1) = 1$, the swap, and the whole sequence is pinned.) This little machine explains why the numbers grow: each derangement is built from smaller derangements plus one honest choice. (Pattern P4: recurrence built by following a forced case analysis.)

1.3 Third attack: package the whole sequence into one object

Here is a thought that looks lazy and is secretly profound. Every permutation is: a set of letters left in their correct envelopes, glued to a derangement of the rest. Sets of letters are counted by $1 + x + x^2/2! + \dots = e^x$. All permutations are counted by $\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n! \frac{x^n}{n!}$. So if $D(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} D_n \frac{x^n}{n!}$ is the derangement bookkeeping machine:

$$e^x \cdot D(x) = \frac{1}{1-x}, \quad \text{that is,} \quad \mathbf{D(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{1-x}}.$$

One line — but why may we multiply? Because gluing two structures built on disjoint label sets obeys a clean law, and the law is double counting in disguise. If structure A can be built on $k$ of the $n$ labels — chosen in $\binom{n}{k}$ ways — and structure B on the remaining $n-k$, then the number of glued structures is $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k}$: the binomial coefficient's whole job is to choose which labels belong to A. And multiplying the bookkeeping machines performs exactly this sum, term by term:

$$\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n \frac{x^n}{n!}\right) \cdot \left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n \frac{x^n}{n!}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k}\right] \frac{x^n}{n!}.$$

So the sentence "every permutation is a set of fixed letters glued to a derangement of the rest" translates mechanically into $e^x \cdot D(x) = \frac{1}{1-x}$, or $D(x) = e^{-x} \cdot \frac{1}{1-x}$. Now unpack the coefficient of $x^n$ on both sides by multiplying the two Taylor series

$$e^{-x} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} x^k \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} x^m.$$

A term of degree $n$ in the product arises by pairing the $x^k$ term of $e^{-x}$ with the $x^{n-k}$ term of $\frac{1}{1-x}$ for each $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$:

$$\frac{D_n}{n!} = [x^n]\left(e^{-x} \cdot \frac{1}{1-x}\right) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!} \cdot 1 = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$

Multiplying through by $n!$ recovers $D_n = n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$. The alternating formula of Section 1.1 falls out of the algebra by itself, without our having to track a single overlap by hand. When a bookkeeping machine reproduces an answer without being told the zigzag, that is a sign you have found the true structure. (Pattern P5: generating functions — a whole sequence treated as a single object.)

1.4 The punchline: nature leaves a fingerprint

✎ Checkpoint. Predict before the reveal: $D_7/7! = 1854/5040$ and $D_8/8! = 14833/40320$. Compute both as decimals, commit in ink to a guess about where the fractions are heading, and only then read on.

Divide by $n!$: $D_n/n! = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k/k! \to \mathbf{1/e \approx 0.3679}$ as $n$ grows. A random arrangement of a million letters has about a $36.8\%$ chance of getting every single one wrong — essentially the same chance as for six letters ($265/720 \approx 36.8\%$). The number $e$, which you met in calculus as a limit of $(1+1/n)^n$, shows up here as a probability about universal mess-making. Nature is economical with her ideas; she reuses them without telling you.

What you now own: count-the-complement with honest overlaps (P3); recurrence-as-explanation (P4); generating-function bookkeeping (P5); and the reflex — when everything must avoid everything, count the complement, but respect the overlap.

Lecture 2 · The Mirror World: the Catalan Numbers

Now a problem that looks like a children's game and hides one of the deepest numbers in mathematics. A walker starts at the corner of a square city grid, at $(0,0)$, and wants to reach the opposite corner $(n,n)$, taking only steps East and North. Nothing special so far: $\binom{2n}{n}$ paths. But this walker is superstitious — she refuses ever to be above the diagonal line $y = x$. At every moment, "north so far" must not exceed "east so far." How many paths?

For $n = 3$: of the $20$ total paths, exactly $5$ survive. Five — the Catalan number $C_3$. (Mind your fingers: our sequence counts from $C_0 = 1$, so $C_3$ is the fourth term; "the fifth Catalan number" is exactly the off-by-one this course is training out of you. Compute $n = 2$ yourself; you should find $2$. Do it — with a pencil — before reading on. This is not optional; the pencil is where the understanding happens.)

2.1 The mirror trick

Count the good paths directly? Hard. Count the bad paths — and this is the move to learn (pattern P6) — by finding a mirror image of them in a world we can count.

A bad path touches the line $y = x + 1$ at some first moment. Take the path up to that moment and reflect it across the line $y = x + 1$ — every East step becomes a North step and vice versa, and the path is reborn in the mirror world. Let me show you one, $n = 2$: the bad path E, N, N, E first touches the forbidden line at its third step. Reflect those three steps: E,N,N $\to$ N,E,E. The mirror path is N,E,E,E — a path that now ends at $(3,1)$ instead of $(2,2)$.

(What, geometrically, is this swap? Reflection across any line of slope $+1$ exchanges East and North steps; what the line's position decides is only where the picture must be translated afterwards. Two honest descriptions of one map. Mirror in the boundary: reflect the head across the forbidden line $y = x + 1$ — the touch point $(t, t+1)$ stays where it is and the tail stays where it was, but the head now begins at $(-1, 1)$, so the whole path is lifted by $(1, -1)$ to start at the origin again. Mirror in the main diagonal: reflect the head across $y = x$ — the origin stays fixed, but the touch point slides to $(t+1, t)$, and now it is the tail that must shift by $(1, -1)$ to rejoin it. Either way the very same path comes out, ending at $(n+1, n-1)$. The step-swap is the primitive; the two mirrors are costumes for it — and when this chapter says "reflect across $y = x + 1$," it means the first description, translation included.)

Drawn, the pair looks like this ($x$ runs right, $y$ runs up; ⊗ marks the relevant first touch, ╱ the relevant line):

Read the left panel: East along the ground, North twice at $x = 1$, then East into $(2,2)$ — the walker first sets foot on the line $y = x+1$ at ⊗. Swap the head up to ⊗ (E,N,N $\to$ N,E,E) and the path is reborn as the right panel, ending at $(3,1)$ instead. To return, swap the right panel's head up to its own ⊗ — a first touch of $x = y + 1$ — and E,N,N,E comes back. The two ⊗'s lie on different lines. That is the whole trap: hunt the wrong line and some partners (like E,E,E,N) offer no touch to find — the lab below rings exactly that alarm.

The miracle: this map is a bijection for every $n \ge 1$ — and the way to know that is to verify both the endpoint step counts and the preservation of the first-touch index. Along any lattice path starting at $(x_0, y_0) = (0,0)$, the quantity $y_j - x_j$ starts at $0$ and changes by $\pm 1$ at each step $j$. A path $P$ from $(0,0)$ to $(n,n)$ is bad if and only if $y_j - x_j \ge 1$ for some $j$, so by unit-step continuity there is a unique earliest step $m = 2t + 1$ at which $y_m - x_m = 1$ — namely, the first vertex $(x_m, y_m) = (t, t+1)$ on the forbidden line $y = x + 1$, while $y_j - x_j \le 0$ for all $0 \le j < m$. The prefix of $P$ of length $m$ has $t$ East steps and $t + 1$ North steps, and the remaining suffix has $n - t$ East steps and $n - (t + 1)$ North steps. Define $\Phi(P)$ by swapping East and North in the prefix of length $m$. The reflected path $\Phi(P)$ has

$$(t + 1) + (n - t) = n + 1 \text{ East steps} \quad \text{and} \quad t + (n - t - 1) = n - 1 \text{ North steps},$$

so it is an unrestricted lattice path from $(0,0)$ to $(n+1, n-1)$. Crucially, at every vertex $(x'_j, y'_j) = (y_j, x_j)$ of the swapped prefix ($0 \le j \le m$), we have $x'_j - y'_j = y_j - x_j$. Therefore $x'_j - y'_j \le 0$ for all $0 \le j < m$ and $x'_m - y'_m = 1$, which means $(x'_m, y'_m) = (t+1, t)$ is the unique first vertex of $\Phi(P)$ on the line $x = y + 1$.

Conversely, along any unrestricted path $Q$ from $(0,0)$ to $(n+1, n-1)$, the difference $x_j - y_j$ starts at $0$, ends at $(n+1) - (n-1) = 2 \ge 1$, and changes by $\pm 1$ per step, so $Q$ has a unique first vertex $(t+1, t)$ at step $m = 2t + 1$ where $x_m - y_m = 1$ (with $x_j - y_j \le 0$ for all $0 \le j < m$). Defining $\Psi(Q)$ by swapping East and North in the prefix up to step $m$ produces a path to $(n,n)$ whose vertices satisfy $y''_j - x''_j = x_j - y_j \le 0$ for $0 \le j < m$ and $y''_m - x''_m = 1$, so $(t, t+1)$ at step $m$ is its first touch of $y = x + 1$. Because $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ reflect at the exact same first-touch index $m$ in both directions and swapping $E \leftrightarrow N$ twice is the identity, $\Psi(\Phi(P)) = P$ and $\Phi(\Psi(Q)) = Q$. (Careful: the inverse looks for the first touch of $x = y + 1$, not $y = x + 1$ — the path E, E, E, N to $(3,1)$ never touches $y = x + 1$ at all. The lab below machine-tests this inverse in both directions.)

Unrestricted paths of length $2n$ to $(n+1, n-1)$ are easy to count: choose which $n - 1$ of the $2n$ steps are North, in $\binom{2n}{n-1}$ ways. Subtracting the bad paths from all $\binom{2n}{n}$ paths to $(n,n)$ and simplifying the factorials gives:

$$C_n = \binom{2n}{n} - \binom{2n}{n-1} = \frac{(2n)!}{n!\,n!} - \frac{(2n)!}{(n-1)!\,(n+1)!} = \frac{(2n)!}{n!\,n!}\left(1 - \frac{n}{n+1}\right) = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.$$

The ugly answer "$5$ out of $20$" for $n = 3$ is now explained: exactly $\frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{4}$ of all paths to $(3,3)$ behave. When a strange fraction becomes transparent, you have found the right idea.

2.2 The equation the paths obey

Look at any nonempty good path. It starts with an East step (a North step would put her above the diagonal immediately) and, at some point, first returns to the diagonal — closing the first "excursion." A good path is:

empty, or E [a good path] N [a good path]

Read that aloud: a path is nothing, or a matched pair wrapping a smaller path and followed by another. That is self-similarity — the object is made of copies of itself. Here is a good path of size $3$ cut at its first return (⊗ marks the first return to the diagonal):

   The path  E E N N E N:

     y=3  ·    ·    ·    ●
                         │
     y=2  ·    ·    ⊗————●
                    │
     y=1  ·    ·    ●    ·
                    │
     y=0  ●————●————●    ·
          0    1    2    3   (x)

Read the shape: the opening E starts the first excursion; the closing N of that excursion lands on ⊗. Inside the pair sat $A = \text{E N}$ (a good path of size $1$, translated); after it marches $B = \text{E N}$ (another). The path is $\text{E } [A] \text{ N } [B]$ with $|A| = 1, |B| = 1$ — and summing over the splits $0+2, 1+1, 2+0$ gives $C_3 = 1\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 1 + 2\cdot 1 = 5$ ✓.

✎ Checkpoint. Now try the translation yourself before seeing it done. If $C(x)$ is the bookkeeping machine for good paths, what should the sentence "a path is empty, or E [path] N [path]" say about $C(x)$? (Decide first what one wrapped pair E…N should cost — there is exactly one natural choice.)

Now the physicist's move. Define the bookkeeping machine $C(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} C_n x^n$ — the ordinary generating function: a path of size $n$ contributes one unit of $x^n$. Notice what is absent here compared with Lecture 1.3, where $D(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} D_n x^n/n!$ carried a factorial in every term. That factorial was load-bearing: derangements live on labeled letters, and gluing two labeled structures forces a choose-which-labels binomial — the $1/n!$ factors are exactly what tames it. A path carries no labels: its decomposition splits it by position — first excursion, then the rest — and the sizes of the pieces are read off the path itself, with nothing to choose. No binomial appears, so no factorial is needed. Exponential bookkeeping for labeled worlds; ordinary bookkeeping for unlabeled ones — a distinction that will earn its own lecture the next time it matters. The sentence above, translated into bookkeeping (one wrapped pair costs a factor $x$), is an equation:

$$C(x) = 1 + x \cdot C(x)^2.$$

That equation is the Catalan structure: $x C(x)^2 - C(x) + 1 = 0$. Comparing coefficients of $x^n$ directly in $C(x) = 1 + x C(x)^2$ gives the convolution recurrence $C_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} C_k C_{n-1-k}$ for $n \ge 1$ with $C_0 = 1$ (check $C_3 = 1\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 1 + 2\cdot 1 = 5$). Better still, we can solve the quadratic for $C(x)$ and extract the closed form without touching a mirror. For $x \ne 0$, the quadratic formula gives

$$C(x) = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2x}.$$

Which sign does nature choose? Since $C(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n x^n$ is continuous near $x = 0$ with $C(0) = C_0 = 1$, we must have $\lim_{x \to 0} C(x) = 1$. Because $\sqrt{1 - 4x} = 1 - 2x + O(x^2)$ as $x \to 0$, the plus branch diverges like $\frac{1}{x}$, whereas the minus branch satisfies $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - (1 - 2x + O(x^2))}{2x} = 1$. Thus $C(x) = \frac{1 - (1 - 4x)^{1/2}}{2x}$ for $|x| < \frac{1}{4}$. Now expand $(1 - 4x)^{1/2}$ by Newton's generalized binomial theorem:

$$(1 - 4x)^{1/2} = 1 + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \binom{1/2}{m} (-4x)^m.$$

For $m = 1$, the term is $\binom{1/2}{1}(-4)^1 x = -2x = -\frac{2}{1}\binom{0}{0}x$. For every integer $m \ge 2$, the numerator of $\binom{1/2}{m}$ has $m$ factors — namely $\frac{1}{2}$ followed by the $m - 1$ negative half-integers $-\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{3}{2}, \dots, -\frac{2m-3}{2}$:

$$\binom{1/2}{m} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)\cdots\left(-\frac{2m-3}{2}\right)}{m!} = \frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{2^m \, m!} \prod_{j=1}^{m-1} (2j - 1).$$

Multiply numerator and denominator of this $(m-1)$-factor odd product by the $(m-1)$-factor even product $\prod_{j=1}^{m-1} (2j) = 2^{m-1}(m-1)!$ to complete the factorial $(2m-2)!$:

$$\binom{1/2}{m}(-4)^m = \frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{2^m \, m!} \cdot \frac{(2m-2)!}{2^{m-1}(m-1)!} \cdot (-1)^m 2^{2m} = -\frac{2}{m}\frac{(2m-2)!}{(m-1)!\,(m-1)!} = -\frac{2}{m}\binom{2m-2}{m-1}.$$

Since $-\frac{2}{m}\binom{2m-2}{m-1}$ also equals $-2$ at $m = 1$, the formula holds for all $m \ge 1$. Substituting it into $C(x) = \frac{1 - (1 - 4x)^{1/2}}{2x}$ and shifting the summation index via $n = m - 1$ (so $m = n + 1$) yields

$$C(x) = \frac{1}{2x} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{m}\binom{2m-2}{m-1} x^m = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n} x^n.$$

Extracting $[x^n]C(x)$ gives $C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$ for all $n \ge 0$, streaming out of pure algebra and matching the mirror world of Section 2.1. Pattern P4/P5 again: decompose at the first return; write the self-similarity as an equation.

Lecture 3 · Count It Twice

The single most productive trick in this entire book fits in one sentence: count the same set two different ways and set the answers equal.

3.1 The committee

From $m$ women and $n$ men, choose a committee of $r$. Obvious answer: $\binom{m+n}{r}$. But classify the same committees by composition — $k$ women and $r-k$ men: $\sum_{k=0}^{r} \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k}$. The two bookkeepings count one world, so:

$$\binom{m+n}{r} = \sum_{k=0}^{r} \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k} \quad \text{— Vandermonde.}$$

Notice what happened: the identity was not algebra; it was an observation about the world dressed in symbols. Two ways to prove it anyway: (i) multiply $(1+x)^m(1+x)^n = (1+x)^{m+n}$ and compare the coefficient of $x^r$ — the bookkeeping machine again; (ii) lattice paths: paths from $(0,0)$ to $(m+n-r, r)$ take $m+n$ steps, $r$ of them North; classify them by $k$, the height at which a path crosses the boundary after step $m$ — the first $m$ steps place their $k$ Norths in $\binom{m}{k}$ ways, the last $n$ steps their $r-k$ Norths in $\binom{n}{r-k}$ ways. Sum over $k$: same identity, third costume. Multiple proofs are not decoration; each proof is a different door into the idea.

3.2 The handshake among subsets

Now the deepest two-line argument in elementary combinatorics.

✎ Checkpoint. Warm the engine by hand first: list all $8$ subsets of $\{1,2,3\}$ and total their sizes — you should get $12$. Now predict, in ink, the average size of a subset of $\{1,\dots,100\}$ before reading the two bookkeepings.

For $n \ge 1$, count ordered pairs $(x, S)$ where $x \in S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$:

  • Choose $S$ first: every $k$-element subset contributes $k$ pairs, giving $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k}$ pairs in all.
  • Choose $x$ first: for each of the $n$ elements $x$, the remaining $n - 1$ elements form $2^{n-1}$ subsets, so $x$ belongs to $2^{n-1}$ subsets $S$; this gives $n 2^{n-1}$ pairs in all.

Thus $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$ for all $n \ge 1$ (and trivially at $n = 0$, where both sides equal $0$). And a third costume: differentiate $(1+x)^n$ at $x = 1$. Three engines, one truth.

The corollary to remember forever: the average subset has $n/2$ elements. Averaging is the baby form of probability, and probability grows into an entire method — one that will later prove things exist without ever constructing them. Plant that seed now; Chapter 4 waters it.

Lecture 4 · The Bit Machine: $2^n$

Shortest lecture of the course. A subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is a row of $n$ switches, each up or down. Switches: $2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2 = \mathbf{2^n}$.

✎ Checkpoint. Before classifying: what must $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}$ equal — and why, using nothing but the switch picture? Say the reason aloud, then check it against the $n = 3$ row of Pascal's triangle.

Classify by the number of "up" switches and you get $\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ — the same world counted by type and by total. The binomial theorem at $x = 1$ says the same thing in formal dress: this is the machine from Lecture 3 with both bookkeepings visible at once.

The switch picture is worth keeping for one reason: it converts statements about sets into statements about strings, where arithmetic is easy. Half the tricks in coding theory and combinatorics start by putting a string costume on a set.

from math import comb, e, factorial

def D_rec(n):                       # Lecture 1.2: D_n = (n-1)(D_{n-1} + D_{n-2}), n >= 2
    if n < 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be >= 0")
    if n == 0:
        return 1
    if n == 1:
        return 0
    D = [1, 0]
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        D.append((i - 1) * (D[i - 1] + D[i - 2]))
    return D[n]

def D_one_step(n):                  # Lecture 1.3 / Ex 8: D_n = n*D_{n-1} + (-1)**n, n >= 1
    if n < 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be >= 0")
    d = 1
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        d = i * d + (-1)**i
    return d

def D_ie(n):                        # Lecture 1.1 & 1.3: inclusion-exclusion / EGF product
    if n < 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be >= 0")
    return sum((-1)**k * comb(n, k) * factorial(n - k) for k in range(n + 1))

def C_rec(n):                       # Lecture 2.2: Catalan first-return recurrence
    if n < 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be >= 0")
    C = [1]
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        C.append(sum(C[k] * C[i - 1 - k] for k in range(i)))
    return C[n]

def C_mirror(n):                    # Lecture 2.1: reflection count comb(2n,n) - comb(2n,n-1)
    if n < 0:
        raise ValueError("n must be >= 0")
    bad = comb(2 * n, n - 1) if n >= 1 else 0
    return comb(2 * n, n) - bad

# Verify all derangement and Catalan engines across boundary cases n = 0..10
assert all(D_rec(n) == D_one_step(n) == D_ie(n) for n in range(11))
assert all(abs(D_rec(n) - factorial(n) / e) < 1 / (n + 1) for n in range(1, 11))
assert all(C_rec(n) == C_mirror(n) == comb(2 * n, n) // (n + 1) for n in range(11))

# Verify Lecture 3 & Lecture 4 double-counting identities (including n = 0)
assert all(
    sum(comb(m, k) * comb(n, r - k) for k in range(r + 1)) == comb(m + n, r)
    for m in range(6) for n in range(6) for r in range(m + n + 1)
)
assert all(sum(comb(n, k) for k in range(n + 1)) == 2**n for n in range(11))
assert all(
    sum(k * comb(n, k) for k in range(n + 1)) == (n * 2**(n - 1) if n >= 1 else 0)
    for n in range(11)

Part 2 — the reflection bijection, machine-tested

A formula can lie about being a bijection — and a small-case test is an error detector, not a substitute for a proof (for instance, the nearest-integer property of Exercise 9 is settled for all $n$ by the alternating-series bound, which no finite run of code can replace). What code adds is a concrete check the eye cannot fake. Here we round-trip Lecture 2.1's reflection map together with its inverse (first touch of $x = y + 1$) over all small paths — the test that separates a genuine bijection from a coincidence of totals:

from itertools import combinations
from math import comb

def all_paths(a, b):
    if a < 0 or b < 0:
        return set()
    n_steps = a + b
    return {
        ''.join('E' if i in east_idx else 'N' for i in range(n_steps))
        for east_idx in combinations(range(n_steps), a)
    }

def first_touch_index(p, target):   # earliest step k where (y - x) == target
    x = y = 0
    for k, s in enumerate(p):
        x += (s == 'E'); y += (s == 'N')
        if y - x == target:
            return k
    return None

def forward(p):    # bad path to (n,n): swap head at first touch of y - x = +1
    k = first_touch_index(p, +1)
    if k is None:
        return None                  # no touch: good path (map undefined)
    return p[:k + 1].translate(str.maketrans('EN', 'NE')) + p[k + 1:]

def inverse(q):    # path to (n+1, n-1): swap head at first touch of x - y = +1
    k = first_touch_index(q, -1)
    if k is None:
        return None
    return q[:k + 1].translate(str.maketrans('EN', 'NE')) + q[k + 1:]

for n in range(5):                   # includes boundary cases n = 0 and n = 1
    total = all_paths(n, n)
    bads = [p for p in total if forward(p) is not None]
    goods = [p for p in total if forward(p) is None]
    images = [forward(p) for p in bads]
    mirror = all_paths(n + 1, n - 1)

    assert len(bads) == len(set(images)) == len(mirror) == (comb(2 * n, n - 1) if n >= 1 else 0)
    assert len(goods) == comb(2 * n, n) // (n + 1)
    assert set(images) == mirror                              # bijection onto mirror world
    assert all(inverse(forward(p)) == p for p in bads)        # round trip, bad side
    assert all(forward(inverse(q)) == q for q in mirror)      # round trip, mirror side
    assert all(                                               # first-touch index preserved
        first_touch_index(p, +1) == first_touch_index(forward(p), -1)
        for p in bads
    )
    if n in (2, 3):
        print("n =", n, "|", len(bads), "bad of", len(total))
        print("      ", len(set(images)), "images,", len(mirror), "mirror paths")

assert forward('EEEN') is None   # the WRONG inverse hunts y = x+1 here: nothing to find

Output of the verification script: For $n = 2$: $4$ bad paths (out of $6$ total), $4$ distinct images, all $4$ paths to $(3,1)$ covered, both round trips exact. For $n = 3$: $15$ bad paths (out of $20$ total, matching $20 - C_3 = 15$), $15$ distinct images, all $15$ paths to $(4,2)$ covered, both round trips exact. And forward('EEEN') returns None: a path to $(3,1)$ need not touch $y = x + 1$ at all — confirming that the inverse map must hunt for the first touch of $x = y + 1$. The lesson: when a proof claims a bijection, implement both the forward map and its inverse and verify the round trips on both sides.

What to explore: Plot $D_n \cdot e / n!$ for $n = 1 \dots 15$ to watch its rapid convergence to $1$, and compare the exact rounding residual $\left|D_n - \frac{n!}{e}\right|$ against the upper bound $\frac{1}{n+1}$ proved in the exercises to see how closely the first omitted term $\frac{1}{n+1}$ tracks the true error.

A8. From $D(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{1-x}$, multiply both sides by $(1 - x)$ to obtain $(1 - x)D(x) = e^{-x}$, or

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{D_n}{n!} x^n - \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_{n-1}}{(n-1)!} x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!} x^n.$$

For every integer $n \ge 1$, extracting the coefficient of $x^n$ on both sides gives

$$\frac{D_n}{n!} - \frac{D_{n-1}}{(n-1)!} = \frac{(-1)^n}{n!}.$$

Multiplying through by $n!$ (and noting $\frac{n!}{(n-1)!} = n$) yields $D_n - n D_{n-1} = (-1)^n$, or $D_n = n D_{n-1} + (-1)^n$ for all $n \ge 1$. ∎ Check: $D_5 = 44 = 5\cdot 9 - 1$ ✓. (Equivalently, in the normalized EGF basis $\frac{x^n}{n!}$, we have $x D(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n D_{n-1} \frac{x^n}{n!}$, so matching $\left[\frac{x^n}{n!}\right]$ for $n \ge 1$ gives $D_n - n D_{n-1} = (-1)^n$ directly.)

A9. Since $\frac{1}{e} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$ and $\frac{D_n}{n!} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$, multiplying their difference by $n!$ gives

$$R_n := (-1)^{n+1}\left(\frac{n!}{e} - D_n\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{j-1} u_{n+j}, \quad \text{where } u_k = \frac{n!}{k!}.$$

For all $k \ge n + 1$, we have $u_k - u_{k+1} = \frac{n!}{k!}\left(1 - \frac{1}{k+1}\right) > 0$, so $(u_k)_{k \ge n+1}$ is strictly positive and strictly decreasing to $0$. Grouping the terms of $R_n$ into consecutive pairs in two ways gives both a strict lower bound and a strict upper bound:

$$R_n = (u_{n+1} - u_{n+2}) + (u_{n+3} - u_{n+4}) + \cdots > 0,$$

$$R_n = u_{n+1} - (u_{n+2} - u_{n+3}) - (u_{n+4} - u_{n+5}) - \cdots < u_{n+1} = \frac{1}{n+1}.$$

Because $0 < R_n < \frac{1}{n+1}$, we have $\left|D_n - \frac{n!}{e}\right| = R_n < \frac{1}{n+1}$. For every integer $n \ge 1$, $\frac{1}{n+1} \le \frac{1}{2}$, so $\left|D_n - \frac{n!}{e}\right| < \frac{1}{2}$ holds strictly for all $n \ge 1$ (even at $n = 1$, where $\left|0 - \frac{1}{e}\right| = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679 < \frac{1}{2}$). A11. First solve the weak ballot problem: for integers $p \ge q \ge 0$, count lattice paths from $(0,0)$ to $(p,q)$ that stay weakly below the diagonal ($y \le x$). If $q = 0$, every path to $(p,0)$ consists solely of East steps along $y = 0 \le x$, so all $\binom{p}{0} = 1 = \frac{p - 0 + 1}{p + 1}\binom{p + 0}{0}$ paths are good. Now assume $p \ge q \ge 1$. A path from $(0,0)$ to $(p,q)$ is bad if and only if it touches the line $y = x + 1$. By the same first-touch bijection as in Lecture 2.1, swapping East and North steps in the prefix up to the first vertex $(t, t+1)$ on $y = x + 1$ maps bad paths to $(p,q)$ bijectively onto unrestricted paths from $(0,0)$ to $(p + 1, q - 1)$ (whose inverse reflects at the first vertex where $x - y = 1$, which exists because $x - y$ goes from $0$ to $(p+1) - (q-1) = p - q + 2 \ge 2$ in steps of $\pm 1$). Since $q \ge 1$, there are $\binom{p+q}{q-1}$ bad paths, and the number of good weak paths to $(p,q)$ is

$$\binom{p+q}{q} - \binom{p+q}{q-1} = \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!} - \frac{(p+q)!}{(p+1)!\,(q-1)!} = \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!}\left(1 - \frac{q}{p+1}\right) = \frac{p - q + 1}{p + 1}\binom{p+q}{q},$$

which therefore holds for all $p \ge q \ge 0$.

Now the strict ballot problem ($a > b \ge 0$): Candidate A is strictly ahead after every ballot ($x > y$ at every step after the origin) if and only if the very first ballot goes to A (a step from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$) and the remaining $a + b - 1$ steps from $(1,0)$ to $(a,b)$ satisfy $x - 1 \ge y$ at every vertex. Shifting the origin to $(1,0)$ translates these remaining steps into paths from $(0,0)$ to $(a - 1, b)$ that stay weakly below the diagonal. Since $a > b \ge 0$ implies $p = a - 1 \ge b = q \ge 0$, applying the weak formula with $p = a - 1$ and $q = b$ gives

$$\frac{(a - 1) - b + 1}{(a - 1) + 1}\binom{a + b - 1}{b} = \frac{a - b}{a} \cdot \frac{(a + b - 1)!}{(a - 1)!\,b!} = \frac{a - b}{a} \cdot \frac{a}{a + b}\binom{a + b}{b} = \frac{a - b}{a + b}\binom{a + b}{b}. \quad \blacksquare$$

(Check $a = 2, b = 1$: $\frac{2-1}{2+1}\binom{3}{1} = 1$, namely AAB; check $a \ge 1, b = 0$: $\frac{a}{a}\binom{a}{0} = 1$ ✓.)

A13. Bijection (P1): Represent $n$ as a row of $n$ unit tokens with $n - 1$ interior gaps between adjacent tokens.   • a left $(k+1)$-gon $P_L$ on vertices $(0, 1, \dots, k)$ with distinguished base edge $(0, k)$, triangulated by a sub-triangulation $T_L$ of size $k - 1$; and
  • a right $(n-k+2)$-gon $P_R$ on vertices $(k, k+1, \dots, n+1)$ with distinguished base edge $(k, n+1)$, triangulated by a sub-triangulation $T_R$ of size $n - k$.

Define $\phi(T)$ recursively by wrapping the left code in an outer pair of parentheses and concatenating the right code:

$$\phi(T) = \texttt{(} \, \phi(T_L) \, \texttt{)} \, \phi(T_R).$$

By induction on $n$, $\phi(T_L)$ is a balanced string of length $2(k-1)$ and $\phi(T_R)$ is a balanced string of length $2(n-k)$, so $\phi(T)$ is a balanced parenthesis string of length $2 + 2(k-1) + 2(n-k) = 2n$.

For $n = 0$, the empty string $\varepsilon$ maps back to the empty triangulation of a single edge. For $n \ge 1$, any non-empty balanced parenthesis string $w$ of length $2n$ begins with ( and has a unique first return to balance — the shortest non-empty balanced prefix, which must close the opening parenthesis at some even position $2k$ ($1 \le k \le n$). Thus $w$ factors uniquely as

$$w = \texttt{(} \, u \, \texttt{)} \, v,$$

where $u$ is a balanced string of length $2(k-1)$ and $v$ is a balanced string of length $2(n-k)$. This unique length parameter $k = \frac{|u|}{2} + 1 \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ determines the apex $k$ of the base triangle $(0, k, n+1)$, while the induction hypothesis uniquely reconstructs the sub-triangulation $T_L = \phi^{-1}(u)$ on $(0, 1, \dots, k)$ and $T_R = \phi^{-1}(v)$ on $(k, k+1, \dots, n+1)$.
