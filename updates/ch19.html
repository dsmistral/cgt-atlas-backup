Chapter 19 · The Lovász Local Lemma

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

Chapter 4 taught the union bound's honest arithmetic: if the failure probabilities sum below one, something survives. This chapter is about what to do when they don't — when there are ten billion bad events, each vanishingly rare, each touching only a handful of the others. The union bound charges every bad event against a single global budget. The Lovász Local Lemma discovers that the budget is local: an event only needs to defend against its own neighborhood, and rare events with sparse overlap cannot gang up. The lemma was born at the Keszthely conference of 1973, in a problem list Erdős and Lovász wrote on $3$-chromatic hypergraphs (published 1975): they needed to $2$-color hypergraphs whose edges were far too many for the union bound, and proved, in passing, the survival principle that has carried the probabilistic method ever since. Spencer made it asymmetric and sharpenable (1977). Shearer found the exact boundary of what any such lemma can say (1985). Erdős and Spencer made it lopsided to handle random permutations and Latin transversals (1991). And in 2009–10, Moser and Tardos did what nobody expected: they made the lemma run — a trivial-sounding resampling algorithm whose termination proof is the lemma, constructive at last.

Lecture 1 · Ten Billion Bad Events: the Problem That Made the Lemma

Encounter. Color the edges of a complete graph red and blue and you are guaranteed a monochromatic triangle — that is $R(3,3) = 6$, Chapter 3's opening scandal. Turn the question inside out and you get the problem that created this chapter: color the edges of a sparse hypergraph — each edge a set of $k$ vertices — with two colors so that no edge is monochromatic. Erdős had been circling such questions for a decade. The random experiment is the obvious one: flip a fair coin for every vertex. Any one edge comes out monochromatic with probability $2 \cdot 2^{-k} = 2^{1-k}$ — hopelessly rare when $k$ is, say, $40$. And an $m$-edge hypergraph has $m$ chances to go wrong.

The union bound (P13) adds the ledger: probability of some failure $\le m \cdot 2^{1-k}$. If that is below $1$, a good coloring exists. So a hypergraph with $m < 2^{k-1}$ edges is $2$-colorable — and that is all the union bound will ever say. Read the ceiling carefully: $2^{k-1}$ is a cap on the total number of edges, with no regard for how many vertices the hypergraph has. A $40$-uniform hypergraph on a million vertices with a million edges sails far over the cap — even though each edge's collisions with the rest of the world might be a few dozen.

Two observations, one comfortable and one that should sting. Comfortable: every instance the certificate approves is satisfiable — $800$ census instances, each brute-checked (all $4{,}096$ assignments) and certificate-checked, zero conflicts, the certificate never lies. Stinging: at these sizes the certificate is far weaker than the truth (everything is satisfiable), and the union bound — which approves every instance with $m \le 7$ — actually out-certificates the local lemma's symmetric form. The local certificate is not a uniformly better union bound. It is a differently shaped one, and the shape is the whole point.

Conjecture. The union bound's ledger is global: every bad event, however remote from the others, charges the same single budget. But two events that share no variables are independent — the failure of one says nothing about the other. The global ledger overcharges hopelessly. What a rare event should actually have to defend against is only its neighborhood: the handful of bad events entangled with its variables. If each event is rare relative to how sparse that entanglement is, the whole system should survive. Erdős and Lovász, writing up the problem list of the 1973 Keszthely conference (published 1975), proved the survival principle in one paragraph and used it to $2$-color hypergraphs far beyond the union bound's reach.

Explain. Say it as a theorem. Events $A_1,\dots,A_n$ with $p_i = \Pr[A_i]$, and a dependency graph $\Gamma$: connect $i$ to $j$ when the two are not provably independent — formally, each $A_i$ must be mutually independent of the entire joint family of events $\{A_j : j \notin \{i\} \cup \Gamma(i)\}$ (pairwise independence does not suffice: conditioning on any conjunction of non-neighbors must leave $\Pr[A_i]$ unchanged). In the variable model, where each $A_i$ is determined by a subset $S_i$ of independent random variables, connecting $i \sim j$ whenever $S_i \cap S_j \neq \emptyset$ automatically guarantees this joint independence because $A_i$ depends only on the coordinates in $S_i$ while all non-neighbors depend only on the disjoint set of coordinates outside $S_i$. Fewer edges is a stronger statement; you choose $\Gamma$, and the art of choosing is Exercise 11's subject. Write $d$ for the maximum degree of $\Gamma$.

The Lovász Local Lemma (symmetric form; Erdős–Lovász 1975). If each $p_i \le p$ and each event depends on at most $d$ others, and
$$e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1,$$
then $\Pr[\text{no } A_i \text{ occurs}] > 0$. In particular a point avoiding all bad events exists.

The $1/e$ is the price of the induction that proves it (Lecture 2) — and the $+1$ inside is easy to misplace; $d+1$, not $d$, because the gang of an event is its $d$ neighbors plus itself. The lemma's shape is exactly the checkpoint guess: rarity compared against local crowding, with $e$ as the exchange rate.

✎ Micro-proof read-through, the two-clause case. Two clauses sharing no variables: $p = 1/8$ each, $d = 0$, $e \cdot (1/8) \cdot 1 = 0.34 \le 1$ — certified, as it must be, since the events are literally independent. Two clauses sharing a variable: $d = 1$, $e \cdot (1/8) \cdot 2 = 0.68 \le 1$ — still certified, though the union bound would have been just as happy. Three clauses all mutually entangled: $d = 2$, $e \cdot (1/8) \cdot 3 = 1.02 > 1$ — the certificate shuts off. The lab's Part 1 measures exactly this collapse: mean $D = 1.62$ at $m = 3$ ($36\%$ certified), $3.31$ at $m = 5$ (none).

Generalize. The dice circle. Stand $100$ fair dice in a circle and call $A_i$ the event die $i$ and die $i+1$ both show six — $p = 1/36$ each, and $A_i$ shares outcomes only with $A_{i-1}$ and $A_{i+1}$: a dependency cycle, $d = 2$. The certificate: $e \cdot (1/36) \cdot 3 = 0.226 \le 1$ — survival certified. The union bound: $\sum p_i = 100/36 \approx 2.8$ — dead. Here is the lemma's signature move in one line: a hundred events whose total failure mass is nearly three full probability budgets, surviving because no local gang can form. (Of course, for dice you could shortcut — the no-six assignment avoids everything, and it is a product event with probability $(5/6)^{100}$. The shortcut is exactly what $k$-SAT and hypergraph coloring do not offer: there, "avoid all bad events" is not a single product event on the vertices, and no closed-form shortcut exists. The lemma is the uniform argument that works when shortcuts die — that is its job description.)

And now the honest generalization, the one the receipts force: certificates are incomparable, not ranked. The lab's Part 1 table records instances ($m \le 7$, small $n$) where the union bound certifies and the symmetric lemma cannot — the global ledger wins whenever events are few and probabilities tiny, because the $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1)$ shape is tuned for many events with sparse overlap. Part 1's second panel ($n = 48$, $m \ge 8$: total failure mass $\ge 1$, union dead) is where the local certificate earns its keep: $13\%$ and $2\%$ of instances certified at $m = 8$ and $10$, against union's flat zero. What to learn: when failure probabilities sum below one, take the union bound and go home (P13, cheaper). When they don't, count the degree of entanglement — if each event's gang is small next to $1/(e \cdot p)$, survival is still provable (P45). Diagnosing which regime you are in is the chapter's first skill.

Lecture 2 · The General Form, and the Induction That Proves It

The symmetric form hides the lemma's real engine. The sharpened statement — due in this shape to Spencer (1977), the paper whose Ramsey applications we meet in Lecture 6 — gives every event its own exchange rate:

The Local Lemma (general asymmetric form). Suppose there exist numbers $x_1,\dots,x_n \in (0,1)$ with
$$p_i \le x_i \cdot \prod_{j \in \Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j) \quad \text{for every } i.$$
Then $\Pr[\text{no } A_i \text{ occurs}] \ge \prod_i (1 - x_i) > 0$.

Read the certificate as a bargaining table: event $i$ may claim budget $x_i$, but every neighbor $j$ must simultaneously survive with probability $1-x_j$, and the neighbors' survival discounts $i$'s allowance. The symmetric form is the special case where every seat bargains identically.

✎ Checkpoint. Derive the symmetric constant yourself before looking: set every $x_i = x$ and every degree $= d$. For which $x$ is $x(1-x)^d$ largest, and how does $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$ fall out? (This derivation is complete in the text below and again as Exercise 3 — it is the chapter's most reused computation.)

From general to symmetric, completely. Maximize $f(x) = x(1-x)^d$ over $x \in (0,1)$. Differentiate: $f'(x) = (1-x)^d - dx(1-x)^{d-1} = (1-x)^{d-1}\cdot(1 - x - dx) = (1-x)^{d-1}\cdot(1 - (d+1)x)$. Zero at $x = 1/(d+1)$; $f$ there is $(1/(d+1))\cdot(d/(d+1))^d = d^d/(d+1)^{d+1}$. So the uniform certificate exists exactly when $p \le d^d/(d+1)^{d+1}$, and the classical loose form $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$ follows because $(1+1/d)^d < e$: $d^d/(d+1)^{d+1} = 1/((d+1)\cdot(1+1/d)^d) > 1/(e(d+1))$. The $1/e$ lost in the last step is pure slack — real proofs keep $d^d/(d+1)^{d+1}$. ($d = 2$: the exact uniform ceiling is $4/27 \approx 0.1481$ against the loose $1/(3e) \approx 0.1226$ — Lecture 5's lab measures this gap from the inside.)

The floor. The general form pays a bonus the symmetric statement doesn't mention: the theorem's conclusion is not merely positive probability but the explicit floor $\Pr[\text{all survive}] \ge \prod(1-x_i)$. A survival probability you can compute, print, and compare — the lab's Part 5 ranks three such floors against the exact truth on a line of events, and the floors' ordering is the chapter's sharpest practical lesson: a certificate is also a quantitative guarantee, and different certificates give wildly different floors to the same process.

The proof, complete by nested induction. The theorem is proved by strong induction on the size $|S|$ of a conditioning set $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\} \setminus \{i\}$, establishing both that $\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] > 0$ and the conditional bound:

Claim (Spencer 1977). For every subset $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$ and every $i \notin S$, if $\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] > 0$ then
$$\Pr\left[A_i \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] \le x_i.$$

Base case and Case A ($S$ contains no neighbor of $i$). When $S \cap \Gamma(i) = \emptyset$ (which includes $|S| = 0$), $A_i$ is mutually independent of $\{A_j : j \in S\}$ by the definition of the dependency graph $\Gamma$. Hence conditioning on $\bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j$ leaves the probability of $A_i$ unchanged: $\Pr\left[A_i \mid \bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] = \Pr[A_i] = p_i \le x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j) \le x_i$, since each factor $(1 - x_j)$ lies in $(0,1)$.

Inductive step, Case B ($S$ contains neighbors of $i$). Fix $|S| \ge 1$ and assume the Claim holds for all conditioning sets of size strictly smaller than $|S|$. Note first that by the induction hypothesis applied sequentially to the elements of $S = \{s_1,\dots,s_r\}$, we have $\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] = \prod_{t=1}^r \left(1 - \Pr\left[A_{s_t} \mid \bigwedge_{u<t} \bar{A}_{s_u}\right]\right) \ge \prod_{j\in S} (1 - x_j) > 0$, so the conditioning event has strictly positive probability. Now partition $S$ into neighbors $S_1 = S \cap \Gamma(i) = \{j_1, j_2, \dots, j_k\}$ (with $k \ge 1$) and non-neighbors $S_2 = S \setminus \Gamma(i)$. By the definition of conditional probability, keeping $S_2$ in the background conditioning throughout,

$$\Pr\left[A_i \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] = \frac{\Pr\left[A_i \wedge \bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right]}{\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right]}.$$

We bound the numerator from above and the denominator from below. For the numerator, dropping the events $\bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j$ can only increase the probability, and since $S_2$ contains no neighbors of $i$, Case A applies directly to give

$$\Pr\left[A_i \wedge \bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right] \le \Pr\left[A_i \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right] = \Pr[A_i] = p_i \le x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j).$$

For the denominator, telescope the conjunction over $S_1 = \{j_1,\dots,j_k\}$ via the chain rule:

$$\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right] = \prod_{r=1}^k \left(1 - \Pr\left[A_{j_r} \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{t=1}^{r-1} \bar{A}_{j_t} \wedge \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right]\right).$$

Each conditioning set $S^{(r)} = \{j_1,\dots,j_{r-1}\} \cup S_2$ avoids $j_r$ and has cardinality $|S^{(r)}| = r - 1 + |S_2| \le (k - 1) + (|S| - k) = |S| - 1 < |S|$. By the strong induction hypothesis applied to event $j_r$ and conditioning set $S^{(r)}$, we have $\Pr\left[A_{j_r} \mid \bigwedge_{m\in S^{(r)}} \bar{A}_m\right] \le x_{j_r}$. Therefore the denominator is at least $\prod_{r=1}^k (1 - x_{j_r}) = \prod_{j\in S_1} (1 - x_j)$. Dividing the numerator bound by the denominator bound and canceling $\prod_{j\in S_1} (1 - x_j) > 0$ yields

$$\Pr\left[A_i \,\middle|\, \bigwedge_{j\in S} \bar{A}_j\right] \le \frac{x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j)}{\prod_{j\in S_1} (1 - x_j)} = x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)\setminus S_1} (1 - x_j) \le x_i.$$

This completes the induction. Finally, chaining the Claim across $T = \{1,\dots,n\}$ one event at a time gives the explicit survival floor: $\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{i=1}^n \bar{A}_i\right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \left(1 - \Pr\left[A_i \mid \bigwedge_{j=1}^{i-1} \bar{A}_j\right]\right) \ge \prod_{i=1}^n (1 - x_i) > 0$. Every step of the bargaining table is accounted for: each neighbor $j \in \Gamma(i)$ charges its survival factor $(1 - x_j)$ against $i$'s allowance $x_i$ so that when the denominator telescopes over any subset of neighbors $S_1 \subseteq \Gamma(i)$, the exact factors needed to cancel the denominator are waiting in the numerator.

Why asymmetric earns its keep — the star, previewed. The general certificate's strength is that rare events may sit in crowded neighborhoods, and crowded common events may lean on rare ones. Build the lab's Part 6 instance: one hub clause of width $4$ ($p_h = 1/16$) and eight satellite clauses of width $3$ ($p_s = 1/8$), each satellite sharing exactly one variable with the hub and none with each other. Three certificates, three verdicts:

  • Union bound: $\sum p = 1/16 + 8\cdot(1/8) = 1.0625 > 1$ — fails.
  • Symmetric lemma: the hub has $d = 8$, so $e\cdot(1/8)\cdot 9 = 3.06 > 1$ — fails.
  • General form: solve the two-variable bargaining system $x_h = (1/16)/(1-x_s)^8$, $x_s = (1/8)/(1-x_h)$. The iteration from zero converges (monotone increasing, the map is increasing in each coordinate): $x_h = 0.3064$, $x_s = 0.1802$ — both below $1$, the certificate holds, a satisfying assignment exists. The brute-force census agrees: $373{,}729$ of $1{,}048{,}576$ assignments satisfy all nine clauses.

The satellite crowd is individually cheap to forgive (each rare-ish, each only leaning on the hub), and the hub is rare enough to absorb eight leanings. No uniform exchange rate can say that; the bargaining table can. What to learn: the general form is not a technicality — it is the lemma learning to price heterogeneous neighborhoods. When a symmetric check fails, do not abandon the Lemma: retune the $x$'s (Exercise 5 hands you a second star to price). When the general form fails too, the crowd is genuinely dangerous — Lecture 4's lopsidedness or Lecture 5's algorithm may still save you.

Lecture 3 · Two Ceilings: Hypergraph Coloring, $k$-SAT, and the Shape That Separates Them

Encounter. Return to the problem that created the lemma. A $k$-uniform hypergraph — every edge a set of $k$ vertices — should have its vertices colored red and blue so that no edge is monochromatic. The random experiment: fair coin per vertex. One edge goes wrong with probability $2\cdot(1/2)^k = \mathbf{2^{1-k}}$. The lemma wants a dependency graph: $A_e$ and $A_f$ are entangled exactly when the edges share a vertex (given all other vertices' colors, the two events still interact through the shared ones); disjoint edges are independent. So the degree $d$ of the dependency graph is bounded by the number of edges that collide with a given edge — call the realized maximum $D$.

Explain, completely. The symmetric certificate for the coloring experiment reads

$$e \cdot 2^{1-k} \cdot (D+1) \le 1.$$

For $k = 4$ that is $p = 1/8$ and $D \le 1$: each edge may collide with at most one other edge of the whole hypergraph. The general form sharpens it, and for once the sharpening can be finished by hand: with uniform exchange rate $x$, an edge with $D$ neighbors is certifiable exactly when $x(1-x)^D \ge 1/8$. For $D = 2$, $x = 1/3$ gives $(1/3)(2/3)^2 = 4/27 = 0.1481 \ge 0.125$ — certified. For $D = 3$, the best uniform $x$ is $1/4$, giving $(1/4)(3/4)^3 = 27/256 = 0.1055 < 0.125$ — and on any $3$-regular (or minimum-degree $\ge 3$) dependency graph, no asymmetric assignment can rescue $p = 1/8$ either: at whichever event $i^*$ attains the minimum seat value $x_{i^*} = \min_i x_i$, every neighbor $j \in \Gamma(i^*)$ has $1 - x_j \le 1 - x_{i^*}$, so the seat condition forces $1/8 \le x_{i^*} \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i^*)} (1 - x_j) \le x_{i^*}(1 - x_{i^*})^{d(i^*)} \le x_{i^*}(1 - x_{i^*})^3 \le 27/256 < 1/8$, a contradiction. (If a degree-$3$ edge instead sits only on degree-$1$ leaf neighbors, as on $K_{1,3}$, an asymmetric assignment can certify it, e.g. $x_{\text{hub}} = 0.34, x_{\text{leaf}} = 0.19$; the uniform-seat ceiling $D \le 2$ is the exact threshold for regular graphs and for any degree-only test.) The lab's Part 3 runs the census on exactly these three verdicts: symmetric ($D \le 1$), general ($D \le 2$), and the union bound ($m \cdot 2^{1-k} < 1$, i.e. $m \le 7$).

✎ Checkpoint. The census says the certificates die of crowding while the truth stays easy. Before reading on: what does the union bound cap, what does the lemma cap, and which one can grow with the number of vertices?

Generalize — the two ceilings, the chapter's centerpiece. The union bound's ceiling: $m \cdot 2^{1-k} < 1$, so a $k$-uniform hypergraph with fewer than $2^{k-1}$ edges is $2$-colorable — a cap on the total, with no regard for $v$. The lemma's ceiling: $e \cdot 2^{1-k} \cdot (D+1) \le 1$, so each edge may collide with up to $2^{k-1}/e - 1$ others — a cap on local crowding. Now translate: if each vertex sits in at most $\Delta$ edges, an edge's $k$ vertices drag in at most $k(\Delta-1)$ colliding edges, and counting vertex-edge incidences gives $m \le v\Delta/k$. Run $k = 20$: the union bound certifies up to $524{,}288$ edges, ever, on any number of vertices. The lemma certifies when each edge collides with at most $192{,}873$ others — which a hypergraph of max vertex degree $\Delta = 9{,}643$ amply satisfies — and on $v = 10^9$ vertices that allows up to $482{,}150{,}000{,}000$ edges. Nine hundred twenty thousand times the union bound's ceiling — and the ratio doubles with every increment of $k$, because the lemma's ceiling is a degree and the union's is a count. This is the shape lesson of Lecture 1 made quantitative: rarity priced against local crowding lets a system of many events survive when a global ledger would call it hopeless. The lab cannot print a $2^{19}$-edge example; the census instead shows the mechanism honestly at the scale it can verify — the certificates sit silent exactly where crowding is high, which is the same sensitivity, read from the other side.

The $k$-SAT cousin works identically (it is the lab Part 1 experiment): a clause of width $k$ is violated with probability exactly $2^{-k}$, two clauses are entangled iff they share a variable, and if each variable appears in at most $L$ clauses then any clause collides with at most $k(L-1)$ others. The certificate $e \cdot 2^{-k}(k(L-1)+1) \le 1$ holds with $L$ roughly $2^k/(ek)$ — so an instance on $n$ variables may carry up to $\sim n \cdot 2^k/(ek^2)$ clauses and still be certified satisfiable, against the union bound's fixed cap of $2^k - 1$ total clauses. Part 1's $n = 48$ panel is this story at reachable scale: at $m = 8$ and $10$ the union bound is dead (total failure mass $\ge 1$) while the local certificate approves $13$ and $2$ instances per hundred — the slice where the lemma earns its keep, honestly recorded as rare, because at $n = 48$ even sparse overlap is hard to come by.

What to learn: for coloring and satisfiability, the union bound caps the number of bad events and the local lemma caps their entanglement. At small scale both certificates are conservative and the gap between certificate and truth is wide; the lemma's wins are asymptotic, where sparse events can number far beyond any global budget.

Lecture 4 · The Lopsided Lemma: Permutations, and a Graph You Must Earn

Encounter. Random permutations break the beautiful variable model of Lecture 2. Take an $n\times n$ array and ask for a Latin transversal: a choice of one cell in each row and each column holding all $n$ symbols distinct — the permutation $\sigma$ (row $i$ picks column $\sigma(i)$) must avoid every "two chosen cells share a symbol" accident. The natural experiment — a uniformly random permutation — has no independent coordinates to resample: forbid column $3$ for row $1$ and every other row's options shift. The dependency-graph calculus of the variable model is gone; worse, the obvious graph is far too crowded: two accident-events that name a common row or column can interact — but interact how? The discovery of Erdős and Spencer (1991) is that for permutations, conditioning on other events being avoided tends to help you, not hurt you — and a lemma that only charges you for what can genuinely hurt is strictly stronger.

Explain. The lopsided local lemma replaces the mutual-independence hypothesis by a one-sided one. Call $\Gamma$ a lopsidependency graph for events $A_1,\dots,A_n$ if for every $i$ and every set $S$ of non-neighbors of $i$:

$$\Pr[A_i \mid \text{all events of } S \text{ are avoided}] \le p_i.$$

Then the same $x$-certificate $p_i \le x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-x_j)$ implies $\Pr[\text{no } A_i] \ge \prod_i (1 - x_i) > 0$. The proof is identical to Lecture 2's nested induction on $|S|$, with one surgical substitution in the numerator of Case B: when $S$ is partitioned into neighbors $S_1 = S \cap \Gamma(i)$ and non-neighbors $S_2 = S \setminus \Gamma(i)$, we no longer need $A_i$ to be mutually independent of $S_2$; instead, the lopsided hypothesis directly bounds the numerator via $\Pr\left[A_i \wedge \bigwedge_{j\in S_1} \bar{A}_j \mid \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right] \le \Pr\left[A_i \mid \bigwedge_{\ell\in S_2} \bar{A}_\ell\right] \le p_i \le x_i \cdot \prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j)$. The denominator still telescopes over $S_1$ using the induction hypothesis to give at least $\prod_{j\in S_1} (1 - x_j)$, and the ratio still cancels to at most $x_i$. Nothing else in the proof touches independence — which is why a one-sided inequality on non-neighbors does the entire job of mutual independence.

Why "one-sided" is the right idea — the two-event case, completely. Conditioning on $\bar{A}_2$ not hurting is a correlation statement:

$$\Pr[A_1 \mid \bar{A}_2] \le \Pr[A_1] \iff \Pr[A_1]-\Pr[A_1\wedge A_2] \le \Pr[A_1]-\Pr[A_1]\Pr[A_2] \iff \mathbf{\Pr[A_1\wedge A_2] \ge \Pr[A_1]\cdot\Pr[A_2]}.$$

The lopsided hypothesis asks exactly that avoiding a non-neighbor makes your failure no likelier — i.e. that bad events are positively correlated across non-edges of the graph. Positive correlation is the friend; the enemy is negative correlation, where someone else's survival taxes you. (For independent coordinates, both correlations vanish and lopsided degenerates to the ordinary lemma — the variable model is the special case where all the hard work is free.)

Experiment. So measure the correlation. The lab's Part 4 puts two $3$-cell forbidden boards in a $6\times 6$ array — board $1$ on rows $\{0,1,2\}$, board $2$ on rows $\{3,4,5\}$ — and asks the exact question over all $T = 720$ permutations: $a_1 = 426$ permutations miss every cell of board $1$ (so the hit event has probability $294/720 = 0.4083$), $a_2$ likewise $426$, and $ab$ permutations miss both. Two panels:

  • Panel a — disjoint columns (lopsidependency non-neighbors): $ab = 265$, and the hit events' correlation is $265/720 - (426/720)^2 = \mathbf{+0.01799 \ge 0}$. Six random boards, six identical verdicts (boards differ only by relabeling) — avoiding one board genuinely helps avoid the other: the conditioning discount is sound, and the lopsided graph need not draw this edge.
  • Panel b — shared column set (neighbors): $ab = 240$, correlation $\mathbf{-0.01674 < 0}$ — negative, six for six. Here conditioning on the other board's survival makes yours harder, exactly the taxing that the lopsided hypothesis forbids — so the graph must draw the edge, and the certificate must pay for it.

(A quiet exactness note the receipts force on us: $265$ is the derangement number $D_6$ — when both boards forbid a full set of columns the joint count is literally the derangement count, and panel c's $14{,}833$ is $D_8$. The enumeration is exact, not sampled.)

Generalize. With the lopsided graph keyed on shared rows and columns, the Latin transversal theorem lands: if no symbol appears in more than $\sim n/(4e)$ cells of the array, a transversal exists (Erdős–Spencer 1991; we state the theorem at flag level and do not reproduce the proof — the constant is quoted as in the standard exposition). Lu and Székely generalized the machinery from permutations to random injections (cited at flag level, venue not web-verified this session — the Verification Notes record the pin level honestly). The reusable lesson is not the transversal: it is that the graph is a claim, not a given. Every edge you decline to draw is a promise that conditioning across that gap cannot hurt you — a promise the variable model underwrites for free and that permutation models make you measure. Exercise 10 sends you back to the lab numbers to check the bookkeeping yourself.

What to learn: when the underlying experiment is not a product of independent coordinates, ask which pairs of bad events are negatively entangled — those, and only those, belong in the graph. Lopsidedness is the local lemma's way of paying only for genuine danger.

Lecture 5 · The Lemma Learns to Run: Moser–Tardos

Encounter. For thirty-five years the lemma was a pure existence statement — it promised that a good assignment existed with positive probability, but a lottery with a $10^{-100}$ win rate is a strange kind of algorithm. Then Moser and Tardos (2010) found the resampling algorithm hiding inside the proof, and it is almost embarrassingly simple:

While some bad event $A_i$ holds: pick one (the lab picks the lowest index), and resample its variables fresh, independently.

That is the whole algorithm. No restarts, no priority scheme, no memory. If the $x$-certificate of Lecture 2 holds, this loop terminates with probability $1$ and its final assignment avoids every bad event — the lemma, made constructive, with the same hypotheses. And it comes with a quantitative runtime guarantee: Moser and Tardos (2010, Theorem 1.1) prove that $\mathbb{E}[\text{number of resamplings of } A_i] \le x_i/(1 - x_i)$. Whenever $x_i \le 1 - p_i$ (so $1 - x_i \ge p_i$), this is further bounded above by the probability-ratio meter $x_i/p_i$ tracked in the lab's Part 2 code (indeed, at a degree-$1$ seat where $x_1(1 - x_1) = p$, we have $x_1/p = 1/(1 - x_1) = 1 + x_1/(1 - x_1)$, i.e. the expected total number of evaluations including the initial draw; note that neither bound is simply $x_i$ itself).

Explain — the witness-tree proof, complete. Why can the loop not run forever? Let the execution log be $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_t, \dots$ where $C_t \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ is the index of the bad event resampled at step $t$. For the $t$-th step (which resamples $A_{C_t}$), we build a rooted, vertex-labeled witness tree $\tau_t$ as follows: start with a single root vertex labeled $C_t$ at depth $0$; working backwards for $s = t-1, t-2, \dots, 1$, if $C_s$ shares variables with the label of at least one vertex already in $\tau_t$ (so $C_s \in \{\text{label}(v)\} \cup \Gamma(\text{label}(v))$ for some vertex $v \in \tau_t$), attach a new vertex labeled $C_s$ as a child of the deepest such vertex $v$ in $\tau_t$ (breaking ties among deepest vertices by choosing the one with the largest label); if $C_s$ shares no variables with any vertex currently in $\tau_t$, discard $s$ and continue.

Lemma 1 (distinct steps produce distinct witness trees). In any execution log, the witness trees $\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots$ are pairwise non-isomorphic as rooted labeled trees: specifically, if a given proper witness tree $T$ (where sibling vertices always have distinct labels) appears in the log at step $t$, the total number of vertices of $T$ labeled $j$ equals the exact number of times $A_j$ was resampled up to step $t$ along the causal chain, so $T$ can occur at most once in the entire run.
Proof. Suppose $\tau_t = \tau_{t'} = T$ with $t < t'$. Both trees have the same root label $C_t = C_{t'}$, so step $t$ itself would have been encountered when scanning backwards from $t'$ and attached as a child of the root or a deeper compatible descendant (since $C_t$ shares variables with $C_{t'} = C_t$), giving $\tau_{t'}$ strictly more vertices labeled $C_t$ than $\tau_t$ — a contradiction. Thus each tree shape $T$ occurs in the log at most once.

Lemma 2 (coupling a witness tree to fresh independent samples). For any fixed proper witness tree $T$, the probability that $T$ appears as $\tau_t$ for some step $t$ in the Moser–Tardos execution log is at most $w(T) = \prod_{v\in V(T)} p_{\text{label}(v)}$.
Proof. Order the vertices of $T$ in reverse breadth-first order (deepest vertices first). When the algorithm runs, each vertex $v \in V(T)$ corresponds to an event $A_{\text{label}(v)}$ that held at its resampling step. Because children in $\tau_t$ were chosen at the deepest possible level during the backward scan, before the event at $v$ is checked at its step in the log, every variable in $S_{\text{label}(v)}$ is either in its initial random state or was most recently resampled by a child of $v$ in $T$. Consequently, traversing $T$ from deepest level to the root presents each vertex $v$ with a completely fresh, independent sample of the underlying variables in $S_{\text{label}(v)}$. The probability that $A_{\text{label}(v)}$ holds on its fresh sample is exactly $p_{\text{label}(v)}$, and independence across the $|V(T)|$ fresh evaluations gives $\Pr[T \text{ appears in the log}] \le \prod_{v\in V(T)} p_{\text{label}(v)}$.

Lemma 3 (the Galton–Watson branching bound). Define a multi-type Galton–Watson branching process that builds a random tree rooted at $i$: a vertex with label $u$ independently spawns a child with label $w$ for each $w \in \{u\} \cup \Gamma(u)$ with probability $x_w$ (and no child with probability $1 - x_w$). Then summing $w(T)$ over all proper witness trees $T_i$ rooted at $i$ satisfies
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{resamplings of } A_i] \le \sum_{T_i} w(T_i) \le \frac{x_i}{1 - x_i}.$$
Proof. In the branching process, the probability of generating a specific proper tree $T_i$ rooted at $i$ (normalized by the root's birth probability $x_i$) is
$$P(T_i) = \frac{1}{x_i} \prod_{v\in V(T_i)} \left[ x_{\text{label}(v)} (1 - x_{\text{label}(v)}) \prod_{u \in \Gamma(\text{label}(v)) \setminus \text{Children}(v)} (1 - x_u) \right] = \frac{1 - x_i}{x_i} \prod_{v\in V(T_i)} \left[ x_{\text{label}(v)} \prod_{u \in \Gamma(\text{label}(v))} (1 - x_u) \right].$$
(Each vertex $v \in V(T_i)$ fails to spawn a non-occurring child $u \in (\{\text{label}(v)\} \cup \Gamma(\text{label}(v))) \setminus \text{Children}(v)$ with probability $1 - x_u$ and spawns each actual child $u \in \text{Children}(v)$ with probability $x_u$, which supplies the factor $x_{\text{label}(u)}$ at the child node and leaves the $(1 - x_{\text{label}(v)})$ factor at the parent; regrouping across all nodes restores the full product $\prod_{u\in\Gamma(\text{label}(v))}(1 - x_u)$ at every node $v$.) By the Local Lemma certificate, $p_\ell \le x_\ell \prod_{u\in\Gamma(\ell)}(1 - x_u)$ for every label $\ell$. Substituting this inequality at every vertex $v \in V(T_i)$ gives
$$w(T_i) = \prod_{v\in V(T_i)} p_{\text{label}(v)} \le \frac{x_i}{1 - x_i} P(T_i).$$
Summing over all proper trees $T_i$ rooted at $i$, since the events $\{\text{the branching process produces } T_i\}$ are mutually exclusive across distinct trees, we have $\sum_{T_i} P(T_i) \le 1$. Therefore $\mathbb{E}[\text{resamplings of } A_i] = \sum_{T_i} \Pr[T_i \text{ appears in log}] \le \sum_{T_i} w(T_i) \le \frac{x_i}{1 - x_i}$.

Experiment. Four receipts panels, four honest readings.

Certified vs uncertified (Part 2). On $40$ certified instances ($n = 20, m = 5$, rejection until $D \le 1$; $554$ tries for $40$ instances), MT terminated on $40$ of $40$ with mean $0.65$ resampling events, max $4$. The theory meters: with $x_1 = (1 - \sqrt{1/2})/2 \approx 0.146447$ for a degree-$1$ clause and $x_0 = 1/8$ for an isolated one, the exact Moser–Tardos Theorem 1.1 bound $\sum_i x_i/(1 - x_i)$ uses $x_1/(1 - x_1) = 3 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.17157$ and $x_0/(1 - x_0) = 1/7 \approx 0.14286$ (averaging $0.8134$ across the $40$ instances), while the lab code's coarser probability-ratio meter $\sum_i x_i/p_i$ uses $x_1/p = 1/(1 - x_1) = 1.17157$ and $x_0/p = 1$ and averages $5.592$ — the sample mean $0.65$ sits below both, and the reason is the meter's fine print: the bound is worst-case over all distributions sharing the dependency graph, while the lab samples the easiest one, the honest product measure. (The certificate's other output, the survival floor $\prod(1-x_i)$, averaged $0.4709$.) The control panel ran $30$ brute-verified satisfiable instances with entanglement degree $\ge 5$ — uncertifiable by any form of the lemma — and MT still terminated on $30$ of $30$, at mean $2.23$, max $10$: about $3.4\times$ the resamplings of the certified set. Sufficient, not necessary — the algorithm patrols well beyond the certificate's fence, exactly as Part 1's census hinted it might.

Beyond Shearer's wall (Part 5B). On the line process (events $A_i = \text{"}b_i \text{ and } b_{i+1} \text{ both fire"}$, $q = \Pr[b = 1]$ ranging $2/5$ to $3/5$, well past Shearer's exact boundary $q = 1/2$), MT ran with a $50{,}000$-round cap and never touched it: medians $2$–$6$ at $m = 16$, $5$–$14$ at $m = 32$, roughly doubling with $m$, no caps in $700$ trials. Kolipaka and Szegedy's efficiency theorem (cited at flag level) says MT can be forced to be slow right up to Shearer's boundary — worst-case over configurations sharing the dependency graph. This process is not that configuration; the receipts say so, and the chapter says so with them.

The star (Part 6). The nine-clause star that only the asymmetric form could certify: $20$ trials, $20$ terminations, mean $1.10$, max $4$. Recall $35.64\%$ of all assignments already satisfy the instance — MT's first round is free more often than not, and its expected total effort is barely above one resampling. The certificate's promise and the algorithm's reality agree comfortably.

Generalize — the counting parent. Moser's original termination argument (with Tardos's simplification) is known as entropy compression: if the loop ran too long, its resampling log would be an incompressible string of too much description cost — counting by description length, P29's counting idea, turned into an algorithmic stop sign. The Atlas's pattern survey recorded honestly that entropy compression never fires standalone in the survey corpus — it lives here, as the engine inside P45's constructive face, with P29 as its counting parent. Its grown-up descendant is field practice: the same description-length discipline underlies the modern sharpenings of Johansson's coloring bound (Lecture 6).

What to learn: the lemma and the algorithm are one object — the $x$-certificate is simultaneously an existence proof, a termination proof, and a runtime meter. Trust the meter as a worst-case ceiling, not an estimate; and remember the algorithm is more generous than the theorem that licenses it.

Lecture 6 · The Field, the Boundary, and the Practice Menu

Encounter. Where does the lemma carry real weight — not as a textbook cameo but as the spine of a theorem people use? The emblem is a coloring question of a different flavor: the list chromatic number. Give every vertex of a graph its own list of available colors and ask for a proper coloring from lists. For triangle-free graphs of maximum degree $\Delta$, the answer is about $\Delta/\ln \Delta$ — dramatically better than the $\Delta+1$ a greedy argument needs — because triangle-freeness makes each vertex's neighborhood locally sparse, and locally sparse + globally many is precisely the local lemma's regime. Johansson proved the bound around 1996; the honest publishing story is an odd one the Atlas records as it stands: the proof lives in a DIMACS technical report (91-5) that was never journal-published, and the LLL step it is famous for survives through the Molloy–Reed book's exposition. The result was sharpened to the exact leading constant by Molloy (2019, J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 134:264–284, arXiv:1701.09133): every triangle-free graph of maximum degree $\Delta$ has list chromatic number at most $(1+o(1))\Delta/\ln \Delta$ — a modern landmark whose load-bearing beam is a carefully tuned asymmetric application of the lemma (the general form, pricing heterogeneous neighborhoods — Lecture 2's bargaining table doing real work, not decoration). We cite the applications at flag level; the chapter has proven and verified what sits below them.

The boundary. How far can any such lemma go? Shearer (1985) found the exact frontier: for uniform $p$ on a dependency graph $G$, the best possible certificate criterion is the positivity of the independence polynomial $\mu(G, p) = \sum_k (-1)^k \cdot I_k(G) \cdot p^k$ ($I_k = \text{number of independent } k\text{-sets}$) on $G$ and every induced subgraph. Neither the symmetric nor the general form reaches it — on the path $P_m$ the three ceilings stack like a ladder, and the lab's Part 5A measures every rung:

  • symmetric LLL: $p \le \mathbf{1/(3e) = 0.12261}$ (the loose $e$-shape; $q \le 0.3502$)
  • uniform-$x$ general form: $p \le \mathbf{4/27 = 0.14815}$ (the exact uniform exchange rate; $q \le 0.3849$)
  • Shearer: $p \le \mathbf{1/4 = 0.25}$ (exact for the path; $q \le 1/2$ — and at exactly $p = 1/4$ the finite paths stay marginally inside: $\mu(P_m, 1/4) = (m+2)/2^{m+1} > 0$, the identity of Exercise 7, machine-checked at $m = 8, 16, 32$: $10/512 = 0.01953$, $18/131072 = 0.000137$, $34/2^{33} = 3.96\cdot 10^{-9}$).

And the floors table turns the ladder into a quantitative lesson: at $q = 1/3$, $m = 32$, the three certified floors for the survival probability are $0.0044$ (LLL floor $(1-x)^m$), $0.01308$ (Shearer floor $\mu$), against an exact truth of $0.04915$ — every floor below the truth, every floor above the last — a certificate is not a verdict, it is a guarantee with a number on it, and different certificates give wildly different numbers for the same process. At $q \ge 2/5$ the LLL floors cease to exist (no certificate) while Shearer's keeps working to the wall — and Part 5B's receipts show the algorithm strolling even past the wall, with the worst-case caveat recorded honestly.

The practice menu — P13 and P45, one diagnosis. The union bound is the local lemma's densest special case, and the two are incomparable, not ranked — the lab made you watch the union bound win Part 1's small panels. So the last skill is triage:

  1. Sum the failure probabilities. $\sum p_i < 1$? Take the union bound and go home — it is cheaper, and at small scale it certifies more (Part 1a: every $m \le 7$ instance).
  2. Count entanglement. Many events, sparse overlap: symmetric check $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$. Crowded: it fails — retune before abandoning.
  3. Retune the exchange rates (the general form): solve $x_i \leftarrow p_i/\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-x_j)$ from zero; the star passed only here. Convergence with all $x_i < 1$ is the certificate — and for uniform $p$ on regular graphs it is provably equivalent to the degree calculus (Lecture 3 proved the $k = 4$ case outright).
  4. Not a product space? Ask which pairs are negatively correlated — the lopsided graph pays only for those (Lecture 4).
  5. Need the object, not just its existence? Run MT — the same certificate is the termination proof, and the algorithm works well beyond it (Part 2's control, Part 5B's line).
  6. Near Shearer's wall? The bound is tight somewhere with that dependency graph — if your instance is close to the boundary, no certificate shape will save you; look for structure (lopsidedness, a better graph) or accept the truth gap.

Where this chapter came from. The Atlas's pattern survey recorded the lemma's in-corpus usage honestly: the load-bearing firings sit in a single survey batch, and the celebrated Johansson application is exposition-level — the survey could not promote the lemma on that corpus evidence alone, and left a decision open: a pattern of its own, or a strengthening of P13? The Atlas resolved the question by structure, not by census: the union bound (P13) is a one-line global ledger, while this chapter's object — rarity priced against local dependence, with its own certificate, its own boundary theorem, and its own algorithm — is a different kind of mathematics, not a scaled-up version of the same trick. The lemma therefore takes its own entry (P45) with the union bound as its parent and densest special case; the survey's open question is closed, and the survey's honest negatives (entropy compression's zero standalone firings, recorded in Lecture 5) are taught where they live. A chapter earns its place the same way a certificate does: by what it lets you verify, not by how often it was cited.

One continuous stream — stream A, seed $20261083$ — carries Parts 1a $\to$ 2a $\to$ 2b $\to$ 3a in exactly that order; fresh engines start at seeds $20261084$ (Part 1b), $20261085$ (Part 3b), $20261086$ (Part 4), $20261087$ (Part 5B), $20261088$ (Part 6's resampling panel). All draws are integer-exact: an index in range $N$ is nxt() % N; a fair bit is nxt() % 2; a biased bit with $P(1) = k/d$ happens iff nxt()*d < k*M31; a clause's $k$ distinct variables come from a partial Fisher–Yates (one draw per index, $i$ from $n-1$ down to $n-k$), and its $k$ sign bits are drawn AFTER the Fisher–Yates; hypergraph edges draw no sign bits; the Part 4 board permutations are full Fisher–Yates (one draw per index, $i$ from $n-1$ down to $0$). The printed Python reproduces every receipt value-for-value; the resampling panels pick the lowest-index violated event and resample its variables in listed order. Part 1 — The $k$-SAT dependency censuses (Lectures 1–2)

The census asks Lecture 1's question with a machine: how often does the symmetric certificate (here $D \le 1$, since $e \cdot (1/8) \cdot (D+1) \le 1$ exactly at $D = 1$) approve a random instance, and does it ever lie? Every instance is brute-checked against all $4{,}096$ assignments. The $n = 48$ panel is the lemma's payoff slice: from $m = 8$ the union bound's ledger $\sum p = m/8$ is $\ge 1$ and dies, while the local certificate — blind to $m$, pricing only the degree — still fires.

M31 = 2147483647
def engine(seed):
    x = [seed]
    def nxt():
        x[0] = 48271 * x[0] % M31
        return x[0]
    return nxt

def draw_clause(nxt, n, k, signs=True):
    if k <= 0 or k > n:
        return []
    arr = list(range(n))
    for i in range(n-1, n-k-1, -1):        # partial Fisher-Yates, one draw per index
        j = nxt() % (i+1)
        arr[i], arr[j] = arr[j], arr[i]
    vs = arr[n-k:]
    if not signs:                          # hypergraph edge: no sign draws
        return [(v, 1) for v in vs]
    return [(v, nxt() % 2) for v in vs]    # k sign bits, AFTER the FY draws

def degrees(clauses):                     # D_i = # other clauses sharing a variable
    m = len(clauses)
    if m == 0:
        return [0]
    return [sum(1 for e in range(m)
                if e != c and any(l1[0] == l2[0]
                                  for l1 in clauses[c] for l2 in clauses[e]))
            for c in range(m)]

def violated(clauses, bits):               # lowest-index clause, all literals false
    for c, cl in enumerate(clauses):
        if all(bits[v] != s for v, s in cl):
            return c
    return -1

def brute_sat(clauses, n):                 # some assignment satisfies every clause
    for mask in range(1 << n):
        if all(any((mask >> v) & 1 == s for v, s in cl) for cl in clauses):
            return True
    return False

def mt(nxt, clauses, n, cap=5000):        # resampling; rounds = resampled events
    bits = [nxt() % 2 for _ in range(n)]   # init: n fair bits in variable order
    r = 0
    while r < cap:
        c = violated(clauses, bits)
        if c < 0:
            return r
        for v, _ in clauses[c]:            # resample listed order, fair bits
            bits[v] = nxt() % 2
        r += 1
    return r

def census_1a(nxt):                        # n=12, k=3, m=3..10, 100 instances each
    for m in range(3, 11):
        sD = cert = sat = cbad = 0
        for _ in range(100):
            cl = [draw_clause(nxt, 12, 3) for _ in range(m)]
            Dmax = max(degrees(cl))
            ok = brute_sat(cl, 12)
            sD += Dmax; sat += ok
            if Dmax <= 1:                  # the sound symmetric certificate
                cert += 1
                if not ok: cbad += 1
        print(m, sD/100, cert, sat, cbad)

def census_1b():                           # n=48, all-positive, fresh engine
    nxt = engine(20261084)
    for m in (8, 10, 12):
        sD = cert = 0
        for _ in range(100):

            Dmax = max(degrees(cl))
            sD += Dmax
            if Dmax <= 1: cert += 1
        print(m, sD/100, cert, m <= 7)     # union certifies iff m <= 7

Part 2 — Moser–Tardos: certified vs. entangled (Lecture 5)

Two panels on the continuing stream. Panel (a) rejects until the instance is certified ($D \le 1$) and runs the resampling algorithm; panel (b) rejects until the instance is satisfiable but uncertified ($\text{brute-SAT} \wedge D \ge 5$) — deliberately entangled — and runs the same algorithm. The theorem promises nothing for panel (b)'s instances; the machine reports what actually happens. The tight Moser–Tardos Theorem 1.1 bound is $\mathbb{E}[\text{resamplings of } A_i] \le x_i/(1 - x_i)$, bounded above here by the probability-ratio meter $x_i/p_i$ (so $\mathbb{E}[\text{total}] \le \sum x_i/(1 - x_i) \le \sum x_i/p_i$) with $x_i$ the small root of $x(1-x)^{D_i} = p$.

def panel_2a(nxt):                        # n=20, m=5, reject until D <= 1
    x1 = (1 - 0.5 ** 0.5) / 2             # root of x(1-x) = 1/8   (D = 1)
    x0 = 0.125                            # root of x = 1/8        (D = 0)
    rounds, bounds, tight, floors, tries = [], [], [], [], 0
    while len(rounds) < 40:
        tries += 1
        cl = [draw_clause(nxt, 20, 3) for _ in range(5)]
        D = degrees(cl)
        if max(D) > 1:
            continue
        rounds.append(mt(nxt, cl, 20))
        bnd, tbnd, flr = 0.0, 0.0, 1.0
        for d in D:
            x = x0 if d == 0 else x1
            bnd += x / 0.125              # coarse ratio meter: x_i/p_i
            tbnd += x / (1 - x)           # MT Theorem 1.1 bound: x_i/(1-x_i)
            flr *= (1 - x)                # general-form survival floor
        bounds.append(bnd); tight.append(tbnd); floors.append(flr)
    print('tries', tries)
    print('rounds', rounds)
    print('mean', sum(rounds)/40, 'max', max(rounds))
    print('mean bound', sum(bounds)/40, 'mean tight', sum(tight)/40, 'mean floor', sum(floors)/40)

Part 3 — Hypergraph Property B, two censuses (Lecture 3)

The same census in the coloring world: $k = 4$ edges on $v$ vertices, a random $2$-coloring makes each edge monochromatic with $p = 2^{1-k} = 1/8$ exactly. The sound uniform-seat certificates: symmetric $D \le 1$, uniform-general $D \le 2$ — and $D \le 2$ is also the exact asymmetric criterion on any regular (or minimum-degree $D$) dependency graph, since at the minimum seat $x_{\min}$ one has $x_{\min}(1-x_{\min})^3 \le 27/256 < 1/8$. The brute check is two-sided (an edge is monochromatic in either color).

def brute_col(edges, v):                  # Property B: some 2-coloring, no mono edge
    for mask in range(1 << v):
        if all(len({(mask >> w) & 1 for w, _ in e}) == 2 for e in edges):
            return True
    return False

Part 4 — Lopsidedness: measuring the neighbor's tax (Lecture 4)

Two $3$-cell boards inside the $6\times 6$ grid; a permutation $\sigma$ ($\text{rows} \to \text{columns}$) hits a board if it touches any of its cells; the bad events are the hits. The lopsided hypothesis asks: does conditioning on a declined edge's survival lower the failure chance? The machine counts misses over all $720$ permutations exactly. Boards are drawn by full Fisher–Yates; the counts are isomorphism-invariant, so every sample prints the same numbers — that invariance is itself the derangement cameo below.

def full_fy(nxt, cols):                    # full Fisher-Yates, one draw per index
    p = list(cols)
    for i in range(len(p)-1, -1, -1):
        j = nxt() % (i+1)
        p[i], p[j] = p[j], p[i]
    return p

def board(nxt, rows, cols):                # one forbidden cell per row
    p = full_fy(nxt, cols)
    return [(r, p[i]) for i, r in enumerate(rows)]

def miss_counts(n, B1, B2):                # over ALL n! permutations: rows -> cols
    T = [0, 0, 0, 0]                       # total, miss1, miss2, miss both
    used, cur = [False]*n, [0]*n
    def rec(r):
        if r == n:
            T[0] += 1
            m1 = all(cur[r] != c for r, c in B1)
            m2 = all(cur[r] != c for r, c in B2)
            T[1] += m1; T[2] += m2; T[3] += (m1 and m2)
            return
        for c in range(n):
            if not used[c]:
                used[c] = True; cur[r] = c
                rec(r+1)
                used[c] = False
    rec(0)
    return T[1], T[2], T[3], T[0]

Part 5 — The three floors, and the algorithm at the fence (Lectures 2, 5–6)

5A — deterministic. The path $P_m$ with $m$ events $A_i = (b_i \wedge b_{i+1})$ on $m+1$ independent bits, each $1$ w.p. $q$, $p = q^2$. Three floors on the survival probability: the certificate floor $(1-x)^m$ (uniform seat, the small root of $x(1-x)^2 = p$), the Shearer floor $\mu(P_m, p)$, and the exact truth — a two-term DP over the $m+1$ bits. No draws anywhere.

import math

def she_mu(m, p):                          # mu(P_m,p) = a_{m+1}, a_n = a_{n-1} - p*a_{n-2}
    a0 = a1 = 1.0
    for _ in range(m):
        a0, a1 = a1, a1 - p * a0
    return a1

    return t1

Worked answers for the twelve graded problems and the three ◆ pattern-unknown ones. The three ◐ Explorations state what you should find and get no worked answer — that is their point. Answer 1 (the one-event audit). (a) Union: $\sum p = 0.9 < 1$, so $P(\bar{A}_1) \ge 1 - 0.9 = 0.1 > 0$ — and in fact exactly $0.1$. (b) Symmetric with $d = 0$: $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) = e \cdot 0.9 \approx 2.446 > 1$ — the form demands $p \le 1/e \approx 0.368$ and refuses. (c) General: $\Gamma(1)$ is empty, so the seat condition is just $p_1 \le x_1$ — any $x_1 \ge 0.9$ works. The floor $1 - x_1$ is best (largest) at the smallest seat $x_1 = 0.9$: floor $0.1$. (d) The truth is $\Pr[\bar{A}_1] = 0.1$: for a single event the general form is exact — its floor equals the survival probability. The symmetric form's $1/e$ is pure loss here, the first glimpse that the forms are incomparable in both directions: Part 1a's union wins ($m \le 7$) and Part 1b's lemma wins ($m \ge 8$) are the same lesson at census scale.

Answer 2 (a $10$-SAT certificate). (a) A width-$10$ clause is violated by exactly one of the $2^{10}$ literal patterns: $p = 2^{-10} = 1/1024$. (b) Each of the clause's $10$ variables sits in at most $L - 1 = 3$ other clauses, so at most $30$ other clauses share a variable with it: $D \le 30$. (c) $e \cdot (1/1024) \cdot 31 = 0.0823 \le 1$ — every such instance is satisfiable. What the certificate never asked for: the number of clauses. Counting incidences bounds it ($10m \le 4n$, i.e. $m \le 2n/5$), but the proof runs without it — degree, not total, is the lemma's currency (Lecture 3's contrast in one line).

Answer 3 (the optimal symmetric bound, completely). (a) $f'(x) = (1-x)^d - dx(1-x)^{d-1} = (1-x)^{d-1}(1 - (d+1)x)$, zero at $x = 1/(d+1)$; $f(1/(d+1)) = (1/(d+1))\cdot(d/(d+1))^d = d^d/(d+1)^{d+1}$. (b) Since $(1+1/d)^d < e$, we get $d^d/(d+1)^{d+1} > 1/(e(d+1))$; replacing the exact ceiling by the loose one gives $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$. At $d = 2$: exact $4/27 = 0.1481$ against loose $1/(3e) = 0.1226$ — a $21\%$ window surrendered ($0.1481/0.1226 = 1.208$) to a one-line shortcut. (c) The $1/e$ enters Lecture 2's derivation exactly at the step $(1 - 1/(d+1))^d \ge 1/e$ — a lower bound on $(d/(d+1))^d$, tight only as $d \to \infty$. The exact form never takes that step: it keeps the maximized value as is. The same computation returns in Exercise 9 and in the Part 5A ladder ($q \le 0.3849$ vs. $q \le 0.3502$).

Answer 6 (counting the witness trees). (a) One event, $p = 1/8$: every resampling is a fresh independent trial, so the count is geometric — $E = p + p^2 + \dots = p/(1-p) = 1/7 = 0.1429$, exact. (b) Two clauses sharing one variable: because $\{A, B\}$ is a single shared-variable clique, every earlier step in the backward scan is compatible with the deepest leaf, making every witness tree a unary chain; in the neighbor-alternating witness family rooted at clause $1$, the chains are $A, AB, ABA, ABAB, \dots$, one per size $k$, each carrying weight $p^k$. The sum: $\sum_{k\ge 1} p^k = 0.1429$, an upper bound on $\mathbb{E}[\text{resamplings of } A]$ by the witness-tree lemma. (c) The lab's coarse meter at the same seat is $x/p = 0.146447/0.125 = 1.1716$ (and the tight Theorem 1.1 bound is $x/(1-x) = 3 - 2\sqrt{2} = 0.1716$) — the chain sum is $8.2\times$ tighter than $x/p$ on this instance ($1.1716/0.1429 \approx 8.2$). (d) Part 2a's $40$ five-clause instances averaged $0.65$ total resamplings $\approx 0.13$ per clause — comfortably inside $0.1429$ per clause, and far inside the coarse meter's $1.17$. The tree sum still overestimates even for the easiest process: it prices every consistent genealogy at $p$ per node, while a single run realizes at most one genealogy — the bound totals the whole forest of possible histories, the run walks one path.

Answer 7 (the Shearer identity on the path). Base: $\mu(P_0, 1/4) = 1 = 2/2^1$ and $\mu(P_1, 1/4) = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4 = 3/2^2$. Step: with $\mu(P_{m-1}) = (m+1)/2^m$ and $\mu(P_{m-2}) = m/2^{m-1}$, the recursion gives $\mu(P_m) = (m+1)/2^m - (1/4)\cdot m/2^{m-1} = (2(m+1) - m)/2^{m+1} = \mathbf{(m+2)/2^{m+1}}$. Checks against the lab (Part 5A prints all three): $m = 8$: $10/512 = 0.01953$; $m = 16$: $18/131072 = 0.000137$; $m = 32$: $34/2^{33} = 3.96\cdot 10^{-9}$ — positive for every finite $m$, so the finite paths sit marginally inside Shearer's region at exactly $p = 1/4$, and the wall is the $m \to \infty$ limit. (This is P4's cameo: a two-line induction on a recursion — the monovariant habit, applied to a boundary.)

Answer 8 (reading the meter). (a) By linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[\text{total}] = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[\text{res}_i] \le \sum_i x_i/(1-x_i)$, and whenever $1 - x_i \ge p_i$ (i.e. $x_i \le 1 - p_i$) each term is bounded above by $x_i/p_i$.
