Chapter 2 · Graph Theory Core

A lecture course in the spirit of Chapter 1: the idea before the formula, the story before the proof, and the proof as a conversation. Every method of the reference canon is preserved inside the narrative; the problem families run from handshaking to the subject's crown jewels — Dirac's theorem, its degree-sequence refinements (Pósa and Chvátal), and the Gallai–Milgram path-cover theorem — and the computational lab verifies Dirac, Pósa, Chvátal's closure lemma, and Gallai–Milgram by exhaustion.

How to read this chapter

Read it slowly, with a pencil. Chapter 1 taught you to count collections; this chapter is about structure — what the wiring of things forces, regardless of what the things are. A graph is just dots and lines: cities and roads, people and friendships, letters and envelopes' addresses. Because the dots have no meaning, whatever we prove is true of every wiring in the world — that is the whole secret of the subject's power.

Notation. $G = (V, E)$, $n = |V|$ vertices, $m = |E|$ edges, $d(v) =$ degree of $v$, $\delta$ and $\Delta =$ min and max degree, $N(v) =$ neighbors of $v$. Graphs are finite, undirected, and simple unless a lecture says otherwise — multigraphs enter with Königsberg.

The exercises are graded: ●○○ easy (a direct application of one lecture idea), ●●○ moderate (two ideas, or one tricky step), ●●● tough (several chapter ideas combined). Hints travel with the problems; worked answers sit at the very end of the chapter. Try honestly before reading a hint — the struggle is the part of learning that cannot be outsourced. Beyond the graded set, ◆ pattern-unknown problems name no method and carry no hint: diagnosing which tool fits is part of the problem. And ◐ Explorations at the end are open-ended investigations with no single right answer — research training, not test preparation.

Lecture 1 · What Degrees Force

Here is a puzzle that looks like a parlor trick: at any party, an even number of people have shaken hands an odd number of times. Prove it — and, more importantly, find the machine behind it.

1.1 Count the ends of the handshakes

Don't track people; track ends of handshakes. A handshake has two ends — that sounds too obvious to be useful, and it is the whole proof. Make a table: rows are the $n$ people, columns are the $m$ handshakes, and put a $\checkmark$ wherever a person participated in a handshake. Now count the check marks two ways.

By rows: person $v$ has one $\checkmark$ per handshake they were in — that is exactly $d(v)$, the number of hands $v$ shook. Row-total: $\sum_{v} d(v)$.
By columns: every handshake column has exactly two $\checkmark$'s. Column-total: $2m$.

One table, two totals — and the totals must agree, because they count the same marks:

$$\sum_{v \in V} d(v) = 2m \quad \text{— the Handshaking Lemma. (Pattern } \mathbf{P2}\text{: count one world two ways.)}$$

Check it on something small before trusting it. Take the triangle $\{A,B,C\}$ with a pendant $D$ hanging off $C$: degrees are $2, 2, 3, 1$; the sum is $8$; and indeed $m = 4$, so $2m = 8$. $\checkmark$

1.2 Parity falls out for free

Now the party puzzle. $\sum d(v) = 2m$ is even. Split the sum into even and odd summands: the even ones contribute an even amount, so the odd ones must too — and a sum of odd numbers is even exactly when there is an even number of them. Done: an even number of people are odd handshakers. (Pattern P7.)

Notice the strange power of this argument: we could not tell you which people are odd, or how many — only that the count is even. Parity arguments are the bargain basement of mathematics: almost no information in, one bit of unbreakable truth out. For instance, no graph can have exactly one odd-degree vertex — not because of anything about graphs, but because of arithmetic.

1.3 Build the graph one edge at a time (a second engine)

Here is the same theorem, explained by history instead of structure. Start from no edges ($\sum d = 0$ $\checkmark$) and add edges one at a time. Each new edge raises two degrees by one each: the sum grows by $2$, so it stays twice the edge count — the identity again. And look at the odd vertices: a new edge flips the parity of exactly two of them, so the number of odd vertices changes by $-2$, $0$, or $+2$ — it stays even forever. The parity fact again, from the construction this time. (Pattern P4.)

Two explanations of one fact, one static (the table) and one dynamic (the build) — when something is true, hunt both. Each explanation generalizes in a different direction, and you will meet both again: the table becomes double counting, the build becomes induction.

1.4 Two people with the same degree (the pigeonhole with one box removed)

One more degree fact, and it hides a lesson about rescuing failed proofs. Claim: in any graph on $n \ge 2$ vertices, two vertices have the same degree. (In any group of $n$ people, two have the same number of friends in the group.)

Try pigeonhole naively: degrees live in $\{0, 1, \dots, n - 1\}$ — that is $n$ boxes for $n$ vertices. Not enough! Pigeonhole needs more objects than boxes, and we are exactly one short. Most people quit here. Instead, inspect the failure: the proof would work if one box were unavailable. Is it? Boxes $0$ (an isolated vertex) and $n - 1$ (a vertex adjacent to everyone) cannot both be occupied — a universal vertex shakes everyone's hand, so nobody is isolated. So the $n$ vertices really live in at most $n - 1$ usable boxes, and two share one. $\blacksquare$ (Pattern P9, refined form.)

The lesson, stated so you'll reuse it: when a pigeonhole count fails by exactly one, look for two categories that cannot be simultaneously occupied. A proof that fails by a hair is not a failure — it is a map, with an X marking the extra fact you need.

What you now own: the ends-table (P2/P7), the static-and-dynamic double explanation, the missing-box pigeonhole.

Lecture 2 · The Walk That Cannot End

Now the first structural theorem. Suppose every vertex of a graph has degree at least $2$ — every room in the house has at least two doors. Must the graph contain a cycle?

Feel it as a story first. You walk through the house by the rule: never turn around — enter a room by one door, leave by another. Since every room has two doors, you can always leave. So the walk never gets stuck; but the house is finite; so the only possible ending is that you walk into a room you have seen before. And the moment that happens, you have found a closed loop: a cycle. Two ways to make this honest:

2.1 Corner the endpoint (the longest-path method)

Take a longest path in the graph, $P = v_0 v_1 \dots v_k$. "Longest" is not a description — it is a weapon. Look at the endpoint $v_0$: every neighbor of $v_0$ lies on $P$, because a neighbor outside $P$ would let us extend the path, contradicting longest-ness. The endpoint is cornered. But $d(v_0) \ge 2$, so $v_0$ has some neighbor $v_j$ with $j \ge 2$ (its other neighbor is $v_1$, the path itself). Then $v_0 v_1 \dots v_j v_0$ is a cycle. $\blacksquare$ (Pattern P8 — the extremal object is so rigid that its border has nowhere to hide.)

The same moment, drawn — the cornered endpoint, its trapped neighbors, and the chord that closes the cycle:

2.2 Just walk (the first-repeat method)

Or run the story literally. Start anywhere and walk: at each step from $v_i$, step to any neighbor other than $v_{i-1}$ — always possible, since every vertex has $\ge 2$ neighbors. The walk is infinite, the vertex set is finite, so some vertex repeats. Take the first repeat: $v_j = v_i$ with $i < j$ and $v_0, \dots, v_{j-1}$ all distinct. Then $v_i v_{i+1} \dots v_j$ is a closed walk with no repeated vertices inside — a cycle. $\blacksquare$ (Pattern P18: the procedure is the proof, with a pigeonhole hiding inside.)

Why you should care about the method and not just the fact. "Take a longest path; the endpoint is trapped" is one of the top three moves in all of graph theory. Watch it return: it finds leaves in trees (Lecture 3), it underlies Prüfer's analysis (Lecture 4), and it proves Dirac's theorem (Lecture 6). When you need to prove something exists in every graph, your first reflex should be: grab the most extreme object available and interrogate its boundary.

What you now own: cornered endpoints (P8); first-repeat walks (P18+P9); the extremal reflex.

Lecture 3 · Trees: Just Enough Roads

A new country, $n$ cities, and a minister of roads who must connect every city to every other — directly or through intermediates — while building not one unnecessary road. The result of her economy is called a tree: a graph that is connected (every city reaches every other) and acyclic (no closed loops of roads). The question this lecture answers: how many roads does such a country have, and why is that number inevitable? The answer, $n - 1$, is almost boring. The reason is a set of ideas you will use for the rest of the book.

3.1 Leaves are forced (the extremal reflex again)

Every tree on $n \ge 2$ vertices has at least two leaves (vertices of degree $1$). To see why, take a longest path in the tree, $P = v_0 v_1 \dots v_k$. Since $n \ge 2$ and the tree is connected, $P$ has at least one edge, so $k \ge 1$ and the endpoints $v_0$ and $v_k$ are distinct. Claim: $d(v_0) = 1$ (and by the same argument at the other end, $d(v_k) = 1$). Suppose $v_0$ had a second neighbor $u \ne v_1$. Where could $u$ sit? It cannot lie on $P$ (say $u = v_j$ with $j \ge 2$), because then $v_0 v_1 \dots v_j v_0$ would close a cycle, forbidden in a tree. And it cannot lie off $P$, because then $u v_0 v_1 \dots v_k$ would be a strictly longer path, forbidden by the choice of $P$. Both routes are blocked, so $v_1$ is the only neighbor of $v_0$, and $v_0$ is a leaf. $\blacksquare$ (Pattern P8, second appearance. Notice how the three hypotheses work in concert: connectedness with $n \ge 2$ guarantees $k \ge 1$, acyclicity rules out a neighbor on $P$, and extremality rules out a neighbor off $P$.)

3.2 The three-way door

For a graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, these are equivalent — and each equivalence is a tool you'll hold for life:

(a) $G$ is connected and acyclic. (b) $G$ is connected with exactly $n - 1$ edges. (c) Every two vertices of $G$ are joined by a unique path.

(a) $\Rightarrow$ (b): peel the leaf. Induct on $n$. A tree with $n \ge 2$ has a leaf $v$ (§3.1). Delete $v$ and its edge: what's left is still connected ($v$ was a dead end, no through-traffic used it) and still acyclic — a tree on $n - 1$ vertices, which by induction has $n - 2$ edges. Put $v$ back with its one edge: $n - 1$ edges. $\blacksquare$ (P4: leaf-peeling — the tree's own structure powers the induction.)

(b) $\Rightarrow$ (a): count the surplus. First a sub-fact, worth having on its own: every connected graph has at least $n - 1$ edges. Proof: from any connected $G$, delete an edge lying on a cycle, if one exists; connectivity is untouched (the edge's endpoints remain connected the long way around). Repeat until no cycles remain: you reach a connected acyclic subgraph — a spanning tree — which by (a)$\Rightarrow$(b) has exactly $n - 1$ edges. Since we only deleted, $G$ itself had $\ge n - 1$ edges. Now suppose $G$ is connected with $n - 1$ edges and has a cycle: deleting any cycle edge leaves it connected with $n - 2$ edges — below the floor just established. Contradiction. $\blacksquare$

(a) $\Rightarrow$ (c): two paths would weld into a cycle. Suppose vertices $u \ne v$ are joined by two distinct simple paths $P = (u = p_0, p_1, \dots, p_r = v)$ and $Q = (u = q_0, q_1, \dots, q_s = v)$. Let $i$ be the first index at which they diverge (so $p_0 = q_0, \dots, p_i = q_i$, but $p_{i+1} \ne q_{i+1}$), and set $x = p_i = q_i$. Since $P$ and $Q$ both end at $v$, the suffix of $P$ after $x$ meets $Q$ again; let $y = p_j$ ($j > i$) be the first vertex on $P$ after $x$ that lies on $Q$. Because $Q$ is a simple path that agreed with $P$ on $\{p_0, \dots, p_i\}$, $y$ must appear on $Q$ strictly after $x$, say $y = q_k$ with $k > i$. By the minimality of $j$, no internal vertex of the subpath $p_i p_{i+1} \dots p_j$ lies on $Q$, so the subpath of $P$ from $x$ to $y$ and the subpath of $Q$ from $x$ to $y$ share only their endpoints $x$ and $y$. Moreover, since $p_{i+1} \ne q_{i+1}$, the two subpaths are not both the single edge $xy$, so their union is a simple cycle of length at least $3$ — impossible in a tree. $\blacksquare$

(c) $\Rightarrow$ (a): unique paths mean connected and no cycles. Unique paths between every pair includes existence, which is connectedness. And if a cycle existed, any two adjacent vertices $x, y$ on it would have two $x\text{–}y$ routes: the edge, and the rest of the cycle. So acyclic. $\blacksquare$

3.3 The consequences you should know cold

  • A forest (acyclic, possibly disconnected) on $n$ vertices with $k$ components has exactly $n - k$ edges — peel a leaf from any component repeatedly (each peel removes one vertex and one edge, components unchanged) until every component is a single vertex.
  • In a tree, every edge is a bridge (delete it and the tree splits into two parts — Lecture 4 uses this constantly) and every non-edge, when added, creates exactly one cycle (the two endpoints have a unique path already).
  • Spanning trees exist in every connected graph (the deletion procedure of (b)$\Rightarrow$(a) constructs one — pattern P18: the proof is the procedure).

What you now own: leaf-peeling induction (P4), the three-way door and its proofs, cycle-deletion for spanning trees (P18), and the extremal reflex in its third job.

Lecture 4 · Naming All the Trees: Prüfer and Cayley

Here is a shock. The number of trees one can build on $n$ labeled vertices $\{1, \dots, n\}$ — plain $n^{n-2}$, a pure exponent — is Cayley's formula (Cayley, 1889). For $n = 4$ it says there are $16$ trees; the lab below counted them all, and there are exactly $16$. Now, how could anyone find such a formula? Not by listing trees. You find it by hoping for a miracle and then checking that the miracle is real: if only trees were secretly sequences — say, sequences of length $n - 2$ over an $n$-letter alphabet — the answer would be obvious, $n^{n-2}$. Prüfer (1918) proved the miracle true: there is a perfect way to name every tree by a sequence.

4.1 The code

To encode a labeled tree on $n \ge 2$ vertices: repeat $n - 2$ times — find the smallest leaf; write down the label of its neighbor; delete the leaf (and its edge). The string of written labels is the Prüfer code.

Work both tiny examples by hand — this is not optional. The star on $\{1,2,3,4\}$ centered at $4$: smallest leaf $1$, write $4$, delete; smallest leaf $2$, write $4$, delete; code $(4,4)$. The path $1\text{–}2\text{–}3\text{–}4$: leaves are $1$ and $4$; smallest is $1$, write its neighbor $2$, delete $1$; now the path $2\text{–}3\text{–}4$ has leaves $2$ and $4$; smallest is $2$, write $3$, delete. Code $(2,3)$. Two different trees, two different names.

4.2 The dictionary — why the code is a perfect name

Why does this lose no information? Because the code keeps an exact tally book of the tree:

Fact 1 (occurrences $=$ degree $- 1$). Label $v$ is written into the code $(a_1, \dots, a_{n-2})$ exactly $d(v) - 1$ times. Proof. Each step deletes a leaf and writes its unique living neighbor. Thus $v$ is written once each time a neighbor of $v$ is deleted while $v$ is still alive. If $v$ is eventually deleted at some step, it is a leaf at that moment, so exactly one of its $d(v)$ neighbors is still alive then; the other $d(v) - 1$ neighbors were deleted earlier, each writing $v$ once. If instead $v$ is one of the final two survivors, its co-survivor is never deleted and its other $d(v) - 1$ neighbors were all deleted earlier, again writing $v$ exactly $d(v) - 1$ times. $\blacksquare$

Fact 2 (current leaves $=$ living labels absent from the remaining code). At the start of step $i$ ($1 \le i \le n - 2$), let $T_i$ be the current tree on the $n - i + 1$ living vertices. A living vertex $v \in V(T_i)$ is a leaf of $T_i$ if and only if $v$ does not appear in the remaining suffix $(a_i, a_{i+1}, \dots, a_{n-2})$. Proof. Sending $T_i$ through the remaining $n - i - 1$ steps of the algorithm is identical to running the full Prüfer encoder on $T_i$, producing the suffix $(a_i, \dots, a_{n-2})$. Applying Fact 1 to the tree $T_i$, each living vertex $v \in V(T_i)$ appears in $(a_i, \dots, a_{n-2})$ exactly $d_{T_i}(v) - 1$ times. Since $T_i$ is a connected tree on at least $3$ vertices, every $v \in V(T_i)$ has $d_{T_i}(v) \ge 1$, so $v$ appears $0$ times in $(a_i, \dots, a_{n-2})$ if and only if $d_{T_i}(v) - 1 = 0$, i.e., $d_{T_i}(v) = 1$. $\blacksquare$

4.3 Decoding, and why it inverts (complete proof of both directions)

To decode any sequence $a = (a_1, \dots, a_{n-2}) \in \{1, \dots, n\}^{n-2}$: maintain the set $U$ of living (not yet deleted) labels, initially $U_1 = \{1, \dots, n\}$, and track the pool $P_i \subseteq U_i$ of living labels that do not appear in the remaining suffix $(a_i, \dots, a_{n-2})$. For $i = 1, \dots, n - 2$, take the smallest label $\ell_i = \min P_i$, add the edge $\{\ell_i, a_i\}$, and retire $\ell_i$ from the living set ($U_{i+1} = U_i \setminus \{\ell_i\}$). At the end, $U_{n-1}$ contains exactly two surviving labels; join them by a final edge.

Why decoding recovers any encoded tree ($\operatorname{decode}(\operatorname{encode}(T)) = T$). If $a = \operatorname{encode}(T)$, Fact 2 guarantees that at step $1$ the leaves of $T_1 = T$ are precisely the labels absent from $(a_1, \dots, a_{n-2})$, which is the initial pool $P_1$. Hence $\ell_1 = \min P_1$ is the smallest leaf of $T_1$, and $a_1$ is by definition its unique neighbor in $T_1$, so $\{\ell_1, a_1\}$ is the exact edge the encoder deleted first. Removing $\ell_1$ leaves $U_2 = V(T_2)$ and the suffix $(a_2, \dots, a_{n-2}) = \operatorname{encode}(T_2)$, so by induction the decoder reconstructs every deleted edge $\{\ell_i, a_i\}$ in order, and finally restores the last surviving edge on $U_{n-1}$. Thus $\operatorname{encode}$ is injective.

Why every sequence decodes to a tree that re-encodes to $a$ ($\operatorname{encode}(\operatorname{decode}(a)) = a$). Take an arbitrary sequence $a = (a_1, \dots, a_{n-2}) \in \{1, \dots, n\}^{n-2}$. At step $i$, $|U_i| = n - i + 1$ while the suffix $(a_i, \dots, a_{n-2})$ has length $n - i - 1$, so at least $(n - i + 1) - (n - i - 1) = 2$ living labels are absent from the suffix; hence $P_i$ is always non-empty, $\ell_i = \min P_i$ is well-defined, and $\ell_i \ne a_i$ (since $\ell_i \in P_i$ is absent from the suffix whereas $a_i$ is its first entry). Moreover, each $a_i$ belongs to $U_i$: the only labels removed before step $i$ are $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_{i-1}$, and each $\ell_j$ ($j < i$) was absent from $(a_j, \dots, a_{n-2})$, so $\ell_j \ne a_i$. Now view the construction in reverse, from $i = n - 2$ down to $1$: the final edge on the two vertices of $U_{n-1}$ forms a tree $G_{n-1}$ on $2$ vertices. Going from $G_{i+1}$ (on vertex set $U_{i+1}$) to $G_i$ (on $U_i = U_{i+1} \cup \{\ell_i\}$) attaches the single new vertex $\ell_i$ to the single existing vertex $a_i \in U_{i+1}$ via the edge $\{\ell_i, a_i\}$. Attaching a pendant vertex of degree $1$ to a tree always produces a tree, so by downward induction every $G_i$—and in particular the full graph $G_1 = \operatorname{decode}(a)$—is a tree! Better still, in $G_i$ each vertex $v \in U_i$ has degree $d_{G_i}(v) = 1 + (\text{occurrences of } v \text{ in } (a_i, \dots, a_{n-2}))$, so the leaves of $G_i$ are precisely the vertices of $P_i$. Therefore, at step $i$ of encoding $G_1$, the remaining tree is $G_i$, its smallest leaf is $\min P_i = \ell_i$, and the unique neighbor of $\ell_i$ in $G_i$ is $a_i$. The encoder therefore writes $a_1, \dots, a_{n-2}$ in turn, proving $\operatorname{encode}(\operatorname{decode}(a)) = a$. Two procedures, mutual inverses—a genuine bijection (Pattern P1). $\blacksquare$

Check it on Lecture 4.1's examples: decode $(4,4)$: pool $\{1,2,3\} \to$ join $1\text{–}4$, then $2\text{–}4$, then $3\text{–}4$: the star. Decode $(2,3)$: pool $\{1,4\}$: join $1\text{–}2$ ($2$'s count drops to $0$, so $2$ enters the pool); join $2\text{–}3$ ($3$ enters the pool); end survivors $\{3,4\}$: join $3\text{–}4$: the path. Checked.

And the punchline: $n - 2$ slots, $n$ choices each, one tree per sequence, one sequence per tree—the count is $n \cdot n \cdots n = \mathbf{n^{n-2}}$. $\blacksquare$

4.4 The dictionary pays rent: degree sequences

Fact 1 says a tree with degrees $(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ corresponds to a code containing label $i$ exactly $d_i - 1$ times. So the number of labeled trees with a given degree sequence is just the number of words of length $n - 2$ with prescribed letter counts—a multinomial coefficient:

$$\text{\# trees with degrees } (d_1, \dots, d_n) = \frac{(n - 2)!}{\prod_{i=1}^n (d_i - 1)!} \qquad \left(\text{when } \sum_{i=1}^n d_i = 2n - 2 \text{ and all } d_i \ge 1\right).$$

Verify on $n = 4$ before trusting it: the degree sum is $2n - 2 = 6$, so the sorted degree sequence of a $4$-vertex tree must be either $(1,1,1,3)$ (a star) or $(1,1,2,2)$ (a path). For stars: there are $\binom{4}{1} = 4$ choices for which vertex has degree $3$, and for each choice the formula gives $\frac{(4-2)!}{0!\,0!\,0!\,2!} = \frac{2!}{2!} = 1$ tree, yielding $4 \times 1 = 4$ stars. For paths: there are $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ ways to choose which two vertices have degree $2$, and for each choice the formula gives $\frac{(4-2)!}{0!\,0!\,1!\,1!} = \frac{2!}{1} = 2$ trees, yielding $6 \times 2 = 12$ paths. Total: $4 + 12 = \mathbf{16}$—recovering Cayley's $4^{4-2} = 16$ directly from the degree dictionary.

4.5 Joyal's other door (complete)

Cayley's formula has a second famous proof, Joyal's (1981), with a different soul — and a different moral: count something bigger, and count it somewhere easier. Prüfer names trees by sequences; Joyal packs a whole doubly-rooted tree into a single function, and Cayley falls out the other side without a word of the code dictionary.

The two worlds, counted first. A doubly-rooted tree is a labeled tree $T$ with two distinguished vertices $a$ and $b$ (possibly equal). Whatever the tree count turns out to be, each tree carries exactly $n^2$ doubly-rooted versions — $n$ choices for $a$ times $n$ for $b$ — so the map "forget the roots", $(T, a, b) \mapsto T$, is exactly $n^2$-to-$1$, by definition and nothing deeper (that is the non-circularity: no step of this proof leans on Cayley). In the other world, a function $f : \{1,\dots,n\} \to \{1,\dots,n\}$ is a list of $n$ values with $n$ choices each: $n^n$ functions, no graph theory involved. Joyal's claim: doubly-rooted trees and functions are in bijection. Comparing counts, $n^2 \cdot (\text{\#trees}) = n^n$, so $\text{\#trees} = n^{n-2}$ — Cayley, proved a second time.

Step 1 — read a function as a picture. Draw the functional digraph of $f$: an arrow $z \to f(z)$ out of every vertex. Follow the arrows from any start: you never get stuck (every vertex has an outgoing arrow), and there are only $n$ vertices — so you walk until a vertex repeats, and the repeat closes a cycle (a one-vertex loop $f(z) = z$ counts). Every function therefore looks the same: some disjoint cycles, with trees hanging off them, every branch arrowing inward toward its cycle. The cycle vertices are the spine-to-be.

Step 2 — $G$: function $\to$ doubly-rooted tree. Take the cycles of $f$. Order them by decreasing smallest label; rotate each to start at its smallest label; concatenate them all, in that order, into one sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k$ — the spine. Set $a = x_1$ and $b = x_k$. Now build $T$: join consecutive spine vertices by edges (the spine becomes a path from $a$ to $b$), and for every vertex $z$ not on a cycle, add the edge $\{z, f(z)\}$ — its old arrow, kept as an undirected rope pulling it toward the spine.

Why the result is a tree: every old cycle has been opened and its vertices reused along one path, so no cycles survive; every off-cycle chain still leads to the spine, so everything is connected. Count the edges while you are at it: the $k$ spine vertices contribute $k - 1$ edges, the $n - k$ others one rope each — $n - 1$ total, connected, acyclic: a tree, by the three-way door of §3.2. This is $G$: $f \mapsto (T, a, b)$.

Worked example ($n = 5$). $f(1) = 5$, $f(2) = 2$, $f(3) = 1$, $f(4) = 2$, $f(5) = 1$. Cycles: $(2)$ — it stands still — and $(1, 5)$, since $1 \to 5 \to 1$; off the cycles, $3 \to 1$ and $4 \to 2$. Decreasing minima: $(2)$ has smallest label $2$, $(1, 5)$ has $1$ — so $(2)$ goes first. Spine: $[2, 1, 5]$; $a = 2$, $b = 5$. Tree: the path $2\text{–}1\text{–}5$, plus the ropes $3\text{–}1$ and $4\text{–}2$ — the tree $4\text{–}2\text{–}1\text{–}5$ with a $3$ hanging off the $1$. Two sanity checks to carry in your pocket: the star $K_{1,3}$ centered at $4$ with $a = b = 4$ has spine $[4]$ and every leaf roped to the center — the constant function $f \equiv 4$. And the path $1\text{–}2\text{–}3\text{–}4$ rooted at $a = 4$, $b = 1$ has spine $[4, 3, 2, 1]$ — and gives back the identity function.

Step 3 — $F$: doubly-rooted tree $\to$ function. Given $(T, a, b)$: the spine is the unique $a$-to-$b$ path, $x_1 = a, \dots, x_k = b$ (§3.2, door (c)). Cut the spine into blocks that begin exactly at the strict left-to-right records of the label sequence — a record being a label smaller than every label to its left. Inside one block $x_i, x_{i+1}, \dots, x_j$ (from a record up to just before the next): set $f(x_m) = x_{m+1}$ for $m < j$ — walk forward — and $f(x_j) = x_i$: the block closes into a cycle at its smallest label. For a vertex $z$ off the spine: $f(z) =$ the neighbor of $z$ on its path toward the spine. This is $F$: $(T, a, b) \mapsto f$.

Step 4 — why the round trip closes, both ways. After $G$, the spine is the concatenation of cycles in decreasing order of minima, each written min-first. So each cycle's minimum is a strict record — it is smaller than the minima of all earlier cycles, hence smaller than every earlier spine label — and no other spine label is a record, since it exceeds its own cycle's minimum, which sits to its left. So $F$'s record rule cuts the spine back into exactly the original cycles, in exactly the original order, and reads each one correctly; and off the spine, $G$ roped $z$ to $f(z)$, so "neighbor toward the spine" hands $f(z)$ right back. That is $F(G(f)) = f$. Conversely, after $F$ the cycles are the blocks — and records strictly decrease, so the blocks arrive already sorted by decreasing minima, min-first — while the spine path and the off-spine chains rebuild $T$ edge for edge, and $a, b$ are the spine's ends. That is $G(F(T, a, b)) = (T, a, b)$. Two procedures, mutual inverses: a bijection (P1 at full power — the named objects are no longer sequences but whole wired structures). $\blacksquare$

Before installation this was machine-verified in both directions (appendix): every one of the $4^4 = 256$ functions on $\{1,\dots,4\}$ and all $5^5 = 3125$ on $\{1,\dots,5\}$ survives $f \to G \to F \to f$; every doubly-rooted tree (all $256$ on $4$ labels, all $3125$ on $5$) survives $(T, a, b) \to F \to G \to (T, a, b)$; zero failures either way.

What you now own: structure-as-sequence bijections (P1), Joyal's pack-the-whole-structure bijection — Cayley's second proof, independent of Prüfer — the two dictionary facts and their proofs, degree-sequence counting for free.

Lecture 5 · Königsberg: the Seven Bridges

The city of Königsberg sat on a river with an island, joined by seven bridges. The citizens asked, on Sunday walks: can one cross every bridge exactly once and come home? Euler answered in 1736 — no — and in answering invented the subject: he declared that only the wiring matters (four land masses, seven connections) and nothing else. Land masses become vertices, bridges become edges (several allowed between the same pair — a multigraph).

Theorem. A connected multigraph has a closed walk using every edge exactly once (an Euler tour) iff every vertex has even degree. (For a walk that need not close — an Euler trail — the condition is: at most two odd vertices, and the walk runs between them.)

5.1 Why even degrees are necessary

On a closed walk, each visit to a vertex consumes its edges in pairs: one edge in, one edge out. Enter three times and you must leave three times. So every degree touched by the walk is even; if the walk covers all edges, all degrees are even. $\blacksquare$ (P7 — parity again, now as a necessary condition.)

And Königsberg dies by Lecture 1: the degrees are $5$ (the island Kneiphof) and $3, 3, 3$ — four odd vertices; check the total against the handshaking lemma: $5+3+3+3 = 14 = 2 \cdot 7$ $\checkmark$. Impossible.

5.2 Why even degrees are enough — Hierholzer's splicing (complete)

Now the converse, and the proof is a machine you can run. Walk greedily from any vertex, never reusing an edge. When can you get stuck? Only at a vertex with no fresh edge left. If you get stuck at some vertex $w \ne \text{start}$, then $w$ was entered once more than it was left — one of its edges used without replacement, contradicting $d(w)$ even. So the walk can only die at the starting vertex, and it dies holding a closed trail $C$. If $C$ misses some edge, then (the graph being connected) some vertex $w$ on $C$ touches an unused edge; the unused edges at every vertex still number even (we removed a closed trail, which used each visited vertex's edges in pairs — pairs again!); so from $w$ we can greedily walk a second closed trail $C'$ through unused edges, and splice $C'$ into $C$ at $w$. Each splice strictly enlarges one closed trail; edges are finite; the machine halts with a single closed trail covering every edge. $\blacksquare$ (P18: the proof is an $O(m)$ algorithm — this is why Euler's theorem is not just true but usable: your route-finder can actually run it.)

5.3 The harder brother — and the lesson of the difference

Change one word in the problem — cross every bridge once becomes visit every land mass once — and you get Hamilton's problem (a Hamilton cycle). The two problems look like twins. They are not. Euler's condition is local (a parity check at each vertex) that composes globally — pieces weld, as in the splicing. Hamilton's is a global booking constraint — every vertex must be visited exactly once in one unbroken chain — and no local check certifies it; deciding Hamiltonicity is NP-hard (Chapter 11 makes this precise). When two problems look like twins but only one has a good theory, the structural difference is the lesson. Here it is: additive, local conditions compose; global one-of-each constraints do not.

But this raises a question worth a whole lecture: since no exact local characterization of Hamiltonicity exists, what is the strongest sufficient local condition? Answer: half the vertices. That is Lecture 6.

What you now own: parity as necessity (P7), splicing as sufficiency (P18), the local-vs-global divide — and the right question to ask next.

Lecture 6 · Dirac: When Half Is Enough

In 1952 a young graph theorist named Gabriel Dirac — who arrived at mathematics by a side door of a famous family: he was the stepson of the physicist Paul Dirac — proved the following: if every vertex of a graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices has degree at least $n/2$, then the graph has a Hamilton cycle. Minimum degree halfway to "adjacent to everyone" already forces a perfect tour. Before the proof, feel why it's plausible: take any two vertices that are strangers — $u \not\sim v$. Both neighborhoods then live inside the same $n - 2$ other vertices, and $|N(u)| + |N(v)| \ge 2 \cdot (n/2) = n > n - 2$, so the two circles cannot miss each other: $N(u) \cap N(v) \ne \emptyset$. The graph is tight everywhere — no two strangers have disjoint acquaintance circles. (Adjacent pairs are exempt from this argument, and rightly so: in $K_{3,3}$ two adjacent vertices from opposite sides have disjoint neighborhoods of size $n/2$ each. Strangers are what the engine consumes.) That tightness is the fuel; the proof's job is to route it into a single unbroken tour.

6.1 The proof — corner both ends at once

Take a longest path $P = v_1 v_2 \dots v_k$ (the extremal reflex, P8, now with both ends working). Each endpoint is cornered: all neighbors of $v_1$ and of $v_k$ lie on $P$ (an outside neighbor would extend $P$). Define two sets of positions along the path:

$$\mathbf{S} = \{ i : 1 \le i \le k - 1 \text{ and } v_1 \sim v_{i+1} \} \quad \text{(positions just before a neighbor of } v_1\text{)}$$ $$\mathbf{T} = \{ i : 1 \le i \le k - 1 \text{ and } v_i \sim v_k \} \quad \text{(positions just after a neighbor of } v_k\text{)}$$

Count them: $v_1$'s neighbors all lie among $v_2, \dots, v_k$, and the rule $i \mapsto v_{i+1}$ matches them to positions one-to-one, so $|S| = d(v_1) \ge n/2$; likewise $|T| = d(v_k) \ge n/2$. Both $S$ and $T$ live in the same $k - 1$ positions. If they never met, $|S| + |T| \le k - 1 < n \le |S| + |T|$ — contradiction. So some $i \in S \cap T$: the two chords $v_1 v_{i+1}$ and $v_i v_k$ both exist. (This is pigeonhole, P9 — but note the upgrade: we are not matching pigeons to holes, we are detecting an overlap of two majority subsets, which is the same arithmetic wearing a better suit.)

Now read off the cycle: start at $v_1$, walk the path to $v_i$, jump the chord to $v_k$, walk the path backwards to $v_{i+1}$, jump the chord home to $v_1$:
$v_1 v_2 \dots v_i v_k v_{k-1} \dots v_{i+1} v_1$ — a cycle through all $k$ vertices of $P$.

If $k < n$, so that $V(G) \setminus V(P)$ is non-empty, connectedness forces at least one edge between $V(G) \setminus V(P)$ and the cycle on $V(P)$: there is some outside vertex $x \notin V(P)$ adjacent to some cycle vertex $v_j$. Breaking the cycle at one of its two edges incident to $v_j$ gives a simple path $x v_j v_{j+1} \dots v_{j-1}$ on $k + 1$ vertices — strictly longer than the longest path $P$. Contradiction. Hence $k = n$ and the cycle is Hamiltonian. $\blacksquare$

6.2 Method 2 — prove more, work less (Ore's theorem)

Look again at the engine: all it consumed was $d(v_1) + d(v_k) \ge n$. Dirac supplies that via $\delta \ge n/2$. But Øystein Ore (1960) noticed that the endpoints of a longest path have a special status — and if they are adjacent, the path $v_1 \dots v_k$ already closes into a cycle covering all $k$ vertices, and the connectivity-extension argument of §6.1 finishes it ($k < n$ would yield a longer path — contradiction). So assume $v_1 \not\sim v_k$: they form a non-adjacent pair, and Ore's hypothesis applies to exactly such pairs:

Ore's theorem. If $d(u) + d(v) \ge n$ for every non-adjacent pair $u, v$, then $G$ is Hamiltonian.

The proof is now §6.1 with "$\delta \ge n/2$" replaced by "Ore's condition on the (non-adjacent) endpoints" — and one repair the substitution exposes: §6.1 bought connectivity from the component-size lemma, which leans on $\delta \ge n/2$, and Ore does not sell that. Restore it directly: if $u, v$ lay in different components, of sizes $a$ and $b$, they would be non-adjacent with $d(u) + d(v) \le (a - 1) + (b - 1) = a + b - 2 \le n - 2 < n$, contradicting Ore's condition before the path engine even starts. So $G$ is connected, and the rest is §6.1 untouched. Dirac's theorem drops out as a corollary, since $\delta \ge n/2$ makes every pair's degree sum $\ge n$. $\blacksquare$

Here is the meta-lesson, one of the best in the book: when an extremal argument stalls at "$d(a) + d(b) \ge n$", do not demand it of every pair — demand it only where the proof actually looks, at the non-adjacent pairs. The theorem gets stronger and the proof does not get harder. Call this the Ore move: generalize the statement until the engine closes, and not one step further. The strongest theorem provable by a given proof is usually the one worth stating.

6.3 The bound is honest

Can $n/2$ be relaxed to $\lfloor (n-1)/2 \rfloor$? No—and here are the witnesses (constructions, Pattern P11 in reverse: not "build to beat a bound" but "build to show a bound cannot move"). For even $n$: two disjoint cliques $K_{n/2} \sqcup K_{n/2}$ have $\delta = n/2 - 1$ and, being disconnected, no Hamilton cycle. Even if we insist on connected witnesses, unbalanced complete bipartite graphs block the tour for every $n \ge 5$: for odd $n = 2k + 1$, $K_{k, k+1}$ has $\delta = k = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$, and for even $n = 2k \ge 6$, $K_{k-1, k+1}$ (such as $K_{2,4}$ on $n = 6$, caught by the lab below) has $\delta = k - 1 = n/2 - 1$. Both are connected, yet neither has a Hamilton cycle: every cycle in a bipartite graph alternates between the two parts, so it must visit equal numbers of vertices from each side and can never cover two parts of unequal size. And the famous Petersen graph ($3$-regular on $10$ vertices, $\delta = 3 < 5$) is connected, bridgeless, and stubbornly non-Hamiltonian—the lab confirmed all of this by brute force. Dirac's $n/2$ is exactly the frontier. Beyond it lies the degree-sequence landscape that §6.4 now enters—Pósa's theorem and Chvátal's closure—and far beyond that, the grail of a "good" characterization, which (Chapter 11) cannot exist unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.

6.4 Pósa: bill the poor vertices (Chvátal's closure)

Dirac prices every vertex at $n/2$ and Ore prices every stranger pair at $n$. Both are demanding landlords: one shabby vertex and the lease is off. But shabby vertices are often harmless. Take $K_{n-1}$ and attach a new vertex $p$ to just two clique vertices: $p$ is as poor as a connected vertex can be ($d(p) = 2$), yet the tour writes itself — $p$, one neighbor, then everyone else in the clique, then the other neighbor, home. In 1962 a fifteen-year-old Budapest schoolboy named Lajos Pósa — Erdős loved to tell the story of finding him at his grandmother's kitchen table, already proving theorems — noticed that Hamiltonicity does not need every vertex rich. It needs the poor to be few, and it bills them exactly:

Pósa's theorem (1962). Let $G$ have $n \ge 3$ vertices, with degrees sorted $d_1 \le d_2 \le \dots \le d_n$. If

$d_k \ge k + 1$ for every integer $k < n/2$ — equivalently, for every $k < n/2$, fewer than $k$ vertices have degree at most $k$ — then $G$ has a Hamilton cycle.

Check the equivalence before reading on: $d_k \ge k+1$ says the $k$-th smallest degree already clears $k$, which says at most $k-1$ degrees are $\le k$. Then check the boundary before trusting the range — the condition must run all the way to $k < n/2$, not stop a step earlier. §6.3's own witness $K_{3,4}$ ($n = 7$) has degrees $3,3,3,3,4,4,4$: $d_1 = 3 \ge 2$ $\checkmark$, $d_2 = 3 \ge 3$ $\checkmark$ — but $d_3 = 3 < 4$ $\times$, and $K_{3,4}$ has no Hamilton cycle. The failure lands exactly at the last index the condition covers. The "$+1$" is just as honest: weaken $d_k \ge k+1$ to $d_k \ge k$ and $K_{3,4}$ slips through ($d_3 = 3 \ge 3$); on six vertices the weakened condition is met by $21{,}218$ graphs, of which $11{,}140$ are tourless (the lab, Part 3). Pósa's line cannot move in either direction.

The plan. A direct assault on the longest path — Dirac's engine — consumes degree sums, and Pósa's condition does not sell them. Pósa's own 1962 proof instead rotated the longest path, counting the vertices his rotations could reach (Exercise 18 develops his rotation lemma — the engine that made his name). The cleanest complete route today is Chvátal's (1972), and it runs on two ideas, each worth owning for life.

Idea 1 — the closure lemma (Bondy–Chvátal, 1976). If $u$ and $v$ are non-adjacent and $d(u) + d(v) \ge n$, then adding the edge $uv$ cannot create or destroy Hamiltonicity: $G + uv$ is Hamiltonian if and only if $G$ is. The "only if" is trivial (a cycle of $G$ is a cycle of $G + uv$); the "if" is §6.1's engine in miniature. Suppose $G + uv$ has a Hamilton cycle using the new edge. Delete $uv$: what remains is a Hamilton path of $G$ from $u$ to $v$ — write it $u = w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n = v$, every step an honest $G$-edge. Hunt a crossing pair of positions, exactly as §6.1 hunted $S \cap T$:

$$\mathbf{A} = \{ i : 2 \le i \le n - 1 \text{ and } w_i \sim v \} \quad \text{(positions of } v\text{'s neighbors)}$$ $$\mathbf{B} = \{ i : 1 \le i \le n - 2 \text{ and } w_{i+1} \sim u \} \quad \text{(positions just before } u\text{'s neighbors)}$$

Because $u$ and $v$ are strangers in $G$, $v$'s neighbors all sit at positions $2\dots n-1$ — so $|A| = d(v)$ — and $u$'s likewise, so $|B| = d(u)$. Both $A$ and $B$ live among the $n - 1$ positions $\{1, \dots, n-1\}$, yet $|A| + |B| = d(u) + d(v) \ge n > n - 1$: some $i \in A \cap B$. Read off the cycle — forward along the path to $w_i$, jump the chord $w_i\text{–}v$, backward along the path to $w_{i+1}$, jump the chord $w_{i+1}\text{–}u$ home. That is Figure 4's picture verbatim, with $(v_1, v_k)$ renamed $(u, v)$, and every edge in it belongs to $G$. $\blacksquare$ (P9, the overlap-of-majorities version — third appearance, now on a Hamilton path.)

Iterate the lemma: keep joining non-adjacent pairs with degree sum $\ge n$ until no pair qualifies. The result is the closure $\operatorname{cl}(G)$, and every addition was certified harmless, so $G$ is Hamiltonian if and only if $\operatorname{cl}(G)$ is.

Idea 2 — sorted degrees only climb. Adding an edge raises two degrees by one each, and the sorted degree sequence never drops at any position. Compare the $j$ smallest degrees before ($a_1 \le \dots \le a_j$) and after: the $j$ smallest after are $j$ of the old values, each either kept or raised by one. Match each to its base value: the bases are $j$ distinct old degrees, so the largest base is at least $a_j$ — and each of the $j$ new values is at least its base. Hence the $j$-th smallest degree after is $\ge a_j$. $\blacksquare$ (P2 in yet another costume: match, then compare.)

Chvátal's theorem (1972). If for every integer $k$ with $1 \le k < n/2$: $d_k \ge k+1$ or $d_{n-k} \ge n-k$, then $G$ ($n \ge 3$) is Hamiltonian.

Proof. First, the condition survives closure: by Idea 2 each $d_k$ and each $d_{n-k}$ only climbs under edge additions, so a satisfied disjunct stays satisfied in the closure $H = \operatorname{cl}(G)$. Note also that $H$ cannot contain an isolated vertex: if $d_1(H) = 0$, then no vertex of $H$ can be adjacent to that isolated vertex, so every vertex of $H$ has degree at most $n - 2$; thus $d_1(H) = 0 < 2$ and $d_{n-1}(H) \le n - 2 < n - 1$, violating both arms of Chvátal's condition at $k = 1$. Hence every vertex of $H$ has degree at least $1$.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $H = \operatorname{cl}(G)$ is not complete. Choose a non-adjacent pair $u, v$ in $H$ with the largest degree sum $d_H(u) + d_H(v)$, labeled so that $1 \le d_H(u) = k \le d_H(v)$. Because the closure is saturated, $d_H(u) + d_H(v) \le n - 1$ (a sum of $n$ or more would have triggered another edge addition). In particular, $2k \le d_H(u) + d_H(v) \le n - 1 < n$, so $1 \le k < n/2$. Now make two counts in $H$, at $u$ and at $v$:

At $u$: Every stranger $w$ of $u$ ($w \notin N_H(u) \cup \{u\}$) is non-adjacent to $u$, so by the extremal choice of $(u,v)$, $d_H(u) + d_H(w) \le d_H(u) + d_H(v) \le n - 1$, giving $d_H(w) \le d_H(v) \le n - 1 - k$. There are $(n - 1) - d_H(u) = n - 1 - k$ such strangers of $u$; together with $u$ itself (which has $d_H(u) = k \le n - 1 - k$), we have $1 + (n - 1 - k) = n - k$ distinct vertices in $H$, every one of degree at most $n - k - 1$. Therefore $d_{n-k}(H) \le n - k - 1$: the second disjunct fails at index $k$.

At $v$: Every stranger $z$ of $v$ ($z \notin N_H(v) \cup \{v\}$) is non-adjacent to $v$, so maximality of $d_H(u) + d_H(v)$ gives $d_H(z) + d_H(v) \le d_H(u) + d_H(v)$, or $d_H(z) \le d_H(u) = k$. Since $d_H(v) \le n - 1 - k$, the number of strangers of $v$ is $(n - 1) - d_H(v) \ge (n - 1) - (n - 1 - k) = k$. Thus at least $k$ vertices of $H$ have degree at most $k$, which forces $d_k(H) \le k$: the first disjunct also fails at index $k$.

Both arms fail in $H$ at the same index $k \in [1, n/2)$—impossible, since $H$ inherited Chvátal's condition from $G$. Therefore $\operatorname{cl}(G) = K_n$, which is Hamiltonian (for $n \ge 3$), and by the closure lemma $G$ is Hamiltonian too. $\blacksquare$

Pósa's theorem is now a corollary: Pósa's condition $d_k \ge k+1$ holds on the entire range $k < n/2$, so it satisfies Chvátal's disjunction by its first arm everywhere — no second arm ever needed. Dirac falls out too: $\delta \ge n/2$ gives $d_k \ge \lceil n/2 \rceil \ge k+1$. And the chain is strict: on six vertices the lab counts $1858$ Dirac graphs inside $5218$ Pósa graphs inside $5788$ Chvátal graphs — $3360$ graphs that Pósa certifies and Dirac cannot (all $1080$ with degree sequence $(2,3,3,3,3,4)$ among them, every single one Hamiltonian), and $570$ more that only Chvátal reaches (all verified Hamiltonian; Exercise 13's table makes you run the diagnostics yourself).

One honest boundary. What Pósa does not claim: a graph failing the condition at some index is simply outside the theorem — some such graphs are Hamiltonian, some are not, and no degree-sequence test can separate them in general. Chvátal's test is the best possible of its kind: if the condition fails at even one index, some non-Hamiltonian graph has a degree sequence dominating yours pointwise (Chvátal's clique-stitching construction — not proved here; see his 1972 paper). And the true prize — a "good" characterization of Hamiltonicity itself — cannot exist unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$ (Chapter 11).

What you now own (Lecture 6, complete): the cornered-both-ends method (P8 + P9 fused), connectivity-from-minimum-degree, the Ore move, the closure move — add what cannot change the answer, then argue about the saturated object — and honest tightness witnesses at every boundary.

Lecture 7 · One From Each Path: Gallai–Milgram

The last lecture opens the door to directed graphs — a lecture says otherwise, and this one does. A digraph is dots with one-way arrows between them (no loops needed here); a directed path is a walk that follows the arrows; and two vertices are adjacent if some arrow runs between them, either way — that is adjacency in the underlying graph, and $\alpha(D)$ will always mean the independence number of that underlying graph.

Here is the seed, already in your pocket. Exercise 20 (Rédei's theorem) says: in any tournament — every pair joined by exactly one arrow — the players can always be ranked in a chain $p_1 \to p_2 \to \dots \to p_n$, a directed Hamilton path. In 1960 Tibor Gallai and Arthur Milgram published a paper whose title, translated, reads "A generalization of a graph-theoretic theorem of Rédei." Generalization how? A tournament is the most hostile geometry imaginable for independence: every two vertices adjacent, so $\alpha = 1$. Rédei says one directed path then covers everything. Gallai and Milgram asked what happens in an arbitrary digraph, where long directed paths can be impossible outright (two vertices with no arrow either way admit no path through both). The answer: the vertices can always be split into few disjoint directed paths — and the price is exactly $\alpha$.

Theorem (Gallai–Milgram 1960). Every digraph $D$ has a path cover — vertex-disjoint directed paths covering every vertex exactly once — consisting of at most $\alpha(D)$ paths.

We will prove something stronger, a form you can see with. Call a set $S$ a cross-cut of a cover $P$ if $S$ contains exactly one vertex from each path of $P$.

Theorem (cross-cut form). Every digraph has a path cover $P$ and an independent cross-cut $S$ of $P$.

The cross-cut form hands over the theorem as a corollary: $|P| = |S| \le \alpha$. But it is more than a repackaging — it names a witness (the independent set) matched path-for-path, which is exactly what the proof constructs. (Try the tiny example of the digraph with arcs $w \to x$ and $v \to x$: the minimum covers $\{(w,x),(v)\}$ and $\{(v,x),(w)\}$ each admit the independent cross-cut $\{w, v\}$ — but note it is not the set of endpoints: $v \to x$ joins the ends of the first cover. The witness lives in the proof, not in the ends.)

7.1 End-minimal covers

A cover's end-set is the set of last vertices of its paths — $k$ paths, $k$ distinct ends. Call a cover end-minimal if no cover of $D$ has an end-set that is a proper subset of its end-set. Two facts make this notion golden:

Every minimum-size cover is end-minimal. A proper sub-end-set has smaller size, so it would belong to a cover with fewer paths — contradiction.

End-minimality is what survives deletion. If we delete a vertex, a cover of the smaller digraph may stop being minimum-size — but with a nested notion, the induction can compare end-sets directly. This is the "prove more, work less" move again: strengthen the induction hypothesis until deletion can't break it. (The Ore move's cousin — generalizing the statement until the proof closes.)

7.2 The proof (induction on the number of vertices, P15)

Claim. Every end-minimal path cover of every digraph admits an independent cross-cut.

Base. The empty digraph: the empty cover, cross-cut $\emptyset$. Induction step: let $D$ have $n$ vertices, $P = \{p_1, \dots, p_k\}$ an end-minimal cover, ends $v_1, \dots, v_k$. If the end-set $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is independent, it is itself a cross-cut — done. Otherwise some arrow joins two ends; relabel so it runs $v_2 \to v_1$ (call it the blue arrow).

First, $p_1$ cannot be the single vertex $v_1$. If it were, splicing $v_2 \to v_1$ onto $p_2$'s end and deleting $p_1$ would leave a cover whose end-set is the old one minus $v_2$ — a proper subset, forbidden by end-minimality. So $p_1$ has at least two vertices; let $v$ be its second-to-last vertex, joined to $v_1$ by the path's own last step (the red arrow $v \to v_1$).

Now delete $v_1$. The digraph $D' = D - v_1$ inherits the cover $P' = \{p_1', p_2, \dots, p_k\}$, where $p_1'$ is $p_1$ shortened by one (its end is now $v$). The end-sets: $\operatorname{Ends}(P') = \{v, v_2, \dots, v_k\}$. The two arrows that used to touch $v_1$:

$P'$ is end-minimal in $D'$. Suppose not: some cover $Q'$ of $D'$ has $\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \subsetneq \operatorname{Ends}(P') = \{v, v_2, \dots, v_k\}$. Reinsert $v_1$ — there are three routes, and each one contradicts $P$'s end-minimality in $D$:

  • Red route ($v \in \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$): Extend the path of $Q'$ ending at $v$ along the red arrow $v \to v_1$. Since $\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \subsetneq \{v, v_2, \dots, v_k\}$ and $v \in \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$, we have $\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \setminus \{v\} \subsetneq \{v_2, \dots, v_k\}$. Thus the resulting cover of $D$ has end-set $$(\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \setminus \{v\}) \cup \{v_1\} \subsetneq \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k\} = \operatorname{Ends}(P),$$ contradicting the end-minimality of $P$. $\times$
  • Blue route ($v \notin \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$ and $v_2 \in \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$): Since $v \notin \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$, we have $\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \subseteq \{v_2, \dots, v_k\}$. Extend the path of $Q'$ ending at $v_2$ along the blue arrow $v_2 \to v_1$. The resulting cover of $D$ has end-set $$(\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \setminus \{v_2\}) \cup \{v_1\} \subseteq \{v_1, v_3, \dots, v_k\} = \operatorname{Ends}(P) \setminus \{v_2\} \subsetneq \operatorname{Ends}(P),$$ again a proper subset of $\operatorname{Ends}(P)$. $\times$
  • Singleton route ($v \notin \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$ and $v_2 \notin \operatorname{Ends}(Q')$): Here $\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \subseteq \{v_3, \dots, v_k\}$. Add the one-vertex path $\{v_1\}$ to $Q'$: the resulting cover of $D$ has end-set $$\operatorname{Ends}(Q') \cup \{v_1\} \subseteq \{v_1, v_3, \dots, v_k\} = \operatorname{Ends}(P) \setminus \{v_2\} \subsetneq \operatorname{Ends}(P),$$ a proper subset once more. $\times$

Every route shrinks the end-set, and end-minimality forbids all three. So $P'$ is end-minimal in $D'$, and the induction hypothesis applies: $P'$ has an independent cross-cut $S'$. But $S'$ avoids $v_1$, so no arrow of $D$ among $S'$'s vertices was deleted — $S'$ is independent in $D$ itself. And its choices still hit $p_1' \subseteq p_1$ and every other $p_i$: it is an independent cross-cut of the original $P$. $\blacksquare$

Sit back and see the shape: an extremal cover, an arc that tempts a merge, a deletion, and a three-way case check that the nested minimality absorbs. No counting, no degree sums — pure structure. That is why Gallai–Milgram generalizes so far.

7.3 What the theorem buys

(a) The partition form. $|\text{cover}| = |\text{cross-cut}| \le \alpha$: every digraph splits into at most $\alpha$ directed paths. (b) Rédei. A tournament has $\alpha = 1$, so one directed path covers everyone — Exercise 20's insertion argument finds it constructively; Gallai–Milgram explains why it must exist. (c) A length bound for free. If the longest directed path of $D$ has $\ell$ vertices, then $n \le \ell \cdot \alpha$, so some directed path has at least $n/\alpha$ vertices — every digraph with small independence number is forced to contain a long directed path (pigeonhole over the $\le \alpha$ covering paths). (d) Undirected graphs too. Replace each edge by arrows both ways: undirected paths are directed paths of the biorientation, so every undirected graph also covers by $\le \alpha$ paths and contains a path on $\ge n/\alpha$ vertices.

And the family resemblance goes deeper. Take a partially ordered set, view it as a digraph (arrow from smaller to larger), and make it transitive: directed paths are chains, independent sets are antichains — and Gallai–Milgram becomes Dilworth's theorem (Chapter 5, Lecture 5): a poset of width $w$ splits into $w$ chains. Gallai–Milgram is Dilworth with the transitivity sanded off — the proof above never used it.

The bound is honest. Take $n/2$ disjoint arrows ($n$ even) and nothing else: a directed path cannot leave its own arrow, so a cover needs at least $n/2$ paths — and the $n/2$ arrows are exactly such a cover. Meanwhile picking one endpoint from each arrow gives an independent set of size $n/2$, so $\alpha = n/2$: cover and independence meet exactly at $\alpha$. (The empty digraph is the other equality: $n$ paths, $\alpha = n$.)

What you now own: end-minimality — extremality by inclusion, minimal not by size but by a nested quantity (P8's spirit, P15's mechanics) — the cross-cut way of seeing transversal witnesses, and a theorem that quietly unifies Rédei and Dilworth.

from collections import Counter

def prufer_encode(edges, n):
    if n < 2:
        raise ValueError("Prufer bijection requires n >= 2")
    adj = {v: set() for v in range(1, n + 1)}
    for u, v in edges:
        adj[u].add(v); adj[v].add(u)
    alive, seq = set(range(1, n + 1)), []
    for _ in range(n - 2):
        leaf = min(v for v in alive if len(adj[v] & alive) == 1)
        nb = next(iter(adj[leaf] & alive))
        seq.append(nb); alive.remove(leaf)
    return seq

def prufer_decode(seq, n):
    if n < 2 or len(seq) != n - 2:
        raise ValueError("Require n >= 2 and len(seq) == n - 2")
    cnt = Counter(seq)
    pool = [v for v in range(1, n + 1) if cnt[v] == 0]
    edges = []
    for a in seq:
        leaf = min(pool); pool.remove(leaf)
        edges.append(tuple(sorted((leaf, a))))
        cnt[a] -= 1
        if cnt[a] == 0:
            pool.append(a)
    u, v = sorted(pool)
    edges.append((u, v))
    return sorted(edges)

def joyal_func_to_tree(f, n):       # G: f -> (T, a, b) from Lecture 4.5
    state, in_cycle, cycles = [0] * (n + 1), set(), []
    for start in range(1, n + 1):
        if state[start]: continue
        path, cur = [], start
        while not state[cur]:
            state[cur] = start; path.append(cur); cur = f[cur]
        if state[cur] == start:
            cyc = path[path.index(cur):]
            m = cyc.index(min(cyc))
            cyc = cyc[m:] + cyc[:m]
            cycles.append(cyc); in_cycle.update(cyc)
    cycles.sort(key=lambda c: c[0], reverse=True)
    spine = [x for c in cycles for x in c]
    edges = [tuple(sorted((spine[i], spine[i + 1]))) for i in range(len(spine) - 1)]
    for z in range(1, n + 1):
        if z not in in_cycle:
            edges.append(tuple(sorted((z, f[z]))))
    return sorted(edges), spine[0], spine[-1]

def joyal_tree_to_func(edges, a, b, n):  # F: (T, a, b) -> f from Lecture 4.5
    adj = {v: [] for v in range(1, n + 1)}
    for u, v in edges:
        adj[u].append(v); adj[v].append(u)
    parent = {a: 0}; stack = [a]
    while stack:
        u = stack.pop()
        for w in adj[u]:
            if w not in parent:
                parent[w] = u; stack.append(w)
    spine, cur = [], b
    while cur:
        spine.append(cur); cur = parent[cur]
    spine.reverse()
    rec_idx, cur_min = [], n + 1
    for idx, x in enumerate(spine):
        if x < cur_min:
            rec_idx.append(idx); cur_min = x
    rec_idx.append(len(spine))
    f = {}
    for b_i in range(len(rec_idx) - 1):
        block = spine[rec_idx[b_i]:rec_idx[b_i + 1]]
        for j in range(len(block) - 1):
            f[block[j]] = block[j + 1]
        f[block[-1]] = block[0]
    spine_set = set(spine)
    q = list(spine); toward = {x: x for x in spine}
    for u in q:
        for w in adj[u]:
            if w not in toward:
                toward[w] = u; f[w] = u; q.append(w)
    return f

def has_hamilton_cycle(adj, n):
    if n < 3:
        return False                # a simple cycle requires at least 3 vertices
    visited = [False] * n
    found = False
    def dfs(v, count):
        nonlocal found
        if found: return
        if count == n:
            if adj[v][0]: found = True
            return
        for u in range(n):
            if adj[v][u] and not visited[u]:
                visited[u] = True; dfs(u, count + 1); visited[u] = False
    visited[0] = True; dfs(0, 1)
    return found

def hierholzer_euler_tour(edges, n):  # Lecture 5.2's O(m) splicing machine
    adj = [list() for _ in range(n)]
    for idx, (u, v) in enumerate(edges):
        adj[u].append((v, idx)); adj[v].append((u, idx))
    if any(len(adj[v]) % 2 == 1 for v in range(n)):
        return None
    used = [False] * len(edges)
    start = next((v for v in range(n) if adj[v]), 0)
    stack, tour = [start], []
    while stack:
        u = stack[-1]
        while adj[u] and used[adj[u][-1][1]]:
            adj[u].pop()
        if not adj[u]:
            tour.append(stack.pop())
        else:
            v, e_idx = adj[u].pop()
            used[e_idx] = True; stack.append(v)
    return tour[::-1] if len(tour) == len(edges) + 1 else None

Three conditions on the sorted degrees $d_1 \le \dots \le d_n$ ($k < n/2$ throughout): Pósa $d_k \ge k+1$; Chvátal $d_k \ge k+1$ or $d_{n-k} \ge n-k$; and the weak condition $d_k \ge k$ that §6.4 warns about. def closure_check(n):               # Ham(G) must equal Ham(G + uv), both ways
    if n < 3:
        raise ValueError("Closure check requires n >= 3")
    pairs = [(a, b) for a in range(n) for b in range(a + 1, n)]
    checked = disagreed = 0
    for mask in range(1 << len(pairs)):
        adj = [[False] * n for _ in range(n)]
        deg = [0] * n
        for i, (a, b) in enumerate(pairs):
            if mask >> i & 1:
                adj[a][b] = adj[b][a] = True; deg[a] += 1; deg[b] += 1
        base_ham = None
        for i, (u, v) in enumerate(pairs):
            if not (mask >> i & 1) and deg[u] + deg[v] >= n:
                checked += 1
                if base_ham is None:
                    base_ham = has_hamilton_cycle(adj, n)
                adj[u][v] = adj[v][u] = True
                flip = has_hamilton_cycle(adj, n)
                adj[u][v] = adj[v][u] = False
                if flip != base_ham: disagreed += 1
    return checked, disagreed

Part 4 — Gallai–Milgram, verified by exhaustion (verified 2026-10-10)

Every loopless digraph on $n$ vertices is a bitmask over its $n(n-1)$ possible arcs. min_path_cover is exact (a subset DP: which vertex sets can be arranged into one directed path, then how few such paths cover everything); alpha_underlying is exact too (independence in the graph that forgets arrow directions).

from itertools import permutations

def min_path_cover(out_adj, n):
    if n <= 0:
        return 0
    full = (1 << n) - 1
    hstart = [0] * (full + 1)      # hstart[S] = bitmask of valid start vertices of a directed path spanning S
    for S in range(1, full + 1):
        for u in range(n):
            if S >> u & 1:
                if S == 1 << u:
                    hstart[S] |= 1 << u
                elif hstart[S ^ (1 << u)] & out_adj[u]:
                    hstart[S] |= 1 << u
    dp = [n + 1] * (full + 1); dp[0] = 0
    for S in range(1, full + 1):
        T = S
        while T:                    # iterate over all non-empty submasks T of S
            if hstart[T] and dp[S ^ T] + 1 < dp[S]:
                dp[S] = dp[S ^ T] + 1
            T = (T - 1) & S
    return dp[full]

def alpha_underlying(out_adj, n):
    if n <= 0:
        return 0
    und = [0] * n
    for a in range(n):
        for b in range(n):
            if out_adj[a] >> b & 1:
                und[a] |= 1 << b; und[b] |= 1 << a
    best = 0
    for S in range(1, 1 << n):
        if all(not (und[v] & S) for v in range(n) if S >> v & 1):
            best = max(best, bin(S).count("1"))
    return best

A6. Bookkeeping (Pattern P2): in any tree on $n \ge 2$ vertices, $\sum_{v \in V} d(v) = 2m = 2n - 2$. Subtract $2n = \sum_{v \in V} 2$ from both sides: $$\sum_{v \in V} (d(v) - 2) = (2n - 2) - 2n = -2.$$ Now group the vertices by degree: each of the $L$ leaves ($d(v) = 1$) contributes $1 - 2 = -1$; each degree-$2$ vertex contributes $2 - 2 = 0$; and vertices of degree $\ge 3$ contribute $d(v) - 2 \ge 1$. Thus $-L + 0 + \sum_{d(v) \ge 3} (d(v) - 2) = -2$, which rearranges immediately to $$L = 2 + \sum_{d(v) \ge 3} (d(v) - 2).$$ Checks: for a path, the sum is empty and $L = 2$; for a star $K_{1,n-1}$ ($n \ge 4$), the center has degree $n - 1$, giving $L = 2 + (n - 1 - 2) = n - 1$; for an unrooted full binary tree with $I$ internal vertices of degree $3$, $L = 2 + I \cdot (3 - 2) = I + 2$ (whereas in a rooted full binary tree, the root has degree $2$ and the other $I - 1$ internal vertices have degree $3$, giving $L = 2 + (I - 1) = I + 1$). Machine check: zero violations across all $16 + 125 + 1296 + 16807$ labeled trees on $4, 5, 6, 7$ vertices.

A7. By the Prüfer dictionary (Lecture 4.4), a degree sequence $(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ with $\sum d_i = 2n - 2$ is realized by $\frac{(n - 2)!}{\prod_{i=1}^n (d_i - 1)!}$ labeled trees. For $n = 6$, $\sum d_i = 10$ with each $d_i \ge 1$, so the partitions of $10 - 6 = 4$ into $6$ parts $(d_i - 1 \ge 0)$ give five degree-multiset families:

  • Stars $\{1,1,1,1,1,5\}$: $\binom{6}{1} = 6$ ways to choose the center, each with $\frac{4!}{4!} = 1$ code $\to 6 \times 1 = \mathbf{6}$.
  • Paths $\{1,1,2,2,2,2\}$: $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ ways to choose the two leaves, each with $\frac{4!}{1!1!1!1!} = 24$ codes $\to 15 \times 24 = \mathbf{360} = \frac{6!}{2}$.

A8. ($\Leftarrow$) Fix a component, root it anywhere at $r$, and color $v$ by $d(r, v) \bmod 2$. Claim: every edge joins opposite colors. If an edge $xy$ joined same-colored vertices, then $d(r,x) + 1 + d(r,y)$ is odd — a closed walk of odd length. Lemma (odd closed walks breed odd cycles): in a closed walk $v_0 \dots v_m$ with $m$ odd, if any internal vertex repeats ($v_i = v_j$ with $0 \le i < j < m$), the walk splits at the repeat into two closed walks of lengths $j - i$ and $m - (j - i)$ whose lengths sum to $m$ — so one of them is odd and strictly shorter. By well-ordering, a shortest odd closed walk has $v_0, v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}$ all distinct; since $m$ is odd and $G$ is simple (no self-loops, ruling out $m = 1$), we have $m \ge 3$, which makes $v_0 v_1 \dots v_m$ a simple odd cycle. That contradicts the hypothesis, so the coloring is proper: bipartite. ($\Rightarrow$) Walk around an odd cycle: each edge demands a color flip, and an odd number of flips cannot return you to your starting color — no $2$-coloring exists. Machine check: on all $1024$ graphs on $5$ vertices, BFS-bipartiteness agrees with brute-force odd-cycle search on every single graph ($376$ bipartite, $648$ carrying an odd cycle — agreement $1024/1024$).

A9. Walk greedily: from the start $s$, keep following unused edges. Where can you get stuck? Only where no unused out-edge remains. Every pass through a vertex $w \ne s$ consumes one in-edge and one out-edge of $w$; being stuck at $w$ means the walk entered $w$ once more than it left, i.e. $\text{used in-edges} \ge \text{used out-edges} + 1$, i.e. $d^-(w) \ge d^+(w) + 1$ — impossible when $\text{in} = \text{out}$. So the walk closes at $s$: you get a closed directed walk. Now Lecture 2.2's chase (directed version) extracts a directed cycle from it; delete that cycle — it removed exactly one in-edge and one out-edge at each of its vertices, so $\text{in} = \text{out}$ survives everywhere; repeat on the rest. Edges are finite; the machine halts with everything partitioned into directed cycles (P18: procedure $=$ proof). A15. The engine (Pattern P2): every edge $e$ of a tree $T$ is a bridge; deleting $e$ splits $T$ into two components of sizes $s_e$ and $n - s_e$, and the unique path between $u$ and $v$ uses $e$ if and only if $u$ and $v$ lie on opposite sides of $e$. Counting pairs by the edges their paths traverse gives the Wiener index: $$W(T) = \sum_{\{u,v\} \subseteq V(T)} d(u,v) = \sum_{e \in E(T)} s_e(n - s_e).$$ The star is the floor: For any integer $s$ with $1 \le s \le n - 1$, we have $$s(n - s) - (n - 1) = (s - 1)(n - 1 - s) \ge 0,$$ so $s_e(n - s_e) \ge n - 1$, with equality if and only if $s_e \in \{1, n - 1\}$ (meaning $e$ is incident to a leaf). Summing over all $n - 1$ edges gives $$W(T) \ge (n - 1)(n - 1) = (n - 1)^2,$$ with equality (for $n \ge 4$) if and only if every edge touches one of $n - 1$ distinct leaves, forcing all $n - 1$ edges to share the remaining vertex as center: the star $K_{1,n-1}$.

The path is the ceiling: Peel a leaf $\ell$ with unique neighbor $p$, leaving the tree $T' = T - \ell$ on $N = n - 1$ vertices. Since distances within $T'$ are unchanged and $d_T(u, \ell) = d_{T'}(u, p) + 1$ for every $u \in V(T')$, we have $$W(T) = W(T') + \sum_{u \in V(T')} \bigl(d_{T'}(u, p) + 1\bigr) = W(T') + N + \sum_{u \in V(T')} d_{T'}(u, p).$$ Sub-lemma: For any tree $T'$ on $N$ vertices and any $p \in V(T')$, $\sum_{u \in V(T')} d_{T'}(u, p) \le \binom{N}{2}$, with equality iff $T'$ is a path with $p$ as an endpoint. Proof: Let $M = \max_{u \in V(T')} d_{T'}(u, p) \le N - 1$, and pick a vertex $z$ at distance $M$ from $p$. Along the unique $p\text{–}z$ path $p = w_0, w_1, \dots, w_M = z$, the $k$ vertices $w_0, w_1, \dots, w_{k-1}$ all lie in the ball of radius $k - 1$ around $p$, so $|\{u \in V(T') : d_{T'}(u, p) \le k - 1\}| \ge k$ for every $1 \le k \le M$. Taking complements inside $V(T')$, at most $N - k$ vertices sit at distance $\ge k$ from $p$: $$\sum_{u \in V(T')} d_{T'}(u, p) = \sum_{k=1}^{M} \bigl|\{u \in V(T') : d_{T'}(u, p) \ge k\}\bigr| \le \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} (N - k) = \frac{N(N - 1)}{2} = \binom{N}{2}.$$ Now induct on $n$ (Pattern P4), with base $n = 1$ ($W = 0 = \frac{1^3 - 1}{6}$). Assuming $W(T') \le \frac{N^3 - N}{6} = \binom{N+1}{3}$, we obtain: $$W(T) \le \frac{N^3 - N}{6} + \frac{N(N - 1)}{2} + N = \frac{N(N^2 - 1) + 3N(N - 1) + 6N}{6} = \frac{N(N^2 + 3N + 2)}{6} = \frac{N(N+1)(N+2)}{6} = \frac{(N+1)^3 - (N+1)}{6} = \frac{n^3 - n}{6},$$ with equality iff $T'$ is a path ($W(T') = \binom{N+1}{3}$) and $p$ is an endpoint of $T'$, so that attaching $\ell$ to $p$ extends $T'$ into a path on $n$ vertices. $\blacksquare$ Machine check: over all labeled trees on $n = 4, 5, 6, 7$ vertices, the minimum is $(n - 1)^2$ ($9, 16, 25, 36$) attained only by stars, and the maximum is $(n^3 - n)/6$ ($10, 20, 35, 56$) attained only by paths.

A16. Existence: Start at any vertex $v$. For any vertex $x$, let $m(x)$ be the maximum number of vertices in a connected component of $T - x$; by definition, $x$ is a centroid iff $m(x) \le n/2$. If $m(v) \le n/2$, we are done. Otherwise, some component $H$ of $T - v$ has $|H| > n/2$; let $u$ be the unique neighbor of $v$ in $H$ and step to $u$. What are the components of $T - u$? One component contains $v$ and consists of $V(T) \setminus V(H)$, which has size $n - |H| < n/2 < |H|$; all other components of $T - u$ are subsets of $V(H) \setminus \{u\}$, so each has size at most $|H| - 1$. Thus $m(u) \le \max(n - |H|, |H| - 1) < |H| = m(v)$. Because $m(x)$ is a positive integer that strictly decreases at each step, the walk cannot continue forever and must halt at a vertex with $m(x) \le n/2$—a centroid (Patterns P8/P18).

Structure and count ($1$ or $2$ adjacent centroids): Suppose $u$ and $v$ are two distinct centroids of $T$. Let $T_v$ be the component of $T - u$ containing $v$, and let $T_u$ be the component of $T - v$ containing $u$. Because the unique $u\text{–}v$ path in $T$ connects $u$ and $v$, every vertex outside $T_v$ (including $u$ itself) reaches $u$ without passing through $v$, so $V(T) \setminus V(T_v) \subseteq V(T_u)$. Moreover, any vertex lying strictly between $u$ and $v$ on the $u\text{–}v$ path belongs to both $T_v$ and $T_u$. Now use the centroid bounds $|T_v| \le n/2$ (since $u$ is a centroid) and $|T_u| \le n/2$ (since $v$ is a centroid): $$\frac{n}{2} = n - \frac{n}{2} \le n - |T_v| = |V(T) \setminus V(T_v)| \le |T_u| \le \frac{n}{2}.$$ Equality must hold throughout! This forces two conclusions at once: first, $V(T) \setminus V(T_v) = V(T_u)$, so $V(T_v) \cap V(T_u) = \emptyset$, meaning no vertex lies strictly between $u$ and $v$—thus $u$ and $v$ are adjacent; second, $|T_v| = |T_u| = n/2$, so the edge $uv$ splits $T$ into two exact halves of size $n/2$ each (which requires $n$ even). Conversely, if an edge $uv$ splits $T$ into two components of size $n/2$, then every component of $T - u$ has size $\le n/2$ and every component of $T - v$ has size $\le n/2$, so both $u$ and $v$ are centroids. Finally, no tree can have three centroids: any three would have to be pairwise adjacent, forming a $K_3$ triangle inside an acyclic graph—impossible. $\blacksquare$ Machine check: across all $18{,}244$ labeled trees on $n = 4\dots 7$, every tree has $1$ or $2$ adjacent centroids, and two centroids occur iff an edge bisects the tree into two $n/2$-vertex halves.

A17. Induction on $n$ (P4). Base: $Q_2$ is the $4$-cycle itself. Step: $Q_n$ is two copies of $Q_{n-1}$ — last bit $0$ and last bit $1$ — joined by the perfect matching $x \leftrightarrow x'$. By induction the $0$-copy has a Hamilton cycle $C$; break any one edge $x\text{–}y$ of $C$, leaving a Hamilton path from $y$ to $x$ through all of the $0$-copy. Now cross the matching $x \to x'$, walk the same cycle backwards through the entire $1$-copy from $x'$ to $y'$, and cross home $y' \to y$. The result is a single cycle visiting every vertex of $Q_n$ exactly once: a Hamilton cycle (copy-internal moves are honest cube edges; the two matching moves flip exactly the last bit). Unrolled, the cycle lists all $2^n$ bitstrings with consecutive strings one bit apart — a Gray code, the same object that keeps shaft encoders from miscounting and Karnaugh maps from scrambling. Machine check: the construction runs and verifies for $n = 2, 3, 4, 5$ — lengths $4, 8, 16, 32$, all vertices distinct, every consecutive pair (including the closing pair) differing in exactly one bit.

A19. The tool is the Prüfer dictionary (Lecture 4.2, Fact 1): vertex $1$ has degree $k$ in a labeled tree $T$ if and only if label $1$ appears in the Prüfer code $(a_1, \dots, a_{n-2})$ exactly $k - 1$ times. How many words in $\{1, \dots, n\}^{n-2}$ contain the letter $1$ exactly $k - 1$ times? Choose which $k - 1$ of the $n - 2$ positions hold the letter $1$ in $\binom{n-2}{k-1}$ ways, and fill each of the remaining $(n - 2) - (k - 1) = n - 1 - k$ positions independently from the $n - 1$ other labels $\{2, \dots, n\}$ in $(n - 1)^{n - 1 - k}$ ways. By the Prüfer bijection, the number of labeled trees with $d(1) = k$ is $$\binom{n - 2}{k - 1}(n - 1)^{n - 1 - k}.$$ Checks: At $k = 1$ (vertex $1$ is a leaf), the formula gives $\binom{n-2}{0}(n - 1)^{n - 2} = \mathbf{(n - 1)^{n - 2}}$ (giving $2, 9, 64, 625, 7776$ for $n = 3\dots 7$). At $k = n - 1$ (the star centered at $1$), it gives $\binom{n-2}{n-2}(n - 1)^0 = 1$. And summing over all possible degrees $k = 1, \dots, n - 1$ (putting $j = k - 1$) invokes the Binomial Theorem: $$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \binom{n - 2}{k - 1} 1^{k-1} (n - 1)^{(n - 2) - (k - 1)} = \bigl(1 + (n - 1)\bigr)^{n - 2} = n^{n - 2},$$ recovering Cayley's formula.

A20. This is Rédei's theorem. Build the listing by insertion: process the players one at a time, always maintaining a directed Hamilton path of those processed. To insert a new player $x$: if $x$ beat the first vertex, prepend $x$; if the last vertex beat $x$, append $x$. Otherwise walk down the current path and let $i$ be the last position whose player beat $x$ — then $x$ beat every player after position $i$ (every pair played, so "did not lose to" means "beat"), in particular the one at $i + 1$. Splice $x$ between positions $i$ and $i + 1$: still a directed path, one vertex longer. Induction finishes: every tournament has a directed Hamilton path. Machine check: all $2^6 = 64$ tournaments on $4$ players and all $2^{10} = 1024$ on $5$ players were enumerated and every single one contains a directed Hamilton path — zero failures (a detector, not a proof; the insertion argument is the proof). Note the contrast with Lecture 5.3: Hamilton cycles are brutally hard; Hamilton paths in tournaments are free — the tournament's completeness is exactly the lubricant the insertion needs.

A22. Both inequalities are constructive — the theorem is a good characterization in miniature. ($\chi$ is at most the minimum.) Fix any orientation $D$ of $G$, and let $\ell(D)$ be the number of vertices on its longest directed path. The tempting proof fails, and it is worth watching it fail before repairing it. Color each vertex $v$ by $f(v) =$ the number of vertices on the longest directed path of $D$ ending at $v$, and hope that an arc $u \to v$ forces $f(v) \ge f(u) + 1$. On the directed triangle $a \to b \to c \to a$ this collapses: every vertex ends a directed path through all three vertices, so $f(a) = f(b) = f(c) = 3$ — adjacent vertices share a color, not a proper coloring at all. The flaw: in an orientation with directed cycles, the longest path ending at $u$ may already pass through $v$, so extending it by $u \to v$ repeats a vertex. Cycles must be handled, not ignored. Throw arcs away until they cannot hurt: let $H$ be an inclusion-maximal spanning acyclic subdigraph of $D$ — keep every vertex, offer the arcs of $D$ one at a time, and skip an arc whenever it would close a directed cycle in what has been built so far. ($H$ is genuinely maximal: an arc skipped at its turn already had its blocking cycle among the arcs present then, and $H$ only grows.) Now color each vertex $v$ by $f(v) =$ the number of vertices on the longest directed path of $H$ ending at $v$, and audit every arc $u \to v$ of $D$ — two cases, both strict. If $u \to v$ belongs to $H$: a longest $H$-path ending at $u$ cannot visit $v$ — if it did, its $v \to \dots \to u$ segment followed by the arc $u \to v$ would close a directed walk, and every closed directed walk hides a directed cycle, which $H$ cannot have. So appending $u \to v$ repeats nothing: $f(v) \ge f(u) + 1 > f(u)$. If $u \to v$ does not belong to $H$: maximality means $H$ already holds a directed path $P$ from $v$ to $u$ — otherwise adding the arc could not have closed a cycle when it was skipped. A longest $H$-path $Q$ ending at $v$ meets $P$ only at $v$: any other shared vertex $w$ would splice $Q$'s $w \to \dots \to v$ segment into $P$'s $v \to \dots \to w$ segment, closing a directed walk — a cycle in $H$, impossible again. So $Q$ followed by $P$ is a genuine directed path ending at $u$, and it is longer than $Q$: $f(u) > f(v)$. Either way $f(u) \ne f(v)$, and every edge of $G$ carries an arc of $D$ one way or the other, so adjacent vertices always get different colors: $f$ is a proper coloring, with values from $1$ up to the longest $H$-path — a path of $D$ — so at most $\ell(D)$ colors are used. Hence $\chi(G) \le \ell(D)$ for every orientation, so $\chi(G)$ is at most the minimum. (On the directed triangle, $H$ keeps two of the three arcs and the colors come out $1, 2, 3$ — proper, using exactly $\ell(D) = 3$ colors.) (The minimum is at most $\chi$.) Take a proper coloring with $\chi$ color classes, numbered $1, \dots, \chi$, and orient every edge from its lower class to its higher. Along any directed path the class numbers strictly increase, so the path visits at most one vertex per class: at most $\chi$ vertices, i.e. $\ell \le \chi$ for this orientation — the minimum over orientations is $\le \chi$. The two inequalities meet: $\chi(G)$ equals the minimum, over all orientations of $G$, of the number of vertices on the longest directed path (Gallai–Hasse–Roy–Vitaver). Notice the family resemblance to Lecture 7: the coloring orientation certifies "some orientation has no long path" exactly the way a cross-cut certifies "few paths suffice" — both directions build the witness, which is what makes the characterization good. Machine check: on all $64$ graphs on $4$ vertices and all $1024$ on $5$ vertices, $\chi$ equals the orientation-minimized longest directed path — zero disagreements; and the repaired coloring itself was audited on every digraph — all $4096$ on $4$ vertices (proper at every arc, both case directions strict, never more than $\ell(D)$ colors) and all $1{,}048{,}576$ on $5$ vertices (proper, strict, zero violations).

A25. We give both the longest-path projection proof (Pattern P8) and the leaf-layer peeling proof (Pattern P18).

Method 1 — Projection onto a longest path: Let $P = v_0 v_1 \dots v_d$ be a longest path in $T$, so its length $d$ is the diameter of $T$. First, for any vertex $u \in V(T)$, let $v_j$ be the unique vertex on $P$ closest to $u$ (the projection of $u$ onto $P$). Claim: $d(u, v_j) \le \min(j, d - j)$. Indeed, if $d(u, v_j) > j$, then the path from $u$ to $v_j$ followed by $v_j v_{j+1} \dots v_d$ would be a simple path of length $d(u, v_j) + (d - j) > j + (d - j) = d$, contradicting that $P$ is a longest path; symmetrically, $d(u, v_j) \le d - j$. From this, the distance from any $v_i \in V(P)$ to any $u \in V(T)$ is bounded by $$d(v_i, u) = |i - j| + d(u, v_j) \le |i - j| + \min(j, d - j) \le \max(i, d - i),$$ with equality achieved at $u = v_0$ ($j = 0$) or $u = v_d$ ($j = d$). Thus every vertex $v_i$ on $P$ has eccentricity $\varepsilon(v_i) = \max(i, d - i)$. Now take any vertex $x \in V(T)$, with projection $v_i$ on $P$. Because the unique paths from $x$ to $v_0$ and from $x$ to $v_d$ both pass through $v_i$, we have $$\varepsilon(x) \ge \max\bigl(d(x, v_0), d(x, v_d)\bigr) = d(x, v_i) + \max(i, d - i) \ge d(x, v_i) + \lceil d/2 \rceil \ge \lceil d/2 \rceil.$$ For $\varepsilon(x)$ to equal the minimum possible value $\lceil d/2 \rceil$, both inequalities must be equalities: $d(x, v_i) = 0$ (so $x = v_i$ lies on $P$) and $\max(i, d - i) = \lceil d/2 \rceil$. When $d$ is even, the unique solution is $i = d/2$, giving the single center vertex $v_{d/2}$. When $d$ is odd, the solutions are $i = (d - 1)/2$ and $i = (d + 1)/2$, giving the two adjacent center vertices $v_{(d-1)/2}$ and $v_{(d+1)/2}$. $\blacksquare$

Method 2 — Simultaneous leaf-peeling: If $n \le 2$, $T$ is $K_1$ or $K_2$ and every vertex is in the center. If $n \ge 3$, let $L$ be the set of all leaves of $T$, and form $T' = T - L$ (which is a non-empty tree, since $n \ge 3$ prevents two leaves from being adjacent to each other). For any $u \in V(T')$, any vertex $z \in V(T)$ at maximum distance $\varepsilon_T(u) \ge 1$ from $u$ must be a leaf of $T$ ($z \in L$), and its unique neighbor $z^-$ lies in $V(T')$, giving $\varepsilon_T(u) = d_T(u, z) = d_{T'}(u, z^-) + 1 \le \varepsilon_{T'}(u) + 1$. Conversely, if $w \in V(T')$ achieves $d_{T'}(u, w) = \varepsilon_{T'}(u)$, then since $w \notin L$ has $d_T(w) \ge 2$, at least one neighbor $y \in V(T)$ of $w$ does not lie on the unique $u\text{–}w$ path, giving $\varepsilon_T(u) \ge d_T(u, y) = \varepsilon_{T'}(u) + 1$. Hence $\varepsilon_T(u) = \varepsilon_{T'}(u) + 1$ for every $u \in V(T')$. Meanwhile, for any leaf $\ell \in L$ with unique neighbor $p \in V(T')$, since $n \ge 3$, the vertex $z$ farthest from $p$ in $T$ cannot be $\ell$ (as $d_T(p) \ge 2$ provides another branch from $p$ of length $\ge 1$), so $\varepsilon_T(\ell) = \varepsilon_T(p) + 1 > \varepsilon_T(p)$. Thus no leaf of $T$ belongs to $\operatorname{Center}(T)$, and on $V(T')$ all eccentricities drop by $1$ in lockstep: $\operatorname{Center}(T) = \operatorname{Center}(T - L)$. Repeating until $\le 2$ vertices remain terminates at $K_1$ (one center vertex) or $K_2$ (two adjacent center vertices). $\blacksquare$

The bound $\delta \ge \lceil (n - 1)/2 \rceil$ is honest (Pattern P11 in reverse): lowering the integer threshold by $1$ to $\delta = \lceil (n - 1)/2 \rceil - 1$ allows both disconnected witnesses—$K_k \sqcup K_{k+1}$ for odd $n = 2k + 1$ ($\delta = k - 1$, e.g. $K_3 \sqcup K_4$ on $n = 7$ with $\delta = 2$) and $K_k \sqcup K_k$ for even $n = 2k$ ($\delta = k - 1$, e.g. $K_3 \sqcup K_3$ on $n = 6$ with $\delta = 2$)—and connected bipartite witnesses $K_{k-1, k+2}$ for odd $n = 2k + 1 \ge 5$ and $K_{k-1, k+1}$ for even $n = 2k \ge 4$, where alternating between the two parts traps any path at length at most $2(k - 1) + 1 = 2k - 1 < n$.
