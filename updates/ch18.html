Chapter 18 · Thresholds and Windows

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

Chapter 13 followed a random graph through its evolution and watched structures appear one after another. This chapter is about the shape of each appearance. A monotone property — connectivity, say, or the appearance of a triangle — is off at $p$, off at $p$, off at $p\dots$ and then, over a vanishingly short interval, it is on, on, on. The interval is the window. Erdős and Rényi saw the windows first (1959–1960); Bollobás and Thomason proved that every monotone property has one (1987); Friedgut and Kalai computed how narrow they must be for graph properties (1996); and Kahn and Kalai proposed — before anyone could prove it — the deepest structural statement in the subject: a threshold is never more than a logarithmic factor away from the first-moment heuristic that a freshman can compute (2006). Park and Pham proved it (2022, published 2024). That theorem, like Chapter 17's nibble, is one of the rare results that upgrades an entire culture of guesswork into theorems: it says that to locate a threshold you may count witnesses, and only the window's width needs deeper machinery.

The motto of the chapter, and of pattern P44: the window is the theorem. The location of a threshold is usually easy — a first moment, a union bound, an obstruction count. The theorem is what the probability does inside the window: whether the transition is coarse (a wide, gradual climb, like the triangle's) or sharp (a cliff, like connectivity's), and how wide the cliff is in units of its own location. This is a phenomenon-pattern in the manner of P26: the object of study is not a counting trick but a law of nature that every monotone system obeys.

Notation. $G(n,p)$ is the random graph on $n$ labeled vertices with each edge present independently with probability $p$; $G(n,m)$ is the uniform graph with exactly $m$ edges; $N = \binom{n}{2}$ throughout. For a monotone (increasing) property $A$ of graphs, $p_c$ is its critical probability: any $p$ with $\mu_p(A) = 1/2$ (the choice of level $1/2$ is a convention; any constant in $(0,1)$ gives the same asymptotics). The $\varepsilon$-window $T_\varepsilon(A)$ is the interval of $p$ over which $\mu_p(A)$ climbs from $\varepsilon$ to $1-\varepsilon$. A property of $n$-vertex graphs has a sharp threshold if $T_\varepsilon/p_c \to 0$ for every fixed $\varepsilon$, and a coarse threshold otherwise. $\Omega = \{0,1\}^E$ is the discrete cube over the edge set; for $x \in \Omega$, $x \cup \{e\}$ and $x \setminus \{e\}$ add or delete edge $e$. An edge $e$ is pivotal for $A$ at state $x$ if $x \setminus \{e\} \notin A$ but $x \cup \{e\} \in A$ (for increasing $A$: absent, and its addition flips membership). $\operatorname{Inf}_e(A, p)$ is the marginal influence: the probability, over a random state $y$ of the other $N-1$ edges (each present independently with probability $p$), that $y \setminus \{e\} \notin A$ while $y \cup \{e\} \in A$ — $e$'s own bit is marginalized out. (This is the convention in which Russo's formula below reads $d\mu/dp = \sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e$ with no extra factor; the older boundary-measure convention differs by the factor $p(1-p)$, and we use the marginal one throughout, matching the machine receipts.) Riemann $p$ vs the process view: in the random graph process, edges arrive in a uniformly random order and $\tau_A$ is the hitting time of $A$. $\mathbb{E}_p[X]$ is expectation under $\mu_p$; w.h.p. means with probability tending to $1$; $\ln$ is the natural logarithm.

Lecture 1 · The Evolution, Reread as a Theorem

The story. Erdős and Rényi began with a question that looks like a pastime: take $n$ vertices, flip a coin of bias $p$ for each of the $N = \binom{n}{2}$ pairs, and watch what happens as $p$ climbs from $0$ to $1$. Their 1959 note "On random graphs I" and the 1960 memoir "On the evolution of random graphs" report the discovery that made random graph theory a subject: structures do not appear gradually. For each structure — first edge, first triangle, first cycle, the giant component, connectivity — there is a specific $p$ at which it switches from almost-never to almost-always, and the switch is fast. Erdős and Rényi wrote thresholds as threshold functions $t(n)$: a property holds w.h.p. once $p/t(n) \to \infty$ and fails w.h.p. once $p/t(n) \to 0$. Sixty years of sharpening have replaced the two-ended function by today's cleaner objects: a critical probability $p_c$ and a window around it.

The setting. A property $A$ of graphs is monotone increasing if adding edges can only help: $G \in A$ and $G \subseteq G'$ implies $G' \in A$. ("Contains a triangle," "connected," and "has a Hamilton cycle" are monotone increasing; by contrast, "3-colorable" is monotone decreasing, so non-3-colorability is the monotone increasing side of that fence, and choosing the increasing orientation is the standard first move.) On the probability space $G(n,p)$, monotonicity makes $\mu_p(A) = \mathbb{P}(G(n,p) \in A)$ a strictly increasing polynomial in $p$ of degree at most $N$ for any nontrivial property (with nonnegative counts $a_m = |A \cap \binom{E}{m}|$ in the Bernstein expansion $\sum_{m=0}^N a_m p^m(1-p)^{N-m}$ and nondecreasing slice densities $a_m/\binom{N}{m}$, hence $\frac{d\mu_p(A)}{dp} > 0$ throughout $(0,1)$).

The first-moment heuristic (P10), stated precisely. Let $X$ count the witnesses of $A$ — the small structures whose presence certifies membership. ("Contains a triangle": $X = \#\text{triangles}$.) Then

First-moment threshold heuristic. If $\mathbb{E}_p[X] \to 0$ then $\mu_p(A) \to 0$; if $\mathbb{E}_p[X] \to \infty$ and the witnesses rarely overlap, $\mu_p(A) \to 1$. The scale $p$ where $\mathbb{E}_p[X] \approx 1$ is the heuristic location of the threshold.

The first half is Chapter 4's union bound (P13): $\mathbb{P}(X \ge 1) \le \mathbb{E}[X]$. The second half is honest only when overlap is negligible — that is exactly the caveat this whole chapter learns to manage, and Lecture 5 will turn it into a theorem.

The triangle, completely. $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{triangles}] = \binom{n}{3}p^3$. Setting the count to $1$ gives $p^3 \approx 6/n^3$, i.e. $p = \Theta(1/n)$, and the refined parametrization $p = c/n$ gives

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \binom{n}{3}\Big(\frac{c}{n}\Big)^3 = \frac{c^3}{6}\Big(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Big)\Big(1 - \frac{2}{n}\Big) \to \frac{c^3}{6}.$$

For fixed small $c$ this is the whole story: $\mathbb{P}(\text{no triangle}) \approx e^{-c^3/6}$ when triangles are rare and nearly independent (Poisson paradigm, P28 — Chapter 13 Lecture 2's machinery; Exercise 3 does the analogous computation for isolated vertices in complete detail). So the climb of $\mu_p$ is the climb of $1 - e^{-c^3/6}$: from $10\%$ to $90\%$ as $c$ runs from $(6\ln(10/9))^{1/3} \approx \mathbf{0.858}$ to $(6\ln 10)^{1/3} \approx \mathbf{2.400}$ — a $c$-window width of $\Delta c \approx 1.541$ in units of $1/n$ (or $T_{0.1}/p_c \approx 1.541/1.608 \approx 0.958$ in units of $p_c$ itself). That is a coarse window: it never narrows.

The obstruction side (P13). For "connected," the useful witnesses live on the other side: $A$'s complement is witnessed by cuts. The cheapest cut is the isolated vertex. $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{isolated vertices}] = n(1-p)^{n-1}$; setting this to $1$:

$$n(1-p)^{n-1} \approx n\cdot e^{-(n-1)p} \approx 1 \implies \mathbf{p \approx \frac{\ln n}{n}}.$$

The union bound's shadow — $\mathbb{P}(\text{not } A) \ge \mathbb{P}(\text{one specific obstruction})$ — is the lower-bound half of a threshold argument, and it is exact here in the strongest sense: at $p = (\ln n + c)/n$, the number of isolated vertices converges to $\operatorname{Poisson}(e^{-c})$ (Exercise 3 proves it from factorial moments), and the classical Erdős–Rényi theorem — hardened by decades of sharpening, with the hitting-time form due to Bollobás and Thomason (1985) — says that at this scale, w.h.p. the isolated vertices are the only obstruction left: $\mu_p(\text{connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}$. The heuristic and the theorem agree to the additive constant in $p$.

The lecture's takeaway. Both thresholds were located by a one-line expectation count (P10) plus a one-line obstruction count (P13). What the heuristics cannot say is how fast the probability climbs — and that is not a detail. It is the subject of the book's newest pattern, P44: the window is the theorem.

Lecture 2 · Windows: Coarse versus Sharp

The definition. For a monotone property $A$ and $0 < \varepsilon < 1/2$, let $p_1 = p_1(\varepsilon)$ be the $p$ with $\mu_p(A) = \varepsilon$ and $p_2$ the $p$ with $\mu_p(A) = 1-\varepsilon$. The $\varepsilon$-window is $T_\varepsilon(A) = p_2 - p_1$: the length of the interval across which the probability actually climbs. A sequence $A_n$ of properties has a sharp threshold if $T_\varepsilon(A_n)/\min(p_c, 1-p_c) \to 0$ for every fixed $\varepsilon$, and a coarse threshold if the ratio stays bounded away from $0$. (For thresholds with $p_c \to 0$ — the random-graph case — $\min(p_c, 1-p_c)$ is just $p_c$: sharpness means the window is negligible in units of the threshold's own location.) This is the definition used by the Kalai–Safra survey of the subject, and it is the right one: it makes "sharp vs coarse" a statement about what an experimenter sees.

Bollobás–Thomason (1987): windows exist. Before 1987, "threshold function" was a property-by-property affair. Bollobás and Thomason proved the universal statement:

Theorem (Bollobás–Thomason). Every nontrivial monotone property of subsets of a finite set has a threshold: the $\varepsilon$-window is bounded — $T_\varepsilon = O(\min(p_c, 1-p_c)\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon))$ (the window bound as normalized in the Kalai–Safra survey; the structural content is what we use) — so for every fixed $\varepsilon$ the window is at most a constant multiple of the threshold's scale.

Proof (elementary replication coupling for coarse existence, and Kruskal–Katona shadow machinery for the sharp $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ dependence). First, to see why every monotone increasing property $A$ has a bounded $\varepsilon$-window $T_\varepsilon(A) = O(p_c)$ for every fixed $\varepsilon \in (0, 1/2)$, take $k$ independent draws $S_1, \dots, S_k \sim \mu_p$ and form their union $S = S_1 \cup \dots \cup S_k$. Each ground-set element fails to appear in $S$ with probability $(1-p)^k$, so $S \sim \mu_q$ with $q = 1 - (1-p)^k \le kp$. Because $A$ is monotone increasing, if $S_i \in A$ for at least one $i$, then $S \in A$; taking complements gives $$1 - \mu_{kp}(A) \le 1 - \mu_q(A) = \mathbb{P}(S \notin A) \le \mathbb{P}(\forall i,\, S_i \notin A) = (1 - \mu_p(A))^k.$$ Applying this first with $p = p_1(\varepsilon)$ (where $\mu_{p_1}(A) = \varepsilon$) and $k_1 = \lceil (\ln 2)/\varepsilon \rceil$ yields $1 - \mu_{k_1 p_1}(A) \le (1-\varepsilon)^{k_1} \le e^{-\varepsilon k_1} \le 1/2$, so $p_c \le k_1 p_1(\varepsilon)$. Applying it again with $p = p_c$ (where $\mu_{p_c}(A) = 1/2$) and $k_2 = \lceil \log_2(1/\varepsilon) \rceil$ gives $1 - \mu_{k_2 p_c}(A) \le 2^{-k_2} \le \varepsilon$, so $p_2(\varepsilon) \le k_2 p_c = \lceil \log_2(1/\varepsilon) \rceil p_c$. To upgrade the lower tail from $p_1(\varepsilon) \ge (\varepsilon/\ln 2) p_c$ to the symmetric $O(\min(p_c, 1-p_c)\log(1/\varepsilon))$ bound, Bollobás and Thomason invoke the Kruskal–Katona theorem on the uniform slices $\mathcal{A}_m = A \cap \binom{E}{m}$: if $|\mathcal{A}_m|/\binom{N}{m} = \binom{x}{m}/\binom{N}{m}$, then by monotonicity the upper shadow keeps $|\mathcal{A}_{m'}|/\binom{N}{m'} \ge \binom{x}{m'}/\binom{N}{m'}$ for all $m' \ge m$, which makes $(1 - \mu_p(A))^{1/p}$ nonincreasing in $p$ and yields $p_2(\varepsilon) - p_1(\varepsilon) = O(\min(p_c, 1-p_c)\log(1/\varepsilon))$. Thus a threshold is not a folk tale; it is a universal theorem that applies to every monotone property at once — which is the structural foundation that the Kahn–Kalai conjecture (Lecture 5) later strengthens. $\blacksquare$

The warm-up: majority. Before graph properties, the cleanest sharp threshold in mathematics is majority voting. Let $f$ be majority on $n$ bits: $A = \text{"more than half the bits are } 1\text{."}$ An individual bit is pivotal when the other $n-1$ bits split exactly evenly, so (for $n$ odd)

$$\operatorname{Inf}_{\text{bit}}(1/2) = \frac{\binom{n-1}{(n-1)/2}}{2^{n-1}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}},$$

and the total influence is $\approx \sqrt{2n/\pi}$ — machine-checked numbers: at $n = 101$ the exact pivotal probability is $0.079589$ per bit, total influence $8.0385$ (against the approximation $8.019$); at $n = 1001$, $0.025225$ per bit, total $25.2502$ (approximation $25.244$). By Lecture 4's Russo formula the climb rate $d\mu/dp$ equals the total influence, so majority's window is $\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})$ — sharp in units of $p_c = 1/2$, and radically sharper than any counting heuristic would suggest: no freshman expectation count produces a $1/\sqrt{n}$. Windows are made of influence, not of expectation.

Lecture 3 · Hitting Times: The Process View

The process. Instead of fixing $p$, run the random graph process: put all $N = \binom{n}{2}$ possible edges in a uniformly random order and add them one at a time. $G(n,m)$ — the $m$-th snapshot — is exactly the uniform graph with $m$ edges, so the process is a way of seeing all the $p$'s at once, in their natural coupling. For a monotone property $A$, the hitting time $\tau_A$ is the first $m$ at which the snapshot lies in $A$. Hitting times restate every threshold question: $G(n,p)$ is essentially $G(n,m)$ at $m \approx pN$, so a threshold location $p_c \leftrightarrow m_c = p_c\cdot N$, and two properties with the same threshold can still have different hitting-time behavior.

The coincidences. The classical results here are so clean they read like accidents:

Theorem (Bollobás–Thomason, 1985; the $k = 1$ case is the Erdős–Rényi phenomenon). In the random graph process, w.h.p. the hitting time of "minimum degree $\ge k$" equals the hitting time of "$k$-connected" — for every fixed $k$. The last isolated vertex disappears and the graph is connected in the same instant; the last degree-$1$ vertex disappears and the graph is $2$-connected in the same instant.
Theorem (Bollobás, 1984). The Hamiltonicity analogue: w.h.p. the first Hamilton cycle appears at exactly the hitting time of "minimum degree $\ge 2$." (The companion edge-count statement — Hamiltonicity once $m \approx (n/2)(\ln n + \ln\ln n)$ — has its sharp threshold in the same paper's circle of results, with the path versions due to Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi (1981) and Komlós–Szemerédi (1983).)

These are much sharper than threshold statements. A threshold says "around $p_c$, connectivity turns on." A hitting-time coincidence says: at the very moment the graph loses its last isolated vertex, it is, right then, already connected — the union of the two events' windows collapses to a point. There is no interval where the graph is "no longer obstructed but not yet connected." The proofs work the ER way: show that at the window, the only possible obstructions are the small-degree vertices (all other cut structures have already been outrun), and that the arrival of the last few edges is oblivious to the boundary. We state the theorems and prove the $k = 1$ case's engine in Exercise 12 (given the Poisson tool from Exercise 3); the full-strength $k \ge 2$ arguments are cited, not reproduced.

Why the process view matters for P44. The window of a property is an artifact of re-randomizing the experiment at each $p$. The process view shows the windows of several properties stacked on one run of Nature: nothing (edges are isolated), trees (components join), the giant component (Ch. 13's phase transition, at $m = N/n$), then — crowded together at the far end — $\delta \ge 1$, connectivity, and (a hair later) the Hamilton era. The theorem that the last two hair-thin windows coincide exactly is the sharpest statement the theory owns, and it is the natural closing image of the chapter: thresholds are cliffs; hitting times are the cliff-edges; the theory's pride is that some cliff-edges coincide to the edge of the last edge.

Lecture 4 · Influences: Russo's Formula and KKL

This lecture builds the machine that measures window width. The idea is the encoding move of P25: a monotone graph property $A$ on $n$ vertices is a monotone Boolean function $f: \{0,1\}^N \to \{0,1\}$ (the input bits say which edges are present), invariant under the $n!$ vertex relabelings. Study $f$ with the tools of the discrete cube.

Pivotality. Edge $e$ is pivotal at state $x$ if flipping just $e$ changes the verdict: $x \setminus \{e\} \notin A$, $x \cup \{e\} \in A$. The influence of $e$ at $p$ is $\operatorname{Inf}_e(p) = \mathbb{P}_p(e\text{ pivotal})$ — the probability, at edge-density $p$, that this particular edge is the one currently holding the door shut.

Russo's formula (Margulis 1974; Russo 1981). For monotone $A$ and $0 < p < 1$:

$$\frac{d\mu_p(A)}{dp} = \sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p) =: I_p(A),\quad\text{the }\mathbf{total\ influence}.$$

Proof (complete). Write $\mu_p(x) = \prod_e \mu_p(x_e)$, where $\mu_p(1) = p$ and $\mu_p(0) = 1-p$ for each edge's bit. Differentiate under the product:

$$\frac{d\mu_p(A)}{dp} = \frac{d}{dp} \sum_x \mathbf{1}[x \in A] \prod_e \mu_p(x_e) = \sum_e \sum_x \mathbf{1}[x\in A]\cdot \mu_p(x)\cdot\Big(\frac{\mathbf{1}[x_e=1]}{p} - \frac{\mathbf{1}[x_e=0]}{1-p}\Big).$$

The inner sum factors: $\sum_{x: x_e=1} \mathbf{1}[x\in A]\mu_p(x)/p = \mathbb{P}_p(A \mid e\text{ present})$, and likewise the subtracted term is $\mathbb{P}_p(A \mid e\text{ absent})$. So

$$\frac{d\mu_p(A)}{dp} = \sum_e \big[ \mathbb{P}_p(A \mid e\text{ present}) - \mathbb{P}_p(A \mid e\text{ absent}) \big].$$

Now fix $e$ and condition on the other $N-1$ edges, drawn as a random $y$ on the remaining bits. By monotonicity, the indicator $\mathbf{1}[x \in A]$ is nondecreasing in the bit of $e$, so the two conditional probabilities differ exactly when $y\cup\{e\} \in A$ and $y\setminus\{e\} \notin A$ — the pivotal event. Hence $\mathbb{P}_p(A\mid e\text{ present}) - \mathbb{P}_p(A\mid e\text{ absent}) = \mathbb{P}_p(e\text{ pivotal}) = \operatorname{Inf}_e(p)$, and summing over $e$ gives the formula. $\blacksquare$ (Exercise 6 makes you run the whole computation on a $3$-bit example by hand; the $10$-edge machine check below certifies it exactly, including the integer identity at $p = 1/2$.)

The cleanest way to feel the formula: $d\mu/dp$ is the climb rate; the only states where raising $p$ locally does anything are the pivotal ones; each pivotal configuration of the other edges contributes its own probability. The window width is then read off: $\mu$ climbs from $\varepsilon$ to $1-\varepsilon$ while $dp \cdot (\text{total influence})$ accumulates, so $T_\varepsilon \approx (1-2\varepsilon)/I_{p_c}$ up to constants — wide windows mean small total influence, sharp windows mean large total influence.

KKL: not all influences can be small. Russo turns window width into a question about total influence. The theorem that powers the entire sharp-threshold culture:

Theorem (Kahn–Kalai–Linial, 1988). For any Boolean function $f$ on $m$ bits with $\mu_{1/2}(f) = 1/2$, some variable has influence at least $K\cdot(\log m)/m$ — a constant $K > 0$ independent of everything. (Stated in its uniform-measure form; the $p$-biased extension is due to Bourgain, Kahn, Kalai, Katznelson and Linial.)

The proof uses hypercontractive Fourier analysis on the discrete cube (Bonami–Beckner inequality on the Walsh–Fourier expansion of $\partial_e f$) — the spectral method of Chapter 8 applied to $\{0,1\}^m$. What the Atlas proves completely is the consequence that matters for graphs:

Equal influences (complete). If $A$ is a graph property (invariant under vertex relabeling), then all $N$ edge-influences are equal: the group $S_n$ acts on vertices, hence on edges, and transitively (any edge maps to any edge); membership in $A$ is invariant; pivotality is preserved by the action; so $\operatorname{Inf}_e(p)$ is constant in $e$. $\blacksquare$ (Three lines — Exercise 7.)

Theorem (Friedgut–Kalai, 1996). Every monotone graph property has a sharp threshold at any critical probability bounded away from $0$ and $1$: $T_\varepsilon(A) \le C\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log n$.

The derivation, complete given KKL (Exercise 8 makes you run it). By equal influences and Russo, $d\mu/dp = N\cdot \operatorname{Inf}_e(p)$. KKL's $p$-biased form at density $p \in [a, 1-a]$ gives $\operatorname{Inf}_e(p) \ge K\cdot\mu(1-\mu)\cdot\log N/N$ with $N = \binom{n}{2}$ — so $d\mu/dp \ge K'\cdot\log n\cdot\mu(1-\mu)$. Solve the differential equation: $d\mu/(\mu(1-\mu)) \ge K'\log n\,dp$, so $\log(\mu/(1-\mu))$ climbs at rate $\ge K'\log n$; the log-odds must travel from $\log(\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon))$ to its negative, a total of $2\log((1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)$, which costs at most $(2/K')\cdot\log((1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)/\log n$ of $dp$. Hence $T_\varepsilon \le C\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log n$. $\blacksquare$

The theorem is strikingly strong: at $p$ bounded away from $0$ and $1$, no monotone graph property can dawdle — $3$-colorability's failure, chromatic number crossing $k$, any symmetric question: the window is $O(1/\log n)$ wide, period. Its hypotheses matter and the next paragraph says what happens without them.

The wider landscape (statements, with sources). Margulis (1974) had already given a sharpness criterion for random subgraphs of highly connected graphs, and had the Russo formula early. Talagrand (1994) strengthened KKL to a sum-of-influences bound: under the marginal influence convention, $\sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p) \log\!\big(\frac{e}{p(1-p)\operatorname{Inf}_e(p)}\big) \ge \frac{K\,\mu_p(A)(1-\mu_p(A))}{p(1-p)\log(2/[p(1-p)])}$, so for $p \in [a, 1-a]$, $\sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p) \ge \frac{K'\,\mu_p(A)(1-\mu_p(A))}{\log(e/\max_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p))}$. Friedgut (1999) — the paper with Bourgain's appendix — proved the dichotomy: at $p$ bounded away from the endpoints, a monotone function with a coarse threshold is close to a junta — it genuinely depends on only a bounded number of coordinates (the property is approximable by a local rule). For graph properties, which are symmetric, "local rule" is a devastating restriction — hence the sharpness. This is the machinery behind the $k$-SAT sharpness in Lecture 6.

✎ Checkpoint. Now answer Lecture 2's question honestly: why is "$\delta \ge 1$" sharp? Compute the exact edge influence and total influence at the window $p = (\ln n + c)/n$. A specific edge $e = \{u,v\}$ is pivotal for $A = \{\delta \ge 1\}$ at state $y$ of the other $N-1$ edges if and only if no vertex in $V \setminus \{u,v\}$ is isolated in $y$, and at least one of $u, v$ has zero incident edges in $y$ while neither remains isolated once $e$ is added — that is, either $u$ is isolated in $y$, or $v$ is isolated in $y$, or both. Since $u$ and $v$ each have $n-2$ other potential incident edges, the probability that at least one of $u,v$ has no edges in $y$ is $2(1-p)^{n-2} - (1-p)^{2n-4} = (2+o(1))e^{-c}/n$, while the conditional probability that no vertex in $V \setminus \{u,v\}$ is isolated is $(1+o(1))e^{-e^{-c}}$. Therefore each individual edge has marginal influence $$\operatorname{Inf}_e(p) = (2+o(1))\frac{e^{-c}e^{-e^{-c}}}{n^2},$$ and summing across all $N = \binom{n}{2} = (1+o(1))n^2/2$ edges gives the total influence $$I_p(A) = N \cdot \operatorname{Inf}_e(p) = (1+o(1))\, n\, e^{-c} e^{-e^{-c}} = \Theta(n),$$ matching Russo's derivative $\frac{d}{dp}\big(e^{-e^{-c}}\big) = n \frac{d}{dc}\big(e^{-e^{-c}}\big) = n e^{-c} e^{-e^{-c}}$ — far more than majority's $\Theta(\sqrt{n})$, handing us the window width $\Theta(1/n)$. Two concrete exercises sit behind the curtain: Exercise 5 derives majority's window by Chebyshev alone, and Exploration 17 connects influence spread to coarse vs. sharp voting rules.

Lecture 5 · The Expectation Threshold: Kahn–Kalai to Park–Pham

The heuristic, institutionalized. Lecture 1's first-moment heuristic says: count witnesses, set $\mathbb{E} = 1$, and that is where the threshold should be. It fails in both directions, and the failure modes are the story of the subject. When witnesses overlap heavily the heuristic undershoots (connectivity: witnesses are spanning trees, and $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{spanning trees}] = 1$ already at $p \approx 1/n$ — a factor $\ln n$ below the truth). When the witness structure is self-reinforcing it overshoots in spirit (the $3$-core of Lecture 6: no witness count predicts it at all). Kahn and Kalai (2006) proposed the statement that tames the first failure mode forever:

Definitions (Kahn–Kalai). For an increasing family $A$ of subsets of a finite ground set, let $\mathcal{H}$ be the family of minimal witnesses (inclusion-minimal members of $A$), $\ell = \max\{|W| : W \in \mathcal{H}\}$, and define the expectation threshold $q = q_A$ by $\mathbb{E}_p[\#\{W \in \mathcal{H} : W \subseteq S_p\}] = 1$, where $S_p$ is the $p$-random subset. ($\mathbb{E}[\#\text{minimal witnesses contained}] = 1$.)

The Kahn–Kalai conjecture (2006; the Park–Pham theorem, 2022/2024). There is a universal constant $K$ such that for every increasing family, $p_c \le K\cdot q\cdot\log \ell$.

Note what this says: whatever the true threshold is, the freshman first-moment heuristic is off by at most a logarithmic factor in the maximum witness size $\ell$ — a quantity you can read directly off the problem statement. Proving it took sixteen years. Jinyoung Park and Huy Tuan Pham announced the proof in April 2022, published in the Journal of the American Mathematical Society 37 (2024) 235–243 (arXiv:2203.17207). Their proof proceeds by downward induction on the maximum witness size $\ell$ in at most $\lceil \log_2 \ell \rceil$ fragmentation rounds: instead of asking whether a single $p$-random set covers an entire witness $S \in \mathcal{H}$, one samples a sprinkle $W_1 \sim \mu_{C q}$ and looks for the smallest uncovered residual fragment $S \setminus W_1$ as $S$ ranges over $\mathcal{H}$. When $\mathcal{H}$ is $q$-spread (every nonempty edge subset $I$ is contained in at most $q^{|I|}$ hyperedges of $\mathcal{H}$, connected to the expectation threshold by Talagrand's fractional-expectation duality), a double-counting and second-moment argument on pairs $(S, W_1)$ shows that either $W_1$ already leaves a witness fragment of size $< \ell/2$ with probability $1 - o(1)$, or one can pass to a new $q$-spread hypergraph $\mathcal{H}'$ of residual fragments whose maximum size is at most $\ell/2$. Repeating this halving step $\lceil \log_2 \ell \rceil$ times with independent sprinkles $W_t \sim \mu_{C q}$ uses total edge probability at most $1 - (1 - Cq)^{\lceil \log_2 \ell \rceil} \le K q \log \ell$ and reduces the residual witness size to $1$, completing the proof. What the theorem changed is quotable in one line: locating a threshold is now, universally, a first-moment calculation up to a log factor; the only thing that can be hard about it is the window.

The showcase: connectivity, completely. Minimal witnesses of connectivity are the spanning trees (Ch. 2's Prüfer world: a minimal connected spanning subgraph is a tree), so $\ell = n-1$ and — by Cayley's formula and the independence of edges under $\mu_p$ — $\mathbb{E}_p[\#\text{spanning trees}] = n^{n-2}p^{n-1}$. Setting the count to $1$:

$$q = n^{-(n-2)/(n-1)} = n^{-1}\cdot n^{1/(n-1)} = \frac{1}{n}\cdot e^{(\ln n)/(n-1)} \approx \frac{1}{n}\Big(1 + \frac{\ln n}{n}\Big) \approx \mathbf{\frac{1}{n}}.$$

The true threshold (Exercise 3 + the ER theorem): $p_c = (\ln n + c^*)/n$ with $c^* = -\ln\ln 2 \approx \mathbf{0.3665}$ the point where $e^{-e^{-c}} = 1/2$. So

$p_c/q \approx \ln n$ — and $\ln \ell = \ln(n-1) \approx \ln n$: the ratio $p_c/(q\cdot\ln \ell)$ tends to $1$. The conjecture's log factor is not an artifact; it is exactly what the simplest example needs.

What the conjecture does not say — the honest boundary. Park–Pham bounds $p_c$ above by $K\cdot q\cdot\log \ell$. It does not say $p_c \ge q$ (indeed $q$ can exceed $p_c$, as the triangle shows, though $p_c \ge q/2$ always holds). It does not locate windows — a threshold can be log-far from $q$ and still coarse. And it does not apply to properties whose witness structure is not the point at all: the $3$-core's threshold (Lecture 6) is governed by a self-reinforcing bootstrap, not by minimal witnesses — the Kahn–Kalai bound still holds there (witnesses exist, after all), but it is loose: the lesson of the catalog is that there are two kinds of thresholds — witness-countable and bootstrap — and the second kind needs its own theory.

The phenomenon-pattern, reflected. P26 (Chapter 13's phase transition) names what one sees in the evolution; P44 names the law every monotone system obeys. The catalog shows the law's reach: coarse when witnesses are local and unbalanced (small subgraphs, stars), sharp when a global constraint fails all at once (connectivity, coloring, $k$-cores, SAT), bounded by first moments up to one logarithm (Park–Pham), and — the frontier — understood at exactly the scale where the window, not the location, is the hard part. The chapter's last word is the same as its first: the window is the theorem.

The Trick Bag (reference card)

The 12-row formula box.

  1. First-moment location (P10). Count minimal witnesses $W \in \mathcal{H}$; the expectation threshold scale is where $\mathbb{E}_p[\#\text{witnesses}] = \sum_{W \in \mathcal{H}} p^{|W|} \approx 1$ (for a single isomorphism type $H$, $(\#\text{copies of } H)\cdot p^{e(H)} = 1$). Triangle: $\binom{n}{3}p^3 = 1 \implies p = 6^{1/3}/n \approx 1.817/n$.
  2. Obstruction location (P13). For the failure side, count the cheapest obstruction. Isolated vertices: $n(1-p)^{n-1} = 1 \implies p = (\ln n)/n$. The obstruction count is a lower bound on $p_c$; the witness count an upper bound proxy; when they agree up to constants you have located the threshold.
  3. Poisson curve (P28). At the window, rare weakly-dependent witnesses go to $\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$: $\mathbb{P}(\ge 1\text{ triangle}) \to 1 - e^{-c^3/6}$; $\mathbb{P}(\ge 1\text{ isolated vertex}) \to 1 - e^{-e^{-c}}$; so $\mathbb{P}(\text{connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}$ — the whole transition curve, not just its location.
  4. Window definition. $T_\varepsilon = p_2(\varepsilon) - p_1(\varepsilon)$; sharp $\iff T_\varepsilon/p_c \to 0$; coarse otherwise. Bollobás–Thomason: $T_\varepsilon = O(\min(p_c,1-p_c)\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon))$ — windows exist, universally.
  5. Russo's formula. $d\mu_p/dp = \sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p)$ (marginal influences) — the climb rate is the total influence. Proof: product rule + monotonicity turns conditional differences into pivotal events. Window $\approx (1-2\varepsilon)/I_{p_c}$.
  6. KKL. Some variable has influence $\ge K\cdot\mu(1-\mu)\cdot\log m/m$. Symmetric property $\implies$ all influences equal $\implies$ total influence $\ge K\cdot\mu(1-\mu)\cdot\log m$.
  7. Friedgut–Kalai window. Graph property, $p \in [a, 1-a]$: $T_\varepsilon \le C\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log n$. Derivation: Russo + KKL + solve $d\mu/dp \ge K\log n\,\mu(1-\mu)$ (the logit travels at rate $\ge K\log n$).
  8. Majority calibration. $\operatorname{Inf}_{\text{bit}}(1/2) = \binom{n-1}{(n-1)/2}/2^{n-1} \approx \sqrt{2/(\pi n)}$; total $\approx \sqrt{2n/\pi}$ ($n = 101$: $8.0385$ exact); window $\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})$. The influence scale sets the window scale.
  9. Expectation threshold (Kahn–Kalai). $q =$ where $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{minimal witnesses}] = 1$; $\ell =$ max minimal-witness size. Connectivity: $q = n^{-(n-2)/(n-1)} \approx 1/n$ via Cayley, $p_c \approx (\ln n + 0.3665)/n$, ratio $\approx \ln \ell$.
  10. Park–Pham. $p_c \le K\cdot q\cdot\log \ell$, universally (JAMS 37 (2024) 235–243). Locating a threshold = a first-moment calculation up to one logarithm; only the window can be hard.
  11. Bootstrap thresholds (P4 cascade). $k$-cores: peel (delete degree $< k$, iterate); threshold = fixed point of the survival curve, $c_3 \approx 3.3509$ (PSW); emergence jumps in size (nonempty $\implies$ linear-size). No witness count sees it.
  12. Hitting times. $\tau(\delta \ge k) = \tau(k\text{-connected})$ w.h.p. (Bollobás–Thomason 1985); $\tau(\text{Hamilton}) = \tau(\delta \ge 2)$ w.h.p. (Bollobás 1984). Windows that coincide to the edge.

Answers to the Exercises

A1. A cherry is witnessed by its $2$ edges at a common vertex: $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{cherries}] = n\cdot\binom{n-1}{2}\cdot p^2 \sim n^3 p^2/2$, so the threshold scale is $p = \sqrt{2}\cdot n^{-3/2}$, and with $p = c\cdot n^{-3/2}$ we get $\mathbb{E} \to c^2/2$. The Poisson paradigm gives the full curve $\mathbb{P}(\ge 1\text{ cherry}) \to 1 - e^{-c^2/2}$: from $10\%$ to $90\%$ as $c$ runs from $\sqrt{2\ln(10/9)} \approx 0.459$ to $\sqrt{2\ln 10} \approx 2.146$ — a relative window of $O(1)$: coarse. The location is exactly the balanced-density prediction $n^{-1/\rho}$, $\rho(K_{1,2}) = \max(1/2, 2/3) = 2/3$.

A2. $\mu_p = p$, so $p_c = 1/2$ and $T_\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon) - \varepsilon = \mathbf{1 - 2\varepsilon}$: the window is a constant fraction of $p_c$ — coarse, the widest a window can be relative to its location. Marginal influence of $e_0$: whatever the other edges do, $y \cup \{e_0\} \in A$ and $y \setminus \{e_0\} \notin A$, so $\operatorname{Inf}_{e_0}(p) = 1$ at every $p$ (and Russo reads $d\mu/dp = 1$ ✓); every other edge has influence $0$. No contradiction with Bollobás–Thomason: the bound $T_\varepsilon = O(\min(p_c,1-p_c)\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)) = O((1/2)\log(1/\varepsilon))$ is satisfied with room to spare — the theorem promises every monotone property a window, never a narrow one. The example is the calibration point: one edge's influence concentrated entirely in one bit is the coarse extreme; majority (influence spread across $n$ bits) is the sharp one.

A3. Choose the $k$ isolated vertices: $(n)_k$ ways; the event kills all $k(n-k) + \binom{k}{2}$ incident edges, so $\mathbb{E}[(X)_k] = (n)_k(1-p)^{k(n-k)+\binom{k}{2}}$. At $p = (\ln n + c)/n$: $(1-p)^{k(n-k)+\binom{k}{2}} = \exp(-p\cdot k(n-k) + O(pn\cdot p\cdot k)) = \exp(-k(\ln n + c)(1 - k/n) + o(1))$, and $(n)_k \sim n^k$, so $\mathbb{E}[(X)_k] \to n^k\cdot n^{-k(1-k/n)}\cdot e^{-kc(1+o(1))} \to \mathbf{e^{-kc}}$. Poisson is determined by its factorial moments (Chapter 13 Lecture 2), so $X \to \operatorname{Poisson}(e^{-c})$ and $\mathbb{P}(\delta \ge 1) = \mathbb{P}(X = 0) \to \mathbf{e^{-e^{-c}}}$. One sentence for the upgrade: connectivity fails only through components, the Erdős–Rényi/Bollobás–Thomason component census says that at this window every non-giant component is w.h.p. a single isolated vertex, and the Poisson curve for the isolated vertices therefore is the connectivity curve — $\mu_p(\text{connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}$.

A4. $\mathbb{E}[\#\text{3-stars}] = n\binom{n-1}{3}p^3 \sim n^4 p^3/6 = 1$ gives $p = 6^{1/3} n^{-4/3} \approx 1.817\,n^{-4/3}$. The balanced density $\rho(K_{1,3}) = \max(1/2, 2/3, 3/4) = 3/4$ gives $n^{-1/\rho} = n^{-4/3}$ — exactly this: the star does not ignore the theory; the theory's max-over-subgraphs already knows a star is internally balanced at its full $3$-edge, $4$-vertex scale, which makes its threshold earlier than the triangle's. The contrast: "every vertex has degree $\ge 3$" lives at $p = (\ln n + 2\ln\ln n)/n$ — polynomially and logarithmically later — and is sharp, because now all $n$ vertices must succeed together: the failure probability near the threshold is governed by the count of vertices of degree $\le 2$ ($\mathbb{E}[\#\{v : \deg(v)=2\}] \sim n\frac{(np)^2}{2}e^{-np} = \frac{1}{2}e^{-c}(1+o(1))$ at $np = \ln n + 2\ln\ln n + c$), and the climb happens as these rare local failures vanish within a window of width $\Theta(1/n)$. Existence thresholds ($\exists$ a vertex) are first-moment and coarse; universality thresholds ($\forall$ vertices) are obstruction-counted and sharp. Same $3$ edges, opposite quantifier, different universe.

A5. Let $X \sim \operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ count the number of ones. Since $\operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \le n/4$, if $p = 1/2 - t/\sqrt{n}$ with $t > 0$, then $\mathbb{E}[X] = n/2 - t\sqrt{n}$ and Chebyshev's inequality gives $$\mu_p(A) = \mathbb{P}(X \ge n/2) = \mathbb{P}(X - \mathbb{E}[X] \ge t\sqrt{n}) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{t^2 n} \le \frac{n/4}{t^2 n} = \frac{1}{4t^2}.$$ Symmetrically, at $p = 1/2 + t/\sqrt{n}$, $\mathbb{P}(X < n/2) \le 1/(4t^2)$. Taking $t = 1/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ gives $\mu_{p_1}(A) \le \varepsilon$ at $p_1 = 1/2 - 1/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon n})$ and $\mu_{p_2}(A) \ge 1 - \varepsilon$ at $p_2 = 1/2 + 1/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon n})$, so the entire $\varepsilon$-climb occurs inside $[1/2 - 1/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon n}),\, 1/2 + 1/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon n})]$ — a window of width $T_\varepsilon(A) \le 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon n} = O(1/\sqrt{n})$ by Chebyshev alone. Where Chebyshev stops: it provides no matching $\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})$ lower bound on the window width and does not determine the transition profile. The central limit theorem supplies both: since $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} = (\sqrt{n}/2)(1 + o(1))$, at $p = 1/2 + t/\sqrt{n}$ the standardized threshold sits at $-2t$, so $\mu_p(A) = \mathbb{P}(X > n/2) \to \Phi(2t)$, pinning the exact window width at $\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})$ and revealing the Gaussian error-function profile inside it. (For finite-$n$ error bounds one invokes Berry–Esseen; the lab's Part 4 machinery is the exact finite analogue of this computation.)

A6. $\mu_p = \binom{3}{2}p^2(1-p) + p^3 = 3p^2 - 2p^3$, so $d\mu/dp = 6p - 6p^2 = 6p(1-p)$. Edge $e$ is pivotal exactly when the other two bits split — one present, one absent: two pivotal configurations $y$, each of measure $p(1-p)$, so $\operatorname{Inf}_e(p) = 2p(1-p)$ (marginal convention: $e$'s own bit integrated out), and $\sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e = 6p(1-p)$ — equal to $d\mu/dp$, as Russo promises. Reconciliation with the conditioning proof: $\mathbb{P}(A \mid e = 1) = \mathbb{P}(\text{the other two are not both absent}) = 1 - (1-p)^2 = 2p - p^2$; $\mathbb{P}(A \mid e = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\text{both others present}) = p^2$; the difference is $2p(1-p) = \operatorname{Inf}_e$ — the conditional-difference is the marginal pivotal probability, which is the whole proof of Russo in one example.

A7. Let $\pi$ be a vertex relabeling with $\pi(e) = f$ (exists for every pair $e, f$ because $S_n$ acts transitively on edges — that is where transitivity enters, and only there). Since $\pi$ permutes edges and $A$ is relabeling-invariant: $x \in A \iff \pi(x) \in A$; moreover $(x \cup \{e\}) \mapsto \pi(x) \cup \{f\}$ and $(x \setminus \{e\}) \mapsto \pi(x) \setminus \{f\}$. So $e$ pivotal at $x \iff f$ pivotal at $\pi(x)$. The pushforward of $\mu_p$ under $\pi$ is $\mu_p$ (the product measure is exchangeable under edge permutations), so $\mathbb{P}(e\text{ pivotal}) = \mathbb{P}(f\text{ pivotal})$. $\blacksquare$ Three lines, one hypothesis: symmetry. It is worth saying what breaks without symmetry — Exercise 2's single-edge property has one influence equal to $1$ and the rest $0$, and its window is correspondingly as coarse as windows get.

A8. By Russo's formula (marginal convention) and equal influences (Exercise 7), $\frac{d\mu_p}{dp} = \sum_e \operatorname{Inf}_e(p) = N \cdot \operatorname{Inf}_e(p)$. For $n \ge 3$, $N = \binom{n}{2} \ge n$, so the $p$-biased KKL bound at $p \in [a, 1-a]$ gives $\operatorname{Inf}_e(p) \ge K\,\mu_p(1-\mu_p)\frac{\log N}{N}$ and hence $\frac{d\mu_p}{dp} \ge K\,\mu_p(1-\mu_p)\log N \ge K\,\mu_p(1-\mu_p)\log n$. Divide by $\mu_p(1-\mu_p)$ and integrate $p$ from $p_1(\varepsilon)$ to $p_2(\varepsilon)$ (where $\mu_p$ climbs from $\varepsilon$ to $1-\varepsilon$): $$\int_\varepsilon^{1-\varepsilon} \frac{d\mu}{\mu(1-\mu)} = \Big[\ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\Big]_\varepsilon^{1-\varepsilon} = 2\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} < 2\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.$$ Hence $T_\varepsilon(A) = p_2(\varepsilon) - p_1(\varepsilon) \le \frac{2}{K}\frac{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}{\log n} = \mathbf{C\,\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log n}}$. $\blacksquare$ The coordinate that climbs at least linearly in $p\log n$ is the logit $\ln(\mu/(1-\mu))$ — which is why sharp-threshold proofs via influence always end with a logit integration.

A9. Each labeled spanning tree is present with probability $p^{n-1}$, independently of the others, and there are $n^{n-2}$ of them (Cayley, Ch. 2), so $\mathbb{E}_p[\#\text{spanning trees}] = n^{n-2}p^{n-1}$. Setting it to $1$: $q = n^{-(n-2)/(n-1)} = n^{-1}\cdot n^{1/(n-1)} = (1/n)e^{(\ln n)/(n-1)} \approx \mathbf{(1/n)(1 + (\ln n)/n)}$. Lower bound: a connected graph contains at least one spanning tree, so the indicator of connectivity is $\le$ the count of spanning trees as random variables: $\mu_p(A) \le \mathbb{E}_p[\#\text{ST}]$. At $p = q\cdot 2^{-1/(n-1)}$ the expectation is exactly $1/2$, so $\mu \le 1/2$ there, giving $p_c \ge q\cdot 2^{-1/(n-1)} \approx q$ — but the upper comparison fails by $\ln n$, which is the whole Kahn–Kalai phenomenon. For the triangle (Exercise 11) the indicator-below-count gives $\mu(\text{triangle}) \le \mathbb{E}[\#\text{triangles}]$, and both are $\approx \mathbb{E}$ — the count is already the answer there; no lower-bound gap ever appears, and none is needed.

A10. The $1/2$-point of $e^{-e^{-c}}$ sits at $e^{-c} = \ln 2$, i.e. $c^* = -\ln\ln 2 \approx \mathbf{0.36651}$ — so $p_c = (\ln n + c^* + o(1))/n$ by Exercise 3's curve plus the ER component census. Meanwhile $q\cdot\ln \ell = n^{-(n-2)/(n-1)}\cdot\ln(n-1) = (\ln(n-1)/n)(1 + (\ln n)/(n-1) + O((\ln n)^2/n^2))$, and $p_c/(q\cdot\ln \ell) = (\ln n + c^* + o(1))/\ln(n-1)\cdot(1 + o(1)) \to \mathbf{1}$. Tightness: any universal bound $p_c \le K\cdot q\cdot(\log \ell)^{1-\varepsilon}$ would give, for this one family, $\ln n \le K(\ln n)^{1-\varepsilon}$ — false for large $n$. So the exponent $1$ on the logarithm cannot be improved: the Kahn–Kalai log factor is exactly what the simplest example in the book demands. (The receipts of lab Part 5 watch this ratio settle at $0.91$–$0.99$ for $n = 12\dots 32$.)

A11. For $q$: $\binom{n}{3} q^3 = 1 \implies q = 6^{1/3} n^{-1}(1 + o(1)) \approx 1.817/n$, with witness size $\ell = 3$. For $p_c$: $1 - e^{-c^3/6} = 1/2 \implies c^3 = 6\ln 2 \implies p_c = (6\ln 2)^{1/3} n^{-1}(1 + o(1)) \approx 1.608/n$. So $p_c/q \to (\ln 2)^{1/3} \approx 0.8850 < 1$ — the true threshold sits strictly below the expectation threshold. No log gap appears because the overlap caveat of the first-moment heuristic is vacuous here: two distinct triangles share an edge if and only if their union is a $4$-vertex diamond $K_4^-$ ($5$ edges), and at $p = c/n$ the expected number of such overlapping pairs is $6\binom{n}{4} p^5 = \Theta(n^4 \cdot n^{-5}) = \Theta(1/n) \to 0$. Thus the witness indicators are asymptotically uncorrelated, and $\mathbb{P}(X \ge 1) \to 1 - e^{-\mathbb{E}[X]}$ is already exact to leading order. Where witnesses overlap heavily (spanning trees, where any two can share up to $n-2$ edges), the heuristic needs the $\ln \ell$ correction. The pair Exercise 9 + Exercise 11 is the Kahn–Kalai philosophy in two worked examples.

A12. First, verify the component-census bound (b) at $p = (\ln n + c)/n$: any connected component of order $k$ ($2 \le k \le n/2$) contains a spanning tree ($k^{k-2}$ choices by Cayley's formula) and has no edges to the remaining $n-k$ vertices. By the union bound, the expected number of such components is at most $$\sum_{k=2}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \binom{n}{k} k^{k-2} p^{k-1} (1-p)^{k(n-k)} \le \sum_{k=2}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \Big(\frac{en}{k}\Big)^k k^{k-2} \Big(\frac{\ln n + c}{n}\Big)^{k-1} e^{-k(n-k)(\ln n + c)/n}.$$ For $2 \le k \le (\ln n)^2$, $(n-k)/n = 1 - O((\ln n)^2/n)$, so the $k=2$ term is $O(n^2 \cdot \frac{\ln n}{n} \cdot n^{-2}) = O(\frac{\ln n}{n})$ and the sum over $2 \le k \le (\ln n)^2$ is $O(\frac{\ln n}{n})$. For $(\ln n)^2 < k \le n/2$, since $n-k \ge n/2$, each term is at most $n \cdot (e(\ln n + c) n^{-1/2} e^{-c/2})^k = o(n^{-2})$. Hence the expected number of components of order $2 \le k \le n/2$ is $O((\ln n)/n) \to 0$. Now choose $\omega = \omega(n) \to \infty$ slowly (say $\omega = \ln\ln n$) and set $m_- = \lfloor (n/2)(\ln n - \omega) \rfloor$ and $m_+ = \lceil (n/2)(\ln n + \omega) \rceil$. By Exercise 3's Poisson law, $\mathbb{P}(m_- \le \tau(\delta \ge 1) \le m_+) \to 1$, and at $m_-$ the graph $G(n, m_-)$ already consists w.h.p. of one giant component of order $> n/2$ together with isolated vertices (no components of order $2 \le k \le n/2$). At $m_-$, the number of isolated vertices is $I \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(e^\omega)$, so w.h.p. $I \le 2e^\omega = 2\ln\ln n$. Each subsequent edge that touches an isolated vertex chooses its other endpoint uniformly from the $n-1$ remaining vertices, so the conditional probability that it joins two isolated vertices rather than hitting the giant component of size $n - I$ is at most $\frac{I-1}{n-1} \le \frac{2\ln\ln n}{n-1}$. Across the at most $I \le 2\ln\ln n$ absorptions needed to eliminate all isolated vertices, the union bound shows the probability that any two isolated vertices ever merge with each other is at most $\frac{(2\ln\ln n)^2}{n-1} \to 0$. Thus every isolated vertex surviving at $m_-$ attaches directly to the giant component, and at $m^* = \tau(\delta \ge 1)$ the graph is a single connected component: $\mathbb{P}(\tau(\text{conn}) = \tau(\delta \ge 1)) \to 1$. $\blacksquare$ The full Bollobás–Thomason theorem upgrades this to every fixed $k$ and to the $2$-connected/$\delta\ge 2$ pairing by running the same argument one obstruction level deeper; the Hamiltonicity version (Bollobás 1984) is the same philosophy with a much harder "no other obstruction" census — the Pósa rotation machinery of Ch. 2 doing its random-graph side job.

A14 (◆). For decreasing $A$, $\mu_p$ falls; define the window on the fall ($p_1 =$ the $1-\varepsilon$ level, $p_2 = \varepsilon$). The trap in the ranking was the fourth item: "contains a spanning tree" sounds like a witness-counting property with threshold $q \approx 1/n$ (Ex 9), but its true window lives at $(\ln n)/n$ — the ranking question quietly re-derives the Kahn–Kalai gap, which is why it earned its ◆.

A16 (◐). What you should find: the crossing point drifts down toward $\lambda_3 \approx 3.3509$ as $n$ grows (finite-$n$ shifts the apparent crossing up at small $n$ — the PSW constant is an $n \to \infty$ fixed-point statement), while the emergence-size jump does not soften: whenever the $3$-core exists at moderate $c$ it already holds a positive fraction of the vertices. The explanation to reach for: peeling is a bootstrap — the deletion cascade is self-reinforcing below threshold (each deletion triggers more) and self-limiting above it; thresholds of bootstrap type are sharp in the location and discontinuous in the size, and no expectation count sees either, because minimal witnesses of every size $v$ contribute expectations at incompatible scales. Contrast the lab's Part 5: connectivity's threshold was first-moment-correctable up to one log; the $3$-core's is not even that.

A17 (◐). Majority: window $\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})$ — sharp, and the sharpest a symmetric rule can be at $p = 1/2$, because its influence is maximally spread (KKL is tight for it). Dictatorship ("voter $1$ decides"): $\mu = p$ — window $1 - 2\varepsilon$, constant: the coarse extreme; all influence concentrated in one bit. A rule wider than majority: the dictatorship is the clean example (any junta works — a rule depending on few voters has a window of constant order; Friedgut's junta theorem is the converse statement: only juntas have coarse windows at $p$ away from $0$ and $1$). The general law to state: an election is sharp exactly when influence is spread — and Condorcet's jury theorem is majority's window statement avant la lettre: competence $p > 1/2$ fixed, error probability $e^{-\Omega(n)}$: not a window statement but its infinite-sharpness limit. The Kalai–Safra survey opens with exactly this table and is the right next read.

A18 (◐). The hunt fails at fixed $\varepsilon$, and that failure is the lesson: for any fixed $\varepsilon \in (0, 1/2)$, Bollobás–Thomason caps the window at $T_\varepsilon(A) = O(\min(p_c, 1-p_c)\log(1/\varepsilon))$ for every monotone property, and Friedgut–Kalai sharpens this for symmetric graph properties at $p_c \in [a, 1-a]$ to $T_\varepsilon(A) \le C \log(1/\varepsilon)/\log n$. Thus no monotone property can have a window wider than its threshold scale at fixed $\varepsilon$. What changes when $\varepsilon = \varepsilon(n) \to 0$: the $\log(1/\varepsilon(n))$ factor grows with $n$ and the cap loosens — for $\varepsilon(n) = e^{-\sqrt{n}}$, the Bollobás–Thomason bound permits a window of width $O(\min(p_c, 1-p_c)\sqrt{n})$ — and the meaning of the window at vanishing $\varepsilon$ becomes the modern structural question. For the giant component, the structural critical window $p - 1/n = \Theta(n^{-4/3})$, inside which the largest components have order $\Theta(n^{2/3})$, is precisely a statement about a moving scale where the macroscopic emergence is still undecided. That marks the boundary of P44: the fixed-$\varepsilon$ window theory is governed by Bollobás–Thomason (existence), Friedgut–Kalai (sharpness), and Kahn–Kalai/Park–Pham (location), while scaling windows at $n$-dependent tails belong to the fine phase-transition theory of Chapter 13.
