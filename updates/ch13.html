Notation. $G(n, p)$ is the random graph on vertex set $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$, each of the $\binom{n}{2}$ possible edges present independently with probability $p$. For a fixed graph $H$ with at least one edge, $m(H) = \max_{F \subseteq H,\, e(F) \ge 1} e(F)/v(F)$ (which for any $H$ containing a cycle equals the maximum over subgraphs with $v(F) \ge 3$) — the density that governs $H$'s appearance threshold $n^{-1/m(H)}$. An up-set is a family $A$ of subsets of $[n]$ such that if $S \in A$ and $S \subseteq T \subseteq [n]$, then $T \in A$. $\chi, \alpha, \omega$ are the chromatic number, independence number, clique number. $\mathbb{P}(\triangle)$ is the probability a random graph contains a triangle. Convergence is as $n \to \infty$; "almost surely" (a.a.s.) means with probability tending to $1$. $e$ is the base of natural logarithms — it will be doing real work in Lecture 6, where it arrives on the side of the count rather than the probability.

Lecture 1 · Correlation: Knowing One Thing Teaches You Another

Chapter 4's coins were honest but cold: each bad event was charged at face value by the union bound, and when events overlapped too much, the Local Lemma stepped in with a budget ($e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$). But something obvious was missing from that whole account. In a random graph, "contains a triangle" and "contains a four-cycle" are both increasing events — more edges can only help them. Intuition says they should lean together: a graph generous enough to host a triangle is a graph hosting other things too. The union bound can never say this (it only bounds intersections from above, pessimistically). This lecture builds the tool that says it precisely, in three increasing levels of generality: Harris–Kleitman for the cube, FKG for lattices with non-product measures, and the Ahlswede–Daykin four-functions theorem that contains both.

The Harris–Kleitman lemma. Let $A$ and $B$ be up-sets of subsets of $[n] = \{1, \dots, n\}$. Then

$$|A \cap B| \cdot 2^n \ge |A| \cdot |B|.$$

In probability language (a uniformly random subset $S$ of $[n]$): $\mathbb{P}(S \in A \text{ and } S \in B) \ge \mathbb{P}(S \in A) \cdot \mathbb{P}(S \in B)$ — increasing events are positively correlated. Kleitman's proof (1966) is an induction the Atlas will hand to you as Exercise 1, because the final line is a two-factor product worth doing in ink; the engine is that an up-set's two coordinate slices satisfy $A_0 \subseteq A_1$ (adding the last element can only help membership), so both slice-differences are nonnegative and their product finishes the induction. $\blacksquare$ (A forerunner for decreasing families appears in Harris's 1960 percolation work; Kleitman's paper is the sharp combinatorial version.)

The gap is $+0.0931$: nearly a third of the product, added back by correlation. (And with both events increasing, the lemma predicted the sign before any counting: positive, always.)

The census underneath the lemma itself: all up-set pairs on the $3$-cube — $20$ up-sets (the Dedekind number $D(3) = 20$, machine-verified), hence $400$ ordered pairs — and all up-set pairs on the $4$-cube — $168$ up-sets ($D(4) = 168$), hence $28,224$ ordered pairs: zero violations of $|A \cap B| \cdot 2^n \ge |A||B|$ in either census. Reversed for one-up-one-down pairs ($A$ increasing, $B$ decreasing), the inequality flips, and the census confirmed the flip with zero exceptions there too. Kleitman's lemma is the kind of statement that is true everywhere it applies — and the machine has now checked every place it applies, twice.

FKG: the lattice upgrade. The cube $\{0,1\}^n$ is a special lattice — meet $\wedge =$ intersection, join $\vee =$ union — with a product measure. Fortuin, Kasteleyn and Ginibre (1971) saw that both conditions can be relaxed. FKG inequality: let $L$ be a finite distributive lattice and $\mu$ a probability measure on it satisfying the lattice condition

$$\mu(x \wedge y) \cdot \mu(x \vee y) \ge \mu(x) \cdot \mu(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y$$

($\mu$ is log-supermodular). Then for increasing functions $f, g$: $\mathbb{E}[fg] \ge \mathbb{E}[f] \cdot \mathbb{E}[g]$ under $\mu$. Increasing events are the $0$–$1$ case, and the conclusion reads $\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) \ge \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)$: positive correlation survives far from product measure. Product measures satisfy the lattice condition with equality (each factor splits across the coordinates where $x, y$ agree); the theorem's value is that other measures do too.

The Atlas's machine-checked example of a genuinely non-product FKG measure is the rank-flattened measure on the cube: fix $N \ge 1$ and $0 < q < 1$, and set $\mu(A) = \frac{1}{Z_N(q)} \frac{q^{|A|}}{\binom{N}{|A|}}$ where $Z_N(q) = \sum_{k=0}^N q^k = \frac{1 - q^{N+1}}{1 - q}$ — weight $\propto q^{\text{rank}}$, spread evenly within each rank. Because the normalization factor $Z_N(q)^{-2}$ cancels from both sides of $\mu(A \cap B)\mu(A \cup B) \ge \mu(A)\mu(B)$, log-supermodularity is a binomial identity in disguise: for sets $A, B \subseteq [N]$ with $|A| = a$, $|B| = b$, $|A \cup B| = u$, $|A \cap B| = i$ (so $a + b = u + i$), the lattice condition reads

$$\binom{N}{a}\binom{N}{b} \ge \binom{N}{i}\binom{N}{u},$$

which is a classical log-concavity fact about binomial coefficients. The machine verified it directly — all $87,380$ ordered pairs of subsets for $N = 1$ through $8$ (every pair, diagonals included), $q = 0.37$: zero violations. An honesty note the Atlas records because it cost a design iteration: the first draft flattened the other way, $\mu(A) = \binom{N}{|A|}\cdot q^{|A|}$ (weight by the count of same-rank peers) — and that measure is log-submodular, $68,210$ violations, the inequality runs the wrong way. Flattening must divide by the rank size, not multiply. The machine caught the error before any prose was written; the chapter keeps the scar as a checkpoint of its own process.

The four-functions theorem. The engine room is Ahlswede and Daykin's 1978 theorem, which subsumes Harris–Kleitman, FKG, and Holley's 1974 comparison theorem in one statement. Call it the AD inequality: for nonnegative functions $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ on a finite distributive lattice $L$ satisfying

$$\alpha(x) \cdot \beta(y) \le \gamma(x \wedge y) \cdot \delta(x \vee y) \quad \text{for all } x, y \in L,$$

it follows that the same inequality holds after summation: for all subsets $A, B \subseteq L$,

$$\alpha(A) \cdot \beta(B) \le \gamma(A \wedge B) \cdot \delta(A \vee B),$$

where $\alpha(A) = \sum_{x \in A} \alpha(x)$ and $A \wedge B = \{x \wedge y : x \in A, y \in B\}$, $A \vee B$ likewise.

Proof of the Ahlswede–Daykin theorem and the FKG inequality. By Birkhoff's representation theorem, every finite distributive lattice $L$ embeds as a sublattice (closed under $\wedge = \cap$ and $\vee = \cup$) of the Boolean cube $2^{[n]}$. Extending $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ by zero outside $L$ preserves the hypothesis $\alpha(x)\beta(y) \le \gamma(x \wedge y)\delta(x \vee y)$ for all $x, y \in 2^{[n]}$, and replacing $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ by $\alpha \mathbf{1}_A, \beta \mathbf{1}_B, \gamma \mathbf{1}_{A \wedge B}, \delta \mathbf{1}_{A \vee B}$ reduces the subset inequality to the global summation bound $\alpha(2^{[n]})\beta(2^{[n]}) \le \gamma(2^{[n]})\delta(2^{[n]})$, which we prove by induction on $n \ge 1$.

Base case ($n = 1$). The lattice is $\{0, 1\}$. Write $\alpha_i = \alpha(i)$ for $i \in \{0, 1\}$, and similarly for $\beta, \gamma, \delta$. We must show $(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1)(\beta_0 + \beta_1) \le (\gamma_0 + \gamma_1)(\delta_0 + \delta_1)$ given the four pointwise inequalities: $$\alpha_0 \beta_0 \le \gamma_0 \delta_0, \quad \alpha_1 \beta_1 \le \gamma_1 \delta_1, \quad \alpha_0 \beta_1 \le \gamma_0 \delta_1, \quad \alpha_1 \beta_0 \le \gamma_0 \delta_1.$$ If $\gamma_0 = 0$ or $\delta_1 = 0$, then $\alpha_0 \beta_1 = \alpha_1 \beta_0 = 0$, so $(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1)(\beta_0 + \beta_1) = \alpha_0 \beta_0 + \alpha_1 \beta_1 \le \gamma_0 \delta_0 + \gamma_1 \delta_1 \le (\gamma_0 + \gamma_1)(\delta_0 + \delta_1)$. Otherwise, $\gamma_0 \delta_1 > 0$. Setting $u = \alpha_0 \beta_1 \le \gamma_0 \delta_1$ and $v = \alpha_1 \beta_0 \le \gamma_0 \delta_1$, we have $uv = (\alpha_0 \beta_0)(\alpha_1 \beta_1) \le (\gamma_0 \delta_0)(\gamma_1 \delta_1)$. Since $(\gamma_0 \delta_1 - u)(\gamma_0 \delta_1 - v) \ge 0$, we obtain $$\alpha_0 \beta_1 + \alpha_1 \beta_0 = u + v \le \gamma_0 \delta_1 + \frac{uv}{\gamma_0 \delta_1} \le \gamma_0 \delta_1 + \frac{\gamma_0 \delta_0 \gamma_1 \delta_1}{\gamma_0 \delta_1} = \gamma_0 \delta_1 + \gamma_1 \delta_0.$$ Adding $\alpha_0 \beta_0 \le \gamma_0 \delta_0$ and $\alpha_1 \beta_1 \le \gamma_1 \delta_1$ yields $(\alpha_0+\alpha_1)(\beta_0+\beta_1) \le (\gamma_0+\gamma_1)(\delta_0+\delta_1)$.

Inductive step ($n - 1 \to n$). For each $S \subseteq [n-1]$, define the projected functions on $2^{[n-1]}$ by summing over the last coordinate $n$: $$\alpha'(S) = \alpha(S) + \alpha(S \cup \{n\}),$$ and analogously for $\beta', \gamma', \delta'$. For any fixed $S, T \subseteq [n-1]$, restricted to the two-point fibres $\{S, S \cup \{n\}\}$ and $\{T, T \cup \{n\}\}$, the four values $\alpha(S), \alpha(S \cup \{n\})$ and $\beta(T), \beta(T \cup \{n\})$ satisfy the $n = 1$ hypothesis against $\gamma(S \cap T), \gamma((S \cap T) \cup \{n\})$ and $\delta(S \cup T), \delta((S \cup T) \cup \{n\})$. By the $n = 1$ base case, $\alpha'(S)\beta'(T) \le \gamma'(S \cap T)\delta'(S \cup T)$ for all $S, T \subseteq [n-1]$. Applying the induction hypothesis on $2^{[n-1]}$ gives $\alpha'(2^{[n-1]})\beta'(2^{[n-1]}) \le \gamma'(2^{[n-1]})\delta'(2^{[n-1]})$, completing the induction.

Deduction of FKG. Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $L$ satisfying $\mu(x)\mu(y) \le \mu(x \wedge y)\mu(x \vee y)$, and let $f, g : L \to \mathbb{R}$ be increasing functions. Shifting $f$ and $g$ by constants does not change $\operatorname{Cov}_\mu(f, g) = \mathbb{E}[fg] - \mathbb{E}[f]\mathbb{E}[g]$, so we may assume $f, g \ge 0$. Any nonnegative increasing function $f$ on a finite lattice decomposes as a positive linear combination $f = \sum_{k} c_k \mathbf{1}_{U_k}$ where $c_k > 0$ and each $U_k = \{x \in L : f(x) \ge t_k\}$ is an up-set. By bilinearity of covariance, it suffices to prove $\mu(A \cap B)\mu(L) \ge \mu(A)\mu(B)$ for up-sets $A, B \subseteq L$. Set $\alpha = \mu \mathbf{1}_A$, $\beta = \mu \mathbf{1}_B$, $\gamma = \mu$, and $\delta = \mu \mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}$. If $x \in A$ and $y \in B$, then because $A$ and $B$ are up-sets and $x \vee y \ge x, y$, we have $x \vee y \in A \cap B$, so $$\alpha(x)\beta(y) = \mu(x)\mu(y) \le \mu(x \wedge y)\mu(x \vee y) = \gamma(x \wedge y)\delta(x \vee y)$$ (and if $x \notin A$ or $y \notin B$, the left-hand side is $0$). Applying the Ahlswede–Daykin theorem over $L$ gives $\mu(A)\mu(B) = \alpha(L)\beta(L) \le \gamma(L)\delta(L) = \mu(L)\mu(A \cap B) = \mu(A \cap B)$. $\blacksquare$

The machine ran the factory floor: $3,000$ constructed hypothesis-holding quadruples on a $2 \times 3$ grid lattice — zero conclusion violations — and FKG's conclusion under the non-product rank-flattened measure on the $4$-cube, all $28,224$ up-set pairs, zero violations.

Why a probabilist cares. The union bound caps $\mathbb{P}(\bigcup \text{bad})$ from above; FKG floors $\mathbb{P}(\bigcap \text{good})$ from below — for increasing good events $A_1, \dots, A_r$ in a product space, iterating the lemma gives $\mathbb{P}(\bigcap A_i) \ge \prod \mathbb{P}(A_i)$. When a proof needs "the random graph simultaneously avoids every one of several decreasing bad events, with room to spare," this is the line to quote — it is the union bound's mirror image, and Act II will lean on it in the exercises (Exercise 3 makes you run the lattice condition yourself).

What you now own: positive correlation as a theorem, not an intuition (Harris–Kleitman); the lattice condition that licenses it beyond product measure (FKG), with a machine-certified non-product example; the four-functions factory (Ahlswede–Daykin); and a reflex — when increasing events must co-occur, multiply the probabilities and call the result a floor, not an estimate.

Lecture 2 · Janson's Inequality, and the Poisson Paradigm

Chapter 4's Lecture 6 closed on a flag: at the triangle threshold $p = 1/n$, the expected count $\mathbb{E}[X] \to 1/6$, and the truth (asserted, not proved) was that $X$ converges to a $\operatorname{Poisson}(1/6)$ variable, so $\mathbb{P}(\text{no triangle}) \to e^{-1/6}$. This lecture pays that debt in full: a bound from below (Janson), a method of moments from above (the Poisson paradigm), and the machine's eye-witness account — $300$-sample censuses at $n = 60$ and $120$, plus the bound's long slide at $n = 100, 1000$, and $10000$.

Janson's inequality. Let $X = \sum_{i \in I} I_i$ be a sum of indicator variables of increasing events $B_i$ in a product space with dependency graph $\Gamma$ ($I_i$ independent of $\{I_j : j \notin \Gamma^+(i)\}$). Write $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i \in I} p_i$, let $\Delta_{\text{ord}} = \sum_{i \sim j} \mathbb{E}[I_i I_j]$ be the sum over ordered dependent pairs, and let

$$\Delta = \sum_{\{i, j\} \in E(\Gamma)} \mathbb{E}[I_i I_j] = \frac{\Delta_{\text{ord}}}{2}$$

be the sum over unordered pairs $\{i, j\}$ joined in $\Gamma$ — the pairs that cannot be treated as independent. Then:

$$\mathbb{P}(X = 0) \le \exp\!\left(-\mu + \frac{\Delta_{\text{ord}}}{2}\right) = \exp(-\mu + \Delta).$$

(Convention note on the factor of $1/2$: In Alon–Spencer's ordered-pair notation $\Delta_{\text{ord}}$, the repair term is written $+\Delta_{\text{ord}}/2$; in our unordered-pair notation $\Delta$, $\Delta_{\text{ord}}/2$ is already $\Delta$. The lab and numerical receipts below also track the half-weighted proxy $\exp(-\mu + \Delta/2)$, which for $\Delta \to 0$ differs from $\exp(-\mu + \Delta)$ by $O(\Delta) = O(1/n)$ and shares the exact same threshold behavior.)

Proof of Janson's inequality (Boppana–Spencer FKG proof). Order the indices $1, \dots, m$ and write $A_i = \overline{B_i} = \{I_i = 0\}$ for the decreasing event that $I_i$ fails. By the chain rule of conditional probability, $$\mathbb{P}(X = 0) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m A_i\right) = \prod_{i=1}^m \mathbb{P}\!\left(A_i \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{j < i} A_j\right) = \prod_{i=1}^m \left(1 - \mathbb{P}\!\left(B_i \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{j < i} A_j\right)\right).$$ To bound $\mathbb{P}(X = 0)$ from above, we bound $\mathbb{P}(B_i \mid \bigcap_{j < i} A_j)$ from below. Partition $\{j < i\}$ into $N(i) = \{j < i : j \sim i \text{ in } \Gamma\}$ and $M(i) = \{j < i : j \not\sim i \text{ in } \Gamma\}$, and set $D_i = \bigcap_{j \in N(i)} A_j$ and $E_i = \bigcap_{j \in M(i)} A_j$. Because $D_i \cap E_i \subseteq E_i$, we have $\mathbb{P}(D_i \cap E_i) \le \mathbb{P}(E_i)$, so $$\mathbb{P}\!\left(B_i \;\middle|\; D_i \cap E_i\right) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B_i \cap D_i \cap E_i)}{\mathbb{P}(D_i \cap E_i)} \ge \frac{\mathbb{P}(B_i \cap D_i \cap E_i)}{\mathbb{P}(E_i)} = \mathbb{P}(B_i \mid E_i) \cdot \mathbb{P}(D_i \mid B_i \cap E_i).$$ By definition of the dependency graph $\Gamma$, the event $B_i$ depends only on its coin support $S_i$, which is disjoint from the coin supports of all events in $M(i)$. Hence $B_i$ is independent of $E_i$, giving $\mathbb{P}(B_i \mid E_i) = \mathbb{P}(B_i) = p_i$. Moreover, conditioning on $B_i$ fixes all coins in $S_i$ to $1$ while leaving the coins outside $S_i$ with their original independent product measure. On the product space of coins outside $S_i$, the restricted event $D_i\big|_{S_i \equiv 1}$ and the event $E_i$ are both decreasing events. Applying the FKG inequality on the coins outside $S_i$ gives $$\mathbb{P}(D_i \mid B_i \cap E_i) \ge \mathbb{P}(D_i \mid B_i) = \mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigcap_{j \in N(i)} A_j \;\middle|\; B_i\right) \ge 1 - \sum_{j \in N(i)} \mathbb{P}(B_j \mid B_i)$$ by the union bound. Multiplying by $p_i = \mathbb{P}(B_i)$ yields $$\mathbb{P}\!\left(B_i \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{j < i} A_j\right) \ge p_i - \sum_{j \in N(i)} \mathbb{P}(B_i \cap B_j) = p_i - \sum_{\substack{j < i \\ j \sim i}} \mathbb{E}[I_i I_j].$$ Using $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ for each $i = 1, \dots, m$ and multiplying over $i$ gives $$\mathbb{P}(X = 0) \le \exp\!\left(-\sum_{i=1}^m p_i + \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{\substack{j < i \\ j \sim i}} \mathbb{E}[I_i I_j]\right) = \exp(-\mu + \Delta) = \exp\!\left(-\mu + \frac{\Delta_{\text{ord}}}{2}\right). \quad \blacksquare$$

(The fuller statement, including the lower-tail version $\mathbb{P}(X \le \mu - t) \le \exp(-t^2/(2(\mu+\Delta_{\text{ord}})))$, lives in Janson–Łuczak–Ruciński's 2000 monograph. A sanity check the chapter runs by machine in the lab: for the birthday problem — $m = 23$ people, $X =$ matching-birthday pairs — $\mu = 253/365 \approx 0.6932$ and $\Delta = \binom{23}{3}\cdot 3/365^2 \approx 0.0399$, so $\exp(-\mu + \Delta) \approx 0.520$ and the half-weighted proxy $\exp(-\mu + \Delta/2) \approx 0.510$ against the true $\approx 0.493$: a real theorem, tight enough to feel.)

✎ Checkpoint. Triangles in $G(n, p)$: the dependency graph joins two triangle-indicators exactly when the triangles share an edge (sharing a vertex but no edge means disjoint edge sets — independence; Exercise 10 makes you prove it, and the $n = 6$ receipts below machine-check the calibration). Compute $\mu$ and $\Delta$ for $p = 1/2$, $n = 100$ — predict whether Janson's bound will be useful there, before reading the numbers.

Here are the two regimes, machine-computed. Dense: at $G(100, 1/2)$, $\mu = \binom{100}{3}/8 = 20,212.5$ and $\Delta = \binom{100}{2}\cdot \binom{98}{2}/2^5 = 735,229.7$ — so $\Delta/2 = 367,614.8$ dwarfs $\mu$ and the bound exponent $-\mu + \Delta/2$ is wildly positive: Janson is vacuous in the dense regime, exactly as your checkpoint predicted if you were honest. The union bound's successor inherits its blind spot at high density — and says so. Sparse: at $p = 1/n$, $\mu = \binom{n}{3}p^3 \to 1/6$ while $\Delta = \binom{n}{2}\binom{n-2}{2}p^5 \to 0$ (asymptotically $c^5/(4n)$ with $c = 1$). The bound at $n = 100, 1000, 10000$: $0.851698 / 0.847010 / 0.846535$ (and $\exp(-\mu + \Delta) = 0.852700 / 0.847115 / 0.846546$), closing down on $e^{-1/6} = 0.846482$ like a hand tightening on the answer. The repair bill shrinks with $n$; the bound becomes sharp.

The Poisson paradigm. A bound from above on $\mathbb{P}(X = 0)$ is half a theorem. The other half: $X$ actually converges to a Poisson. The method of moments does it cleanly, because Poisson laws are pinned by their factorial moments: a variable with $\mathbb{E}[(X)_r] = \lambda^r$ for every fixed $r$ is $\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$ in the limit (the Poisson paradigm). For triangles at $p = c/n$ the bookkeeping is Exercise 10's spine, and its two combinatorial identities were machine-verified to the last pair before being printed here: at $n = 6$, all $190$ unordered triangle-pairs classify as $90$ edge-sharing (the $\Delta$ term, probability $p^5$ for the pair), $90$ vertex-sharing (disjoint edge sets — independent, $p^6$, the same currency as fully disjoint pairs), and $10$ disjoint, with $2$-edge-sharing pairs impossible; ordered: $180$ edge-sharing, $200$ edge-disjoint, $380$ total, confirming $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] = 200p^6 + 180p^5$ at $n = 6$ — and at $n = 1000$, $c = 1$, the exact factorial moment $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] = 0.02810798$ against $\mu^2 = 0.02761147$, a ratio of $1.018$ sliding to $1$. Every fixed $r$ runs the same race, so $\mathbb{E}[(X)_r] \to (c^3/6)^r$, and:

The triangle count in $G(n, c/n)$ converges to $\operatorname{Poisson}(c^3/6)$. In particular $\mathbb{P}(\text{no triangle}) \to e^{-c^3/6}$. At $c = 1$: $e^{-1/6} \approx 0.846$ — Chapter 4's flag, paid in full. Lecture 3 · Martingales: The Exposure That Cannot Run Away

Chapter 4's second moment proved that the triangle count concentrates. But some variables resist second moments entirely — the chromatic number $\chi$, for one, whose exact distribution is a wall. For those, the probabilistic method has a different engine, and it is about information, not size: reveal the random object gradually, and if knowing one more piece can only move your conditional expectation a little, then the final answer cannot be far from the initial one. That is the whole idea, and it wears two costumes.

Doob's exposure martingales. Take any random object — a graph, an assignment, a coloring — and reveal it in stages: $Y_0, Y_1, \dots, Y_n$ where $Y_i$ is the conditional expectation of the final score after the first $i$ pieces are revealed. The sequence $Y_i$ is a martingale: $\mathbb{E}[Y_{i+1} \mid Y_0, \dots, Y_i] = Y_i$ — each step is fair, given everything seen. Two exposures matter for graphs:

  • Edge exposure: reveal the $\binom{n}{2}$ coins one edge at a time; $Y_i = \mathbb{E}[\chi(G) \mid \text{first } i \text{ edges known}]$.
  • Vertex exposure: reveal all edges touching vertex $1$, then vertex $2$, and so on; $Y_i = \mathbb{E}[\chi(G) \mid \text{the first } i \text{ vertices' edges known}]$.

Azuma–Hoeffding. If a martingale satisfies the Lipschitz condition $|Y_{i+1} - Y_i| \le c_i$ always, then

$$\mathbb{P}(|Y_n - Y_0| \ge t) \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sum c_i^2}\right).$$

Fairness plus bounded steps forbids straying — a gambler who cannot bet more than a dollar a hand cannot end the night a thousand dollars up by luck.

Proof of the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality. Set $D_i = Y_i - Y_{i-1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, so $\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid \mathcal{F}_{i-1}] = 0$ and $|D_i| \le c_i$ almost surely. For any $\lambda > 0$ and any $x \in [-c_i, c_i]$, writing $x = \frac{c_i + x}{2c_i} c_i + \frac{c_i - x}{2c_i}(-c_i)$ and applying the convexity of $x \mapsto e^{\lambda x}$ on $[-c_i, c_i]$ gives $$e^{\lambda x} \le \frac{c_i + x}{2c_i} e^{\lambda c_i} + \frac{c_i - x}{2c_i} e^{-\lambda c_i} = \cosh(\lambda c_i) + \frac{x}{c_i}\sinh(\lambda c_i).$$ Taking conditional expectations given $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$ and using $\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid \mathcal{F}_{i-1}] = 0$ together with the Taylor bound $\cosh(u) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{u^{2k}}{(2k)!} \le \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{u^{2k}}{2^k k!} = e^{u^2/2}$ yields $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda D_i} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right] \le \cosh(\lambda c_i) \le \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2 c_i^2}{2}\right).$$ By induction on $n$ via the tower law of conditional expectation, $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(Y_n - Y_0)}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(Y_{n-1}-Y_0)} \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda D_n} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]\right] \le \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2\right).$$ Applying Markov's inequality to $e^{\lambda(Y_n - Y_0)}$ and choosing the optimal parameter $\lambda = t \big/ \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2$ gives $$\mathbb{P}(Y_n - Y_0 \ge t) \le e^{-\lambda t} \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}\right).$$ Applying the same bound to the negated martingale $-Y_i$ bounds the lower tail $\mathbb{P}(Y_n - Y_0 \le -t)$ identically, and union-bounding the two tails yields $2\exp\!\left(-t^2 \big/ \left(2\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2\right)\right)$. $\blacksquare$

The Lipschitz condition is where combinatorics enters, and the Atlas certified both natural versions by exhaustive census before printing: flipping one edge changes $\chi$ by at most $1$, and deleting one vertex changes $\chi$ by at most $1$. The census — all graphs on $n = 2$ through $5$ vertices, every edge ($5,325$ checks) and every vertex deletion ($5,404$ checks), zero violations, with hand-checks on $C_5$ and the Petersen graph clean — is the small-scale license for the two workhorse corollaries:

Edge-exposure: $|Y_{i+1} - Y_i| \le 1$, so $\chi(G(n,p))$ is within $t$ of its mean except with probability $2\exp(-t^2/(2\binom{n}{2}))$ — a window of order $n$ at the natural scale $t \approx \sqrt{2\binom{n}{2}} \approx n$. Vertex-exposure (the Shamir–Spencer form): revealing a whole vertex can move $\chi$ by more than an edge does, but the total is bounded by the vertex's potential effect, giving the cleaner window $\sqrt{n-1}$: $\mathbb{P}(|\chi - \mathbb{E}\chi| > \lambda\sqrt{n-1}) < 2e^{-\lambda^2/2}$ for $G(n,p)$, any $p$ — Shamir and Spencer's 1987 line of attack.

Lecture 4 · The Evolution of a Random Graph: A Life Story in Phases

Fix $p = c/n$ and let $n$ grow. The random graph is now a process — an organism that develops as $c$ ramps from $0$ to $\infty$ — and the probabilistic method's Act II toolkit reads its whole biography. This lecture is the biography, and every quantitative claim in it was run before printing.

Act I of the organism ($c < 1$): subcritical. The expected degree is $c$. Components are trees or unicyclic, all small: the largest component is of order $\log n$, an honest fraction of $0$. The machine at $n = 1000$, $c = 0.5$ ($20$ samples): mean largest fraction $0.0112$ — no giant, exactly as the theory demands, and worth a checkpoint of its own: $\mathbb{P}(\text{a fixed vertex is isolated}) = (1-p)^{n-1} \to e^{-c}$, so at $c = 0.5$ an honest $e^{-0.5} \approx 0.607$ of the vertices stand alone; the machine's $20$ samples give $0.6082$. The $e^{-c}$ law is doing the bookkeeping for the whole subcritical era.

✎ Checkpoint. The critical point. The giant component, when it emerges, has fraction $\rho(c)$ solving a fixed-point equation you can derive with a one-line first-moment argument (Exercise 11 hands you the derivation): $\rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho}$, the fraction of a $\operatorname{Poisson}(c)$ branching process that survives. Predict $\rho(2)$ from a one-minute hand iteration — then read on.

Act II ($c > 1$): the giant. At $c = 1.5, 2, 3$ the equation gives $\rho =$ $0.583, 0.797, 0.940$ (by iteration; $\rho = 0$ is always a root, and for $c > 1$ the nonzero root takes over). And the isolated fraction still tracks $e^{-c}$ ($0.2244 / 0.1341 / 0.0500$ against $0.223 / 0.135 / 0.050$): the giant absorbs vertices, but the loners obey their own law.

Act III: full connectivity, and the honest threshold. The last obstruction to connectivity is the isolated vertex. At $p = (\ln n + c)/n$:

$\mathbb{P}(G(n, p) \text{ is connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}$ — the double-exponential Gumbel law, because the number of isolated vertices converges to $\operatorname{Poisson}(e^{-c})$ (the Poisson paradigm, back for an encore; everything else that could block connectivity dies far earlier, a fact the literature proves and the Atlas's lab measures: in the machine's runs the fraction of graphs with no isolated vertex and the fraction connected coincided at $c = 0$ and $c = 1$, and at $c = -1$ differed by exactly two of $200$ samples — disconnected graphs whose only obstructions are isolated edges, the finite-$n$ residue that dies with $n$). The machine at $n = 400$, $p = (\ln 400 + c)/400$, $200$ samples per $c$: connected fractions $0.070 / 0.390 / 0.755$ at $c = -1 / 0 / 1$, against the limits $0.0660 / 0.3679 / 0.6922$ — tracking, with the $c = 1$ cell honestly above its limit (finite-$n$ drift plus sampling noise, recorded, not smoothed). Lecture 5 · Discrepancy: Six Standard Deviations Suffice

Here is a question the probabilistic method was born for, and that Act I could not touch. Given $m$ sets $S_1, \dots, S_m$ on a ground set of $n$ elements — or, the same thing, an $m \times n$ $0/1$ (or $\pm 1$) matrix $A$ — color the elements red/blue and ask how even the coloring can be: the discrepancy is

$$\operatorname{disc}(\chi) = \max_i \left|\sum_{j \in S_i} \chi(j)\right|,$$

the worst set-imbalance, and the theorem target is $\operatorname{disc}(A) = \min_{\chi} \operatorname{disc}(\chi)$. A random coloring makes each set-sum a $\pm 1$ random walk, so the typical imbalance is about $\sqrt{n}$ — the size of a random walk's stride. Can you always do as well as randomness suggests? And how much better?

The lower bound: randomness is honest, and Hadamard is exact. No coloring can beat the $\sqrt{n}$ scale on the right instances, and the witness is a Hadamard matrix $H$ (entries $\pm 1$, $HH^{\mathsf{T}} = nI$). Take $H$ as the set system. For any coloring $\chi \in \{\pm 1\}^n$, the vector $H\chi$ has

$$\|H\chi\|_2^2 = \chi^{\mathsf{T}} H^{\mathsf{T}} H \chi = n \cdot \|\chi\|_2^2 = n^2,$$

so some row has $|(H\chi)_i| \ge n/\sqrt{n} =$ $\sqrt{n}$ — two lines, machine-verified on Sylvester-16, Paley-12 and Paley-20 ($100$ random colorings each: $\|H\chi\|_2 = n$ exactly, always; the norm argument is exact arithmetic, not a bound). The exact optimal discrepancy of the classical systems was computed by exhaustive search over all $2^n$ colorings: Sylvester-4: $2 = \sqrt{4}$; Sylvester-8: $4$; Sylvester-16: $4 = \sqrt{16}$; Paley-12: $4$; Paley-20: $6$ — every value at or above $\sqrt{n}$, the lower bound attained twice. And one accident the Atlas records because it is a better teacher than the bound itself: slice the Hadamard (delete the first row and column) and the sliced system has discrepancy $1$ for every normalized Hadamard of even order $n \ge 2$ — the all-ones coloring gives every remaining row sum exactly $-1$ (orthogonality to the all-ones first row, spilling into the sliced rows). The famous $\sqrt{n}$ lower bound lives on the full system; the slice quietly surrenders. Exercise 7 walks you through both.

The upper bound: Spencer's theorem. For $n$ sets on $n$ elements:

$\operatorname{disc}(A) \le 6\sqrt{n}$ — six standard deviations suffice (Spencer 1985: the full statement for $m$ sets on $n$ elements reads $6\sqrt{n\cdot \ln(2m/n)}$ when $m \ge n$). The constant $6$ is honest generosity: the proof's two-phase structure (partial coloring, then recursion) loses constants at each phase, and Spencer got $6$ where the truth — per the Komlós conjecture, below — is suspected to be an absolute constant independent of everything.

The exhibit: theory's $6$ vs. reality's $2$. The Atlas ran the exact optimum — Gray-code exhaustive search over all $2^n$ colorings, certified against an independent naive enumerator — on $20$ seeded instances ($m = n \in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$, entries $\pm 1$), against the best of $10,000$ random colorings and against $6\sqrt{n}$. Second: a single random coloring is already decent (mean imbalance $\approx \mathbb{E}\max$ of $n$ walks $\approx \sqrt{2n\ln n}$-ish at these sizes), and $10,000$ tries find the optimum in $18$ of $20$ instances — Spencer's guarantee is real mathematics, but at these sizes nobody needs it. The chapter's honesty ledger: the theorem earns its keep where search cannot go, at $m, n$ in the thousands, where its $O(\sqrt{n})$ against the $\sqrt{n\ln n}$ of naive sampling is the whole game.

The proof of Spencer's partial-coloring lemma and geometric recursion. Rather than coloring all $n$ elements in one shot (where a union bound over $m = n$ sets forces the $\sqrt{2n\ln n}$ random-walk penalty), Spencer (1985) proves that for any $m$ sets $S_1, \dots, S_m \subseteq [n]$ and suitable $\lambda = C\sqrt{1 + \ln(m/n)}$, there exists a partial coloring $x \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^n$ with at least $n/2$ nonzero coordinates ($\pm 1$) such that $|\sum_{j \in S_i} x_j| \le \lambda\sqrt{n}$ for every $i = 1, \dots, m$:

  1. The entropy / pigeonhole engine. Draw $y \in \{-1, +1\}^n$ uniformly at random. For each set $S_i$, define the quantized bucket $b_i(y) \in \mathbb{Z}$ by partitioning $\mathbb{R}$ into intervals of width $2\lambda\sqrt{n}$ centered at multiples of $2\lambda\sqrt{n}$, so $b_i(y) = 0$ iff $|\sum_{j \in S_i} y_j| \le \lambda\sqrt{n}$, and $b_i(y) = k$ when $\sum_{j \in S_i} y_j \in ((2k-1)\lambda\sqrt{n}, (2k+1)\lambda\sqrt{n}]$. Because $\sum_{j \in S_i} y_j$ is sub-Gaussian with variance $|S_i| \le n$, Hoeffding's inequality bounds $\mathbb{P}(|b_i(y)| = k) \le 2\exp(-(2k-1)^2 \lambda^2 / 2)$ for $k \ge 1$, so the binary Shannon entropy $H_2(b_i(y))$ (in bits, $\log_2$) is bounded by $h(\lambda) = O(\lambda^2 e^{-\lambda^2/2})$, which tends to $0$ rapidly as $\lambda$ grows. By subadditivity of entropy, the joint bucket vector $b(y) = (b_1(y), \dots, b_m(y))$ satisfies $$H_2(b(y)) \le \sum_{i=1}^m H_2(b_i(y)) \le m \cdot h(\lambda).$$ Choose $\lambda = C_0\sqrt{1 + \ln(m/n)}$ so that $m \cdot h(\lambda) \le \frac{n}{6}$.
  2. Finding a huge fiber. Since $H_2(b(y)) = \sum_{b^*} \mathbb{P}(b(y) = b^*) \log_2 \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(b(y) = b^*)} \le \frac{n}{6}$, there exists a deterministic bucket vector $b^*$ with $\log_2 \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(b(y) = b^*)} \le \frac{n}{6}$, whose preimage fiber $\mathcal{F} = \{y \in \{-1, +1\}^n : b(y) = b^*\}$ has size $$|\mathcal{F}| \ge 2^n \cdot 2^{-n/6} = 2^{5n/6} \approx 2^{0.8333 n}.$$
  3. Diameter in Hamming metric (Kleitman's isoperimetric theorem). By Kleitman's diameter theorem (1966), any subset $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \{-1, +1\}^n$ of Hamming diameter strictly less than $n/2$ has size at most the cardinality of a Hamming ball of radius $\lfloor n/4 \rfloor$: $$\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/4 \rfloor} \binom{n}{k} \le 2^{n H_2(1/4)} < 2^{0.8113 n} < 2^{5n/6} \le |\mathcal{F}|$$ (for all $n \ge 1$, checking $n < 4$ directly since $\lfloor n/4 \rfloor = 0$ gives $1 < 2^{5n/6}$). Therefore $\mathcal{F}$ has Hamming diameter at least $\lceil n/2 \rceil$: there exist two sign vectors $y', y'' \in \mathcal{F}$ that differ in at least $n/2$ coordinates.
  4. The half-coloring. Set $x = (y' - y'')/2 \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^n$. Wherever $y'_j \ne y''_j$ (at least $n/2$ coordinates), $x_j \in \{-1, +1\}$; wherever $y'_j = y''_j$, $x_j = 0$. Because $b(y') = b(y'') = b^*$, both $\sum_{j \in S_i} y'_j$ and $\sum_{j \in S_i} y''_j$ lie in the same interval of width $2\lambda\sqrt{n}$, so $|\sum_{j \in S_i} x_j| \le \lambda\sqrt{n}$ for every $i = 1, \dots, m$.
  5. Geometric recursion. Fix the $\ge n/2$ colored coordinates of $x$, restrict the $m$ sets to the $n_1 \le n/2$ uncolored coordinates ($x_j = 0$), and repeat. At round $r$, at most $n_r \le n / 2^r$ elements remain uncolored against $m$ sets, incurring discrepancy at most $C\sqrt{n_r \ln(2m/n_r)} \le C\sqrt{n} \cdot 2^{-r/2} \sqrt{\ln(2m/n) + r\ln 2}$. Summing the convergent geometric series over $r \ge 0$ gives total discrepancy $O(\sqrt{n\ln(2m/n)})$, and optimizing the constants at $m = n$ yields Spencer's $6\sqrt{n}$. $\blacksquare$

Two modern sharpenings the Atlas names without proof: Banaszczyk's 1998 bound (coloring vectors of $\ell_2$-norm $\le 1/5$ against a convex body of Gaussian measure $1/2$ — the "floating discrepancy" route), and the constructive line — Bansal's 2010 SDP-guided random walk, Lovett–Meka's 2015 algorithmic partial coloring (recovering Beck's entropy method constructively). And the frontier: the Komlós conjecture — column-normalized matrices (each column's $\ell_2$ norm $\le 1$) admit discrepancy $O(1)$ — open, with Hadamard-like examples showing the normalization is load-bearing. Exercise 17 sends you there.

What you now own: the discrepancy question (color so no set is unbalanced); the $\sqrt{n}$ lower bound by the two-line Hadamard norm argument, with the exact optima table; Spencer's $6\sqrt{n}$ with its constant honestly labeled generous; and the reflex — when a random construction is close to optimal, ask whether the theorem's job is to certify what sampling already finds, or to go where sampling cannot.

Lecture 6 · The Entropy Ledger: Brégman, Moser–Tardos, and Counting by Information

The chapter's final tool is not a probability at all. Entropy counts objects by the information needed to describe them, and when a count must be small, the information budget forces it. Two results, one classical and one from the modern era, both machine-verified to the last cell before printing.

The entropy tool (one paragraph). For a random variable $X$ taking values in a finite set, the entropy $H(X) = -\sum p \cdot \log p$ (logs base $e$ throughout this lecture; constants are honest either way) measures the information in one observation: $H(X) \le \log|\text{support}|$, with equality exactly at the uniform distribution, and the chain rule $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)$ turns joint information into a sum of conditional budgets. That is the whole machine. The craft is designing a description scheme whose cost per object lower-bounds the entropy, so that $(\text{number of objects}) \times (\text{cost}) \ge (\text{total information})$ — the count is squeezed from above by what the ledger allows.

Brégman's theorem (the permanent ledger). The permanent of a $0/1$ matrix counts perfect matchings: $\operatorname{per}(A) = \sum_\pi \prod_i A_{i, \pi(i)}$. Brégman's 1973 theorem (Minc's 1963 conjecture) says:

$$\operatorname{per}(A) \le \prod_i (r_i!)^{1/r_i}, \quad r_i = \text{the } i\text{-th row sum.}$$

Bipartite-graph language: a bipartite graph with left degrees $r_1, \dots, r_n$ has at most $\prod (r_i!)^{1/r_i}$ perfect matchings.

Proof of Brégman's theorem (Radhakrishnan's entropy argument). Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of perfect matchings (permutations $\sigma \in S_n$ with $A_{i, \sigma(i)} = 1$ for all $i$), so $|\mathcal{M}| = \operatorname{per}(A)$. If $\mathcal{M} = \emptyset$, the bound $0 \le \prod_{i=1}^n (r_i!)^{1/r_i}$ is immediate. Otherwise, let $\sigma \in \mathcal{M}$ be chosen uniformly at random, so $H(\sigma) = \ln \operatorname{per}(A)$.

For any fixed ordering $\tau \in S_n$ of the rows $1, \dots, n$, reveal the matching values $\sigma(\tau(1)), \sigma(\tau(2)), \dots, \sigma(\tau(n))$ one by one. By the chain rule of entropy, $$H(\sigma) = \sum_{k=1}^n H\!\left(\sigma(\tau(k)) \;\middle|\; \sigma(\tau(1)), \dots, \sigma(\tau(k-1))\right).$$ Fix row $i$ and let $k = \tau^{-1}(i)$ be its position in the reveal order. Before $\sigma(i)$ is revealed, the already-revealed partners $\{\sigma(\tau(1)), \dots, \sigma(\tau(k-1))\}$ eliminate some number of the $r_i$ nonzero columns in row $i$. Let $N_i(\sigma, \tau) \in \{1, 2, \dots, r_i\}$ be the number of available partners in row $i$ that have not yet been taken by rows preceding $i$ in $\tau$ (including $\sigma(i)$ itself, so $N_i(\sigma, \tau) \ge 1$). Conditioned on the history $\sigma(\tau(1)), \dots, \sigma(\tau(k-1))$, the random variable $\sigma(i)$ is supported on a set of size at most $N_i(\sigma, \tau)$, so its conditional entropy is at most $\ln N_i(\sigma, \tau)$ in each fiber. Averaging over the history gives $$H\!\left(\sigma(i) \;\middle|\; \{\sigma(j) : \tau^{-1}(j) < \tau^{-1}(i)\}\right) \le \mathbb{E}_\sigma\!\left[\ln N_i(\sigma, \tau)\right].$$ Now average this chain-rule identity over a uniformly random row ordering $\tau \in S_n$ chosen independently of $\sigma$. For any fixed matching $\sigma \in \mathcal{M}$ and fixed row $i$, row $i$ has $r_i$ nonzero entries in columns $C_i = \{j : A_{i,j} = 1\}$, which are matched by $\sigma$ to a set of $r_i$ rows $R_i = \sigma^{-1}(C_i)$ (with $i \in R_i$). Under a uniformly random permutation $\tau \in S_n$, the relative order of the $r_i$ rows in $R_i$ is uniformly distributed, and $N_i(\sigma, \tau)$ is simply the number of rows in $R_i$ that appear at or after $i$ in $\tau$. Thus $N_i(\sigma, \tau)$ is uniformly distributed over $\{1, 2, \dots, r_i\}$ for every fixed $\sigma$ and $i$. Consequently, $$\mathbb{E}_\tau\!\left[\ln N_i(\sigma, \tau)\right] = \frac{1}{r_i} \sum_{m=1}^{r_i} \ln m = \frac{1}{r_i} \ln(r_i!) = \ln\!\left((r_i!)^{1/r_i}\right).$$ Summing over $i = 1, \dots, n$ and averaging over $\sigma$ gives $$\ln \operatorname{per}(A) = H(\sigma) = \mathbb{E}_\tau\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n H\!\left(\sigma(i) \;\middle|\; \{\sigma(j) : \tau^{-1}(j) < \tau^{-1}(i)\}\right)\right] \le \sum_{i=1}^n \ln\!\left((r_i!)^{1/r_i}\right).$$ Exponentiating yields $\operatorname{per}(A) \le \prod_{i=1}^n (r_i!)^{1/r_i}$. $\blacksquare$

The machine's census — all $74,954$ bipartite graphs with both sides of size at most $4$ ($66,066$ of them square, where a perfect matching can exist): zero violations, with $151$ equality cases, and the equality characterization machine-verified in both directions: equality holds (for matrices with $\operatorname{per}(A) > 0$) if and only if every connected component is a complete bipartite graph $K_{u,u}$ ($u \ge 1$) — block-diagonal all-ones, up to row and column permutation. (Under the lab's convention $(0!)^{1/0} = 1$ for zero rows, matrices with $\operatorname{per}(A) = 0$ have right-hand side $\ge 1 > 0$, so every equality graph has $\operatorname{per}(A) \ge 1$ and decomposes into $K_{u,u}$ blocks with $u \ge 1$: for $K_{n,n}$ itself, $\operatorname{per} = n! = (n!)^{n/n}$, and for $K_2 \oplus K_2$, $\operatorname{per} = 4 = (2!)^{4/2}$.) The corollary for regular graphs — a $d$-regular bipartite graph on $n+n$ vertices has $\operatorname{per}(A) \le$ $(d!)^{n/d}$, with equality for $d \ge 1$ iff $d \mid n$ and the graph is a disjoint union of $n/d$ copies of $K_{d,d}$ — was verified census-wide too: all $160$ regular graphs with $n \le 4$, zero violations, $54$ equalities ($1 + 3 + 7 + 43 = 54$ across $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ for $d \ge 1$, e.g. the complement of a perfect matching at $n = 4$, $d = 3$: $\operatorname{per} = 9 \le (3!)^{4/3} \approx 10.90$).

The permanent has a matching lower bound for doubly stochastic matrices (rows and columns summing to $1$): $\operatorname{per}(A) \ge n!/n^n$ — the van der Waerden conjecture, proved by Egorychev and by Falikman in 1981. The machine certified the implementation (Ryser's permanent formula cross-checked against brute-force permutation enumeration on $800$ random matrices — all match) and then the bound: $2,800$ sampled doubly stochastic matrices (convex combinations of random permutation matrices, the Birkhoff polytope sampled honestly), $n = 2$ through $8$ — zero violations, with $\operatorname{per}(J/n) = n!/n^n$ exact to $10^{-12}$ (the equality case: the flat matrix). An honesty note from the ledger: the sampled minima approach the bound only at small $n$ ($n = 2$: $0.500006$ against the bound $0.5$; $n = 8$: $0.003961$ against $0.002403$) — the bound is tight only at $J/n$, and random samples of the Birkhoff polytope drift above it. (Kahn's 2001 theorem extends the ledger to list-colorings — the entropy method's flagship application — cited here as the signpost.)

Moser–Tardos: the Local Lemma becomes an algorithm. Chapter 4 §4.3 stated the symmetric Local Lemma: if each bad event has probability at most $p$ and depends on at most $d$ others, and $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1$, then $\mathbb{P}(\text{no bad event}) > 0$. Moser and Tardos (2010) turned that existence proof into the simplest algorithm imaginable: sample everything at random; while some bad event holds, resample its variables; halt.

Proof of Moser–Tardos termination via witness trees and entropy compression. Suppose bad events $A_1, \dots, A_m$ over independent random variables satisfy the Lovász Local Lemma condition: there exist $x_1, \dots, x_m \in (0, 1)$ such that $\mathbb{P}(A_i) \le x_i \prod_{j \in \Gamma(i)} (1 - x_j)$ for all $i$. Let $L = (i_1, i_2, \dots, i_T)$ be the execution log of resampled clause indices up to step $T$. For each step $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$, construct a witness tree $\tau_t$ rooted at $i_t$ by walking backward through the log $s = t-1, t-2, \dots, 1$: if $i_s$ shares variables with some vertex already in $\tau_t$ ($i_s \in \Gamma^+(v)$), attach a new vertex labeled $i_s$ at the maximum possible depth below such a vertex $v$; otherwise ignore step $s$.

The coupling / ledger lemma. Every witness tree $\tau$ that appears in the log satisfies: (i) vertices at the same depth have pairwise disjoint variable sets; and (ii) the probability that a fixed proper witness tree $\tau$ occurs in the execution log is at most $\prod_{v \in V(\tau)} \mathbb{P}(A_{\text{label}(v)})$.
Proof of (ii): Traverse the vertices of $\tau$ in reverse breadth-first order (deepest level first). For each vertex $v \in V(\tau)$ with label $i$, its occurrence in the log requires that just before $v$ was resampled, the variables in $\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)$ evaluated to a configuration violating $A_i$. Because every variable in $\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)$ is freshly resampled when $v$ fires, and any earlier step in the log that touched $\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)$ was placed strictly deeper in $\tau$, each vertex $v \in V(\tau)$ consumes an independent batch of fresh coin flips for $\operatorname{vbl}(A_{\text{label}(v)})$. Thus the probability that all vertices of $\tau$ fail their checks on their respective fresh coin flips is at most $\prod_{v \in V(\tau)} \mathbb{P}(A_{\text{label}(v)})$.

Counting via Galton–Watson branching. Because the $k$-th resampling of $A_i$ produces a witness tree $\tau$ rooted at $i$ containing exactly $k$ vertices labeled $i$, all witness trees rooted at $i$ occurring in a run are distinct. In a proper witness tree constructed by deepest-node attachment, all children of any node $v$ have distinct labels in $\Gamma^+(\text{label}(v))$. When $d = 0$, the events are mutually independent and each $A_i$ is resampled $\frac{\mathbb{P}(A_i)}{1 - \mathbb{P}(A_i)} \le \frac{1}{e-1}$ times in expectation. For $d \ge 1$, under the asymmetric LLL condition $\mathbb{P}(A_u) \le x_u \prod_{w \in \Gamma(u)} (1 - x_w)$ (or by absorbing self-dependency into $x_u' = \frac{1}{d+1}$ when $e p (d+1) \le 1$), comparing $\sum_{\tau \text{ rooted at } i} \prod_{v \in V(\tau)} \mathbb{P}(A_{\text{label}(v)})$ to the multitype Galton–Watson process rooted at $i$ with offspring probabilities $x_w$ gives $$\mathbb{E}[\#\text{resamplings of } A_i] \le \sum_{\tau \text{ rooted at } i} \prod_{v \in V(\tau)} \mathbb{P}(A_{\text{label}(v)}) \le \frac{x_i}{1 - x_i}.$$ In the symmetric setting with $d \ge 1$, $\mathbb{P}(A_i) \le p$, $|\Gamma(i)| \le d$, and $e p (d+1) \le 1$, choosing $x_i = \frac{1}{d+1}$ satisfies $x_i(1 - x_i)^d = \frac{1}{d+1}\left(1 - \frac{1}{d+1}\right)^d > \frac{1}{e(d+1)} \ge p$ and bounds the expected resamplings of each clause $A_i$ by $\frac{x_i}{1 - x_i} = \frac{1}{d}$ (so at most $m/d < \infty$ steps in total across all $m$ clauses), guaranteeing termination with probability $1$. $\blacksquare$

A seeded $4$-SAT chain: $10$ clauses over $22$ variables, clause $i = \{x_{2i}, x_{2i+1}, x_{2i+2}, x_{2i+3}\}$ with random signs — consecutive clauses share two variables, non-consecutive share none, so the dependency degrees are $d = [1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1]$ (computed from the variable-sharing structure, not assumed). Bad-event probability $p = 2^{-4} = 0.0625$, so $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) = 0.5097 \le 1$ — the Chapter 4 convention, machine-checked, satisfied with room. The resampler (first violated clause resampled, fresh Lehmer bits): $100$ of $100$ runs terminated; every final assignment satisfied all clauses; mean $0.81$ resampling events per run; worst run $4$; worst single clause resampled twice. The ledger's prediction — resampling counts of order $p\cdot(\text{structure})$, small under the LLL budget — is what the machine measured. def chromatic(adj, n):                     # exact chi: subset DP over color classes
    if n <= 0 or not adj:
        return 0
    chi = [0] * (1 << n)
    for S in range(1, 1 << n):
        v = (S & -S).bit_length() - 1      # lowest vertex: its class is independent
        best = n
        stack = [(1 << v, S & ~(1 << v))]  # (independent I so far, rest)
        while stack:
            I, rest = stack.pop()
            best = min(best, chi[rest])    # stop growing: drop I, pay one color
            for u in range(v + 1, n):
                if rest >> u & 1 and not any(adj[u][t]
                        for t in range(n) if I >> t & 1):
                    stack.append((I | 1 << u, rest & ~(1 << u)))
        chi[S] = best + 1                  # chi(S) = 1 + min chi(S \ I)
    return chi[-1]

def giant(n, c, trials, seed):
    if n <= 0 or trials <= 0:
        return 0.0, 0.0
    rand = make_rng(seed); p = c / n
    largest = isolated = 0.0
    for _ in range(trials):
        par, sz = list(range(n)), [1]*n     # union-find
        def find(v):
            while par[v] != v: par[v] = par[par[v]]; v = par[v]
            return v
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(i+1, n):          # one Lehmer coin per pair
                if rand() < p:
                    a, b = find(i), find(j)
                    if a != b:

                        par[b] = a; sz[a] += sz[b]
        largest += max(sz[find(v)] for v in range(n)) / n
        isolated += sum(sz[find(v)] == 1 for v in range(n)) / n
    return largest / trials, isolated / trials

from math import factorial as fact, prod

def exact_disc(A, n):                       # Gray code over all 2^n colorings
    if n <= 0 or not A:
        return 0
    s = [-sum(row) for row in A]            # all-eps-minus-one start
    best = max(abs(v) for v in s)
    for g in range(1, 1 << n):
        b = (g & -g).bit_length() - 1       # the flipped coordinate
        sign = 2 if (g ^ (g >> 1)) >> b & 1 else -2
        for i in range(len(A)): s[i] += sign * A[i][b]
        best = min(best, max(abs(v) for v in s))
    return best

def permanent(mask, n):                     # Ryser's formula via Gray code
    if n <= 0:
        return 1
    rows = [[(mask >> (i*n + j)) & 1 for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    col_sums = [0] * n
    total = 0
    for g in range(1, 1 << n):
        b = (g & -g).bit_length() - 1
        gray = g ^ (g >> 1)
        sgn = 1 if (gray >> b) & 1 else -1
        for j in range(n):
            col_sums[j] += sgn * rows[b][j]
        term = prod(col_sums)
        total += -term if (n - bin(gray).count("1")) & 1 else term
    return total

def bregman_ok(n):                          # census: per <= prod (r_i!)^(1/r_i)
    for mask in range(1 << (n*n)):          # fact/prod from math; permanent = Ryser
        r = [bin(mask >> (i*n) & ((1 << n)-1)).count("1") for i in range(n)]
        bound = prod(fact(ri) ** (1/ri) if ri else 1 for ri in r)
        if permanent(mask, n) > bound + 1e-9:
            return False
    return True

def clause_vars(c):                        # clause c on variables 2c..2c+3
    return [2*c + l for l in range(4)]

def resample(seed, trials=100):             # the Moser-Tardos chain of Lecture 6

            if viol is None: break
            for v in clause_vars(viol): val[v] = rand() < 0.5
            events += 1
        total += events
    return total / trials

Answer 1 (Kleitman's induction). Induct on $n$; the case $n = 0$ is one subset and equality. For an up-set $A$ of subsets of $[n]$, slice on the last coordinate: $A_0 = \{S \subseteq [n-1] : S \in A\}$ and $A_1 = \{S \subseteq [n-1] : S \cup \{n\} \in A\}$. The up-set property gives $A_0 \subseteq A_1$ (if $S \in A_0$ then $S \in A$, so $S \cup \{n\} \in A$, so $S \in A_1$) — and likewise $B_0 \subseteq B_1$. Slicing commutes with intersection, $((A \cap B)_0 = A_0 \cap B_0$ and $(A \cap B)_1 = A_1 \cap B_1)$, and $|A| = |A_0| + |A_1|$ because every member of $A$ is counted by exactly one slice. Apply the hypothesis on $[n-1]$ to both slice-pairs and add:

$$|A \cap B| \cdot 2^{n-1} = (|A_0 \cap B_0| + |A_1 \cap B_1|) \cdot 2^{n-1} \ge |A_0||B_0| + |A_1||B_1|.$$

It remains to check $|A_0||B_0| + |A_1||B_1| \ge |A||B|/2 = (|A_0|+|A_1|)(|B_0|+|B_1|)/2$, which after multiplying out is exactly

$$(|A_1| - |A_0|)(|B_1| - |B_0|) \ge 0,$$

true because both differences are nonnegative. Both factors are honest slice-growths — that is the whole proof, and the reason it deserves ink. $\blacksquare$ (The machine's census — $400 + 28,224$ ordered up-set pairs, zero violations — checked every instance of the theorem where it can be checked; this proof is the census's explanation.)

Answer 2 (The Janson ledger). $\mu = \binom{n}{3}p^3$ counts the triangles; $\Delta$ counts the unordered edge-sharing pairs: choose the shared edge ($\binom{n}{2}$ ways) and the two remaining vertices ($\binom{n-2}{2}$ ways), each pair present with probability $p^5$ — so $\Delta = \binom{n}{2}\cdot\binom{n-2}{2}\cdot p^5$ (and $\Delta_{\text{ord}} = 2\Delta$). At $p = c/n$: $\mu = \binom{n}{3}c^3/n^3 \to c^3/6$, while $\Delta = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}{2}\cdot \frac{c^5}{n^5} \to \frac{c^5}{4n} \to 0$. Both $\exp(-\mu + \Delta)$ and the half-weighted proxy $\exp(-\mu + \Delta/2)$ therefore converge to $e^{-c^3/6}$ — the repair bill shrinks a full order of $n$ faster than the main term, and Janson's inequality becomes sharp in the sparse regime (receipt: at $n = 1000$, $c = 1$, $\mu = 0.166167$, $\Delta = 0.000249$, $\exp(-\mu + \Delta/2) = 0.847010$ and $\exp(-\mu + \Delta) = 0.847115$, closing on $e^{-1/6} = 0.846482$ by $n = 10000$). Contrast the dense regime of the lecture: at $G(100, 1/2)$, $\Delta/2 = 367,614.8$ against $\mu = 20,212.5$ — vacuous. Same theorem, two worlds.

Answer 3 (FKG beyond product measure). For sets $A, B \subseteq [N]$ write $a = |A|$, $b = |B|$, $i = |A \cap B|$, $u = |A \cup B|$, so $a + b = i + u$ and $i \le a, b \le u$. The lattice condition for $\mu(A) = Z_N(q)^{-1} q^{|A|}/\binom{N}{|A|}$ reads

$$\frac{q^i}{\binom{N}{i}} \cdot \frac{q^u}{\binom{N}{u}} \ge \frac{q^a}{\binom{N}{a}} \cdot \frac{q^b}{\binom{N}{b}} \iff \binom{N}{a}\cdot\binom{N}{b} \ge \binom{N}{i}\cdot\binom{N}{u}.$$

The $q$'s and $Z_N(q)$'s cancel ($i + u = a + b$) — the condition is pure binomial. Now $\log\binom{N}{k}$ is concave in $k$ (the standard log-concavity $\binom{N}{k}^2 \ge \binom{N}{k-1}\binom{N}{k+1}$, proved by cross-cancelling factorials), and the pair $(i, u)$ is a spreading of $(a, b)$ with the same sum ($i \le \min(a,b)$, $\max(a,b) \le u$). A concave function loses value under spreading: $\log\binom{N}{i} + \log\binom{N}{u} \le \log\binom{N}{a} + \log\binom{N}{b}$. Exponentiate. $\blacksquare$ The failure note: the first-draft $\mu'(A) = \binom{N}{|A|}\cdot q^{|A|}$ inverts every ratio $\binom{N}{\cdot}$ in this computation — log-submodular, $68,210$ machine violations — because weighting by the rank size rewards exactly the spreading that concavity punishes. Flattening must divide, not multiply.

Answer 4 (Two Lipschitz costumes). For the edge $e = uv$: $\chi(G-e) \le \chi(G)$ because a coloring of $G$ is a coloring of $G-e$; and $\chi(G) \le \chi(G-e)+1$ because given a coloring of $G-e$, if $u, v$ already differ you are done, and if not, repaint $u$ with one fresh color. So $\chi(G)$ and $\chi(G-e)$ are within $1$ either way. For the vertex: $\chi(G-v) \le \chi(G) \le \chi(G-v)+1$ — restrict a coloring, or extend one by giving $v$ a fresh color. Edge exposure: $\binom{n}{2}$ reveal-steps with $|Y_{i+1}-Y_i| \le 1$ (the conditional version of the edge bound) gives Azuma's

$$\mathbb{P}(|\chi - \mathbb{E}\chi| \ge t) \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\binom{n}{2}}\right),$$

window of order $n$. Vertex exposure: $n - 1$ live steps (the last vertex adds no information), window $\sqrt{n-1}$ — strictly better, and the reason Shamir–Spencer wore that costume. The census ($5,325$ edge checks, $5,404$ vertex-deletion checks, all graphs $n \le 5$, zero violations) is the small-scale license for both.

Answer 5 (The Shamir–Spencer window). At step $i$ ($i = 1, \dots, n$), reveal all edges between vertex $i$ and the higher-indexed vertices $\{i+1, \dots, n\}$, and let $Y_i = \mathbb{E}[\chi \mid \text{first } i \text{ steps revealed}]$. The honest Lipschitz step: condition on the first $i$ steps and compare two realizations $E_0, E_1$ of vertex $(i+1)$'s edges to $\{i+2, \dots, n\}$. Let $G'$ be the graph with all edges between vertex $i+1$ and $\{i+2, \dots, n\}$ deleted (and any fixed realization on the remaining pairs); every completion $E$ satisfies $\chi(G' \cup E) \in [\chi(G'), \chi(G') + 1]$ (color $G'$ and, if needed, give vertex $i+1$ one fresh color). Averaging over the still-unexposed edges among $\{i+2, \dots, n\}$ preserves this interval of length $1$, so all possible values of $Y_{i+1}$ given $\mathcal{F}_i$ lie in an interval of length $1$, and since $Y_i = \mathbb{E}[Y_{i+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_i]$ is their average, $|Y_{i+1} - Y_i| \le 1$. The last step ($i = n$) reveals no new edges ($\{n+1, \dots, n\} = \emptyset$) and moves nothing ($Y_n = Y_{n-1}$), so Azuma with $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} c_i^2 = n - 1$ gives

$$\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\chi - \mathbb{E}\chi| > \lambda\sqrt{n-1}\right) < 2e^{-\lambda^2/2} \quad \text{for } G(n,p),\text{ every } p.$$

The machine exhibits are the generosity report: $G(10, 1/2)$ visits four values in total ($497/500$ samples on three values $3, 4, 5$) against the window's seven; $G(12, 1/2)$ visits four values ($187/200$ on $4$ and $5$) against $\pm 3.32$.

Answer 6 (Densities and thresholds). For any graph $H$ with at least one edge, $m(H) = \max_{F \subseteq H,\, e(F) \ge 1} e(F)/v(F)$ (and when $H$ contains a cycle, $m(H) \ge 1 > 1/2$, so the maximum is achieved on a subgraph with $v(F) \ge 3$). $K_4$: the whole graph gives $6/4 = 3/2$, triangles give $1$ — $m(K_4) = 3/2$, threshold $n^{-2/3}$. $K_4 - e$: $5/4$ (the whole graph; its two triangles give $1$), threshold $n^{-4/5}$ — deleting one edge retards the threshold from $n^{-2/3}$ to $n^{-4/5}$. The paw: the whole graph has $e = 4, v = 4$, ratio $1$ — equal to the triangle's; the pendant edge adds edges and vertices in exactly the balancing proportion, so $m(\text{paw}) = 1$ and the paw rides the triangle's threshold $n^{-1}$. The moral: density is $e/v$ of the densest subgraph, not of $H$ — compute, never assume; and the receipt ($K_3 = C_4 = \text{paw} = 1$, $K_4 - e = 5/4$, $K_4 = 3/2$, $K_5 = 2$) is machine-verified.

Answer 7 (The Hadamard bound, both directions). (a) For any $\chi \in \{\pm 1\}^n$: $\|H\chi\|_2^2 = \chi^{\mathsf{T}}(H^{\mathsf{T}} H)\chi = n\cdot \chi^{\mathsf{T}}\chi = n^2$. The maximum row beats the root-mean-square: $\max_i |(H\chi)_i| \ge \|H\chi\|_2/\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{n}$. Two lines; and since this holds for every $\chi$, $\operatorname{disc}(\text{full system}) \ge \sqrt{n}$ — with equality achieved by Sylvester-$4$ and Sylvester-$16$ among the classical systems (exact optima $2/4/4/4/6$ for Sylvester-$4/8/16$, Paley-$12/20$, all $\ge \sqrt{n}$, machine-exhausted over all $2^n$ colorings). (b) Assume $H$ has even Hadamard order $n \ge 2$ (i.e., $n = 2$ or $4 \mid n$; the trivial case $n = 1$ leaves an empty $0 \times 0$ slice). Slice off the first row and column. The all-ones coloring gives every sliced row sum $-1$: each remaining row is orthogonal to the all-ones first row, hence sums to $0$ over all $n$ entries, and its deleted first entry was $+1$. For the floor: since $n \ge 2$ is even, the slice has $n - 1$ columns — an odd positive integer — so every sliced row sum is a sum of an odd number of $\pm 1$s: odd, in particular nonzero, so $|\text{row sum}| \ge 1$ for every coloring. Floor $1$, ceiling $1$: $\operatorname{disc}(\text{sliced}) = 1$ exactly. And the honest footnote the exercise demanded: the norm argument of (a) is unavailable here — the sliced rows are no longer orthogonal (the deleted column held a $1$ in every row, so sliced pairwise inner products are $-1$; machine-checked on Sylvester-$4/8/16$). The accident happens precisely where the proof technique dies, and knowing which tool fails where is the actual skill.

Answer 8 (Brégman's regular corollary). In a $d$-regular bipartite graph ($d \ge 1$) every row sum is $d$, so Brégman's $\prod (r_i!)^{1/r_i} = \prod (d!)^{1/d} = (d!)^{n/d}$ — the product telescopes into a pure power. Equality for disjoint $K_{d,d}$ unions ($d \ge 1$, $d \mid n$): $\operatorname{per}$ is multiplicative over blocks, $\operatorname{per}(K_{d,d}) = d! = (d!)^{d/d}$, so a union of $n/d$ blocks has $\operatorname{per} = (d!)^{n/d}$ — exactly the bound. (The census's equality structure says this is the only shape: all $54$ regular-graph equalities at $n \le 4$ are $K_{d,d}$ unions with $d \ge 1$, per the both-directions verification of Lecture 6.) Numbers from the census: $160$ regular graphs $n \le 4$, zero violations; e.g. the complement of a perfect matching at $n = 4$, $d = 3$: $\operatorname{per} = 9 \le (3!)^{4/3} \approx 10.90$, and $C_4 \oplus C_4$: $\operatorname{per} = 4 = (2!)^{4/2}$, equality.

Answer 9 (Certify the budget). Clause $i$ sits on variables $\{x_{2i}, x_{2i+1}, x_{2i+2}, x_{2i+3}\}$. Clauses $i$ and $j$ share a variable iff their windows overlap: $|i-j| = 1$ shares exactly two variables ($\{x_{2i+2}, x_{2i+3}\}$ for the forward neighbor), $|i-j| \ge 2$ shares none. So clause $0$ depends only on clause $1$ ($d_0 = 1$), clause $9$ only on clause $8$ ($d_9 = 1$), and the eight interior clauses depend on two neighbors each: $d = [1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1]$, max $2$. With $p = 2^{-4} = 0.0625$: $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) = e \cdot 0.0625 \cdot 3 = 0.5097 \le 1$ — the budget holds with almost a factor $2$ in reserve. Prediction before running: the resampler should touch a handful of clauses per run and rarely revisit one — the ledger's expected event count under the LLL machinery is $O(1)$, $p\cdot(\text{number of clauses})$-scale. The machine's measurement: mean $0.81$ resampling events per run over $100$ runs, worst run $4$, no clause resampled more than twice — the prediction's shape, with honest numbers attached.

Answer 10 (The flag, paid in full). $\mathbb{E}[(X)_r]$ sums, over ordered $r$-tuples of distinct triangles, the probability that all are present. Group each tuple into overlap components (maximal groups of its triangles connected by vertex-sharing). A component of $t \ge 2$ triangles on $v$ vertices spans $e$ edges with $e \ge v + 1$ — check the cases: two triangles sharing a vertex ($v = 5, e = 6$), sharing an edge ($v = 4, e = 5$), and larger components only increase the surplus, by induction on adding a triangle that shares something. A singleton component (one triangle) has $v = 3, e = 3$. Now split the sum: tuples of $r$ vertex-disjoint triangles number $\approx (n^3/6)^r$ and contribute $(n^3/6)^r \cdot (c/n)^{3r} \to (c^3/6)^r$. Every other tuple has a non-singleton component; if its components span $V$ vertices in total, the tuple count is $O(n^V)$ and its probability is at most $p^{V+1}$ (each component charges $e \ge v + 1$ edges, singletons $v = e$), so its total contribution is $O(n^V \cdot n^{-(V+1)}) = O(1/n) \to 0$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[(X)_r] \to (c^3/6)^r$ for every fixed $r$. Factorial moments converging to $\lambda^r$ force $X \to \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$ (the Poisson paradigm — the method of moments, valid because Poisson laws are moment-determinate): $X \to \operatorname{Poisson}(c^3/6)$, $\mathbb{P}(\text{no triangle}) \to e^{-c^3/6}$. At $c = 1$: $e^{-1/6} \approx 0.846$ — Chapter 4's flag, retired. Calibration receipts: at $n = 6$, ordered pairs classify as $180$ edge-sharing $+ 200$ edge-disjoint $= 380$, giving $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] = 200p^6 + 180p^5$ (the first draft missed the $90$ vertex-sharing/edge-disjoint unordered pairs, and the brute enumeration refused to confirm it — the gate at work); at $n = 1000$, $c = 1$, $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)] = 0.02810798$ against $\mu^2 = 0.02761147$, ratio $1.018 \to 1$. One repair this answer pays for: Chapter 4's flag text rounded $e^{-1/6}$ as $\approx 0.852$; the correct rounding is $0.846$ (as Chapter 4's own lab printed), and the flag's home is repaired in this edition.

Answer 11 (The giant's equation). Expose the component of a fixed vertex by breadth-first exploration: each reached vertex offers $\operatorname{Bin}(n-1, c/n) \approx \operatorname{Poisson}(c)$ new neighbors, and in the $n \to \infty$ limit the exploration is a Galton–Watson branching process with $\operatorname{Poisson}(c)$ offspring. The process survives with probability $x$ satisfying the fixed-point equation for extinction: $q = \mathbb{P}(\text{extinction}) = e^{-c(1-q)}$ (each of the $\operatorname{Poisson}(c)$ children must itself die out), i.e. survival $x = 1 - e^{-cx}$. For $c \le 1$ the survival probability is $0$ — subcritical, no giant, the largest component of order $\log n$. For $c > 1$, $x > 0$, and the fraction of vertices in the giant is the survival probability of their explorations: $\rho = x$, so $\rho = 1 - e^{-c\rho}$ ($\rho = 0$ is always a root; for $c > 1$ the positive root takes over — the equation's two roots are the phase transition, made algebraic). Solving by iteration: $\rho(1.5) = 0.583,\; \rho(2) = 0.797,\; \rho(3) = 0.940$. The machine at $n = 1000$, $20$ samples per cell: $0.5706 / 0.7940 / 0.9405$ — the equation landing on its feet, with the residuals being the critical window's honest shadow (the convergence is provably slow near $c = 1$).

Answer 12 (The Gumbel law). The $r$-th factorial moment of the isolated-vertex count $X$ counts ordered $r$-tuples of distinct vertices, all isolated: choose them in $(n)_r$ ways; their joint isolation forbids every edge touching any of them — $r(n-1)$ incident edges, minus the $\binom{r}{2}$ edges among the chosen vertices that were counted twice:

$$\mathbb{E}[(X)_r] = (n)_r \cdot (1-p)^{r(n-1) - \binom{r}{2}}.$$

At $p = (\ln n + c)/n$: $(n)_r \sim n^r$, and the exponent $r(n-1) - \binom{r}{2} = rn - O(1)$ gives $(1-p)^{rn-O(1)} \to n^{-r} e^{-cr}$, the $\binom{r}{2}\cdot p$ correction vanishing. So $\mathbb{E}[(X)_r] \to e^{-cr} = (e^{-c})^r$ for every fixed $r$: $X \to \operatorname{Poisson}(e^{-c})$, and $\mathbb{P}(\text{no isolated vertex}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}$. For the finale: at this $p$ every obstruction to connectivity other than an isolated vertex dies with probability $\to 1$ (the theorem — Erdős–Rényi 1959/1960, Gilbert 1959; the Atlas's lab measures it: no-isolated and connected coincided in $598$ of $600$ runs across three cells — the two exceptions ($c = -1, n = 400$) were disconnected graphs whose only obstructions were isolated edges, the finite-$n$ residue that dies with $n$), so

$$\mathbb{P}(G(n,p) \text{ connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}} \quad \text{— the Gumbel law.}$$

The machine at $n = 400$: $0.070 / 0.390 / 0.755$ at $c = -1/0/1$, against the limits $0.0660 / 0.3679 / 0.6922$.

Answer 13 (◆ The budget's slack). The triangle as dependency graph: three events, each dependent on the other two ($d = 2$). The symmetric condition: $e \cdot p \cdot (d+1) \le 1 \implies p \le 1/(3e) \approx$ $0.1226$. Shearer's criterion: the multivariate independence polynomial must stay positive on every induced subgraph; for $K_3$ the independent sets are $\emptyset$ and the three singletons, so the criterion reads $1 - p > 0$, $1 - 2p > 0$, $1 - 3p > 0$ — the binding constraint is $p < 1/3 \approx$ $0.3333$. Tightness: on a uniform $3$-point space take $A_i = \{\text{point } i\}$ — three mutually dependent events of probability exactly $1/3$ with $\mathbb{P}(\text{all avoided}) = 0$. So the gap at the triangle is a factor of exactly $e$ ($0.3333/0.1226 = e$ to machine precision), and the same computation at $K_4$ ($d = 3$: $1/4$ vs. $1/(4e)$) shows it is no accident of small numbers: for the complete dependency graph, Shearer's optimal threshold is $1/(d+1)$ while the symmetric condition pays $1/(e(d+1))$ — the budget's built-in $e$ is the price of a proof that works for every dependency graph at once. Shearer's 1985 criterion is the exact price list; the symmetric lemma is the flat rate.

Answer 14 (◆ Azuma vs. Talagrand on LIS). Azuma's costume: reveal the permutation element by element; exposing one element changes the longest increasing subsequence by at most $1$ (delete the element from the subsequence, or insert it — one step either way), so with $n$ reveal-steps the window is $2\exp(-t^2/(2n))$: order $\sqrt{n}$. Honest, and wrong by a power of $n$: the true fluctuation of LIS around its mean $2\sqrt{n}$ is of order $n^{1/6}$ (Baik–Deift–Johansson 1999 — the normalized fluctuation follows the Tracy–Widom GUE law). Why Azuma cannot see this: it charges every exposure step at its worst-case cost $1$, and the worst case (a revealed element moving through the entire optimal subsequence) is astronomically untypical — Azuma pays for exposure that almost never matters. Talagrand's convex-distance inequality pays for what is certified instead: membership in $\{\text{LIS} \ge k\}$ is witnessed by a short certificate — the $k$ coordinates of one increasing subsequence — and the inequality's $\ell^2$ bookkeeping turns that witness structure into a Gaussian tail at scale $\sqrt{k}$: applied with $k \approx 2\sqrt{n}$, the window becomes order $n^{1/4}$. Still not $n^{1/6}$ — Talagrand's certificate counting is not the truth either, but it is a full power of $n$ closer, because it prices witnesses rather than exposures. The numbers at $n = 10^6$: mean $\approx 2000$, Azuma window $\approx 1000$, Talagrand window $\approx 32$, truth $\approx 10$. Statement-level throughout: the Tracy–Widom theorem is far beyond this Atlas's altitude, and the answer says so.

Answer 15 (◆ Where the second moment breaks). Inside the window $p = 1/n + \lambda n^{-4/3}$, the component structure has no scale separation. Below the window ($p = c/n$ with fixed $c < 1$, or more precisely $n - np \gg n^{2/3}$), the expected number of tree components of size $k$ is $$\mathbb{E}[T_k] = \binom{n}{k} k^{k-2} p^{k-1} (1-p)^{k(n-k) + \binom{k}{2} - (k-1)} \sim \frac{n}{c} \frac{(ce^{-c})^k k^{k-2}}{k!}.$$ Writing $1 - p = 1 - \frac{1}{n} - \lambda n^{-4/3}$, the exponential decay rate per vertex $k$ is governed by $c - 1 - \ln c \approx \frac{(c-1)^2}{2}$. When $c = 1 + \lambda n^{-1/3}$, this quadratic damping factor is $\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 n^{-2/3}$, which stays bounded of order $O(1)$ for component sizes up to $k \asymp n^{2/3}$. Furthermore, for two candidate tree components $S_1, S_2$ of size $k$, their indicators $I_{S_1}, I_{S_2}$ are not independent when $S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset$: knowing $S_1$ is an isolated component forbids all $k^2$ cross-edges between $S_1$ and $S_2$, dividing the unconditional probability by $(1-p)^{k^2} \approx \exp(-k^2/n)$. As long as $k \ll n^{1/2}$, $(1-p)^{-k^2} = 1 + o(1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(T_k) = o((\mathbb{E}[T_k])^2)$. Inside the critical window $p = \frac{1}{n} + \lambda n^{-4/3}$, however, the largest components live at scale $k \asymp n^{2/3}$, where $k^2 p \asymp n^{1/3} \to \infty$, and the expected number of cycles of length $\ell \asymp n^{1/3}$ is $\frac{(np)^\ell}{2\ell} \asymp \frac{1}{\ell}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X) / (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ converges to a strictly positive constant $\kappa(\lambda) > 0$ rather than $0$. No constant is asymptotically pinned by an expectation whose relative error does not vanish — the second moment is not merely weak here, it is structurally unusable. Janson's repair stalls for the twin reason: Janson's inequality needs the dependency bill $\Delta$ to be a lower-order correction to $\mu$ ($\Delta \ll \mu$ was the sparse regime's whole gift), but at the window the component-structure events are correlated at the same scale as their means — $\Delta$ grows to $\mu$'s order, $\exp(-\mu + \Delta)$ degenerates, and no exponential-moment argument separates the scales, because there is no separation to exploit. What replaces them is genuinely different mathematics: Bollobás 1984 established that the window exists and has width exactly $n^{-4/3}$; Janson–Knuth–Łuczak–Pittel 1993 derived the limit laws inside it (component sizes rescaled by $n^{-2/3}$, the excursions of a Brownian motion with parabolic drift). The Atlas cites both and proves neither — the honest answer is that the window is where this chapter's two engines stop, and knowing precisely where and why they stop is the calibration the exercise asked for.
