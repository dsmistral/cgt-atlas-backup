Chapter 14 · Regularity and Density

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

Turán's theorem (Chapter 2) and Erdős–Stone (this chapter, Lecture 3) say the same scandalous thing: the extremal question "how many edges before $H$ must appear" is governed by one number, the chromatic number of $H$ — a single integer steering an $n^2$-scale question. Why should that be true? What is it about large dense graphs that makes one parameter so powerful? The answer is the Szemerédi regularity lemma, and it is the engine room of this chapter. Regularity (Lecture 1): every large graph, however adversarial, is a bounded blow-up of a small pattern graph, draped in quasirandom noise. Counting and removal (Lecture 2): within that structure, copies of any fixed $H$ are counted by multiplying densities — and a graph with few copies of $H$ can be cleaned of them all for the price of few edge deletions. Stability (Lecture 3): near-extremal $H$-free graphs are not just few in edges — they are close to the Turán graph itself. Roth (Lecture 4): triangle removal $+$ a layered construction $\implies$ every positive-density set of integers holds a $3$-term arithmetic progression — combinatorics proving number theory. Density Ramsey (Lecture 5): Szemerédi's theorem for all $k$, the density Hales–Jewett theorem, and the honest hierarchy between them. The cap set theorem (Lecture 6): the 2016 surprise — the polynomial method of Chapter 6 returning with slice rank, and the constant $2.7551046130^n$.

Notation. For vertex sets $X, Y$ in a graph $G$, $e(X, Y)$ counts the ordered pairs $(x, y) \in X \times Y$ with $xy \in E(G)$ (so $e(X, Y)$ is the number of cross-edges when $X, Y$ are disjoint, and $e(X, X) = 2|E(G[X])|$ when $X = Y$), and $d(X, Y) = e(X, Y)/(|X||Y|)$ is the density between them. A pair $(X, Y)$ is $\varepsilon$-regular if $|d(A, B) - d(X, Y)| \le \varepsilon$ for every $A \subseteq X$, $B \subseteq Y$ with $|A| \ge \varepsilon|X|$, $|B| \ge \varepsilon|Y|$. A partition $V = V_1 \cup \dots \cup V_k$ is equitable if the parts differ in size by at most one, and $\varepsilon$-regular if all but at most $\varepsilon k^2$ of the pairs $(V_i, V_j)$ are $\varepsilon$-regular. The index (or energy) of a partition $P$ is $q(P) = \sum_{i,j} \frac{|V_i||V_j|}{n^2} \cdot d(V_i, V_j)^2$, diagonal included — the weighted average of squared densities. $\operatorname{ex}(n, H)$ is the maximum number of edges of an $n$-vertex $H$-free graph; $\chi(H)$ its chromatic number; $\pi(H) = 1 - \frac{1}{\chi(H) - 1}$ its Turán density. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is the integers mod $n$ ($n$ odd throughout, so $2$ is invertible); $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ is the $n$-dimensional vector space over $\{0, 1, 2\}$. $o(\cdot)$ is as $n \to \infty$.

Lecture 1 · The Regularity Lemma: Every Graph Wants to Be Simple

Here is a fact it took combinatorics until 1975 to notice. Take any graph on a million vertices — the most adversarial construction you can imagine, edges placed by an enemy to defeat every theorem. The regularity lemma says: at the right magnification, your enemy's graph is boring. Its vertices split into a bounded number of parts, the edges between any two parts behave like a coin flip, and the only thing your enemy actually chose is the small pattern of densities between the parts. Structure at the macro level, noise at the micro level — whether the graph is a Turán graph, a Paley graph, or pseudorandom garbage, this description is forced.

The notion that makes "behaves like a coin flip" precise is $\varepsilon$-regularity. Say it once more, slowly: a pair $(X, Y)$ is $\varepsilon$-regular if no pair of large test sets can detect a bias — for every $A \subseteq X$ and $B \subseteq Y$ with $|A| \ge \varepsilon|X|$ and $|B| \ge \varepsilon|Y|$, the density $d(A, B)$ stays within $\varepsilon$ of the overall density $d(X, Y)$.

✎ Checkpoint. Before reading on: at $\varepsilon = 1/4$, among all $65,536$ bipartite graphs on $4 + 4$ labeled vertices, how many do you expect to be $\varepsilon$-regular — most of them, about half, or almost none? Commit to a number.

The machine answer was run as an exhaustive census (every graph, every test pair — no sampling): at $\varepsilon = 1/4$ exactly $2$ of the $65,536$ graphs are $\varepsilon$-regular. Regularity at a meaningful tolerance is rare, a genuine structural property and not a formality — which is the point: the lemma will force it. At $\varepsilon = 1/2$ the count rises to $46,408$ (about $71\%$), and at $\varepsilon = 3/4$ it reaches $65,536$ — the property has gone vacuous, as every bound in this chapter does when its tolerance is loosened far enough. The census also fixes the worst a witness can be forced to show: some non-regular pair at $\varepsilon = 1/4$ has a test pair $(A, B)$ with $|d(A, B) - d(X, Y)| = 0.9375$, nearly the full range — non-regularity is not always subtle.

The Szemerédi regularity lemma. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every integer $m$ there is $M(\varepsilon, m)$ such that every graph on $n \ge m$ vertices admits an equitable partition into $k$ parts with $m \le k \le M(\varepsilon, m)$, all but at most $\varepsilon k^2$ of the pairs $\varepsilon$-regular. (E. Szemerédi, "Regular partitions of graphs", Problèmes Combinatoires et Théorie des Graphes, Orsay 1976, Colloq. Internat. CNRS 260, 399–401.)

The proof is an energy argument — averaging with a spotlight, P10 upgraded. For a partition $P$ define the index $q(P) = \sum_{i,j} \frac{|V_i||V_j|}{n^2} \cdot d(V_i, V_j)^2$ (the diagonal terms $i = j$ included; with them the weights sum to exactly $1$, so $0 \le q \le 1$ with both ends honest: $q = 0$ for the empty graph, $q \to 1$ for the complete graph partitioned into singletons). Two monotonicity facts drive everything:

  1. Refinement raises the index. If $P'$ refines $P$, then $q(P') \ge q(P)$. Proof: Let $X = \bigcup_{a=1}^r X_a$ and $Y = \bigcup_{b=1}^s Y_b$ be decomposed into subparts of $P'$. Because $e(X, Y) = \sum_{a,b} e(X_a, Y_b) = \sum_{a,b} |X_a||Y_b| d(X_a, Y_b)$, the overall density $d(X, Y)$ is the weighted average $\sum_{a,b} p_{a,b} d(X_a, Y_b)$ with weights $p_{a,b} = \frac{|X_a||Y_b|}{|X||Y|}$ summing to $1$. By the elementary variance identity $\sum_{a,b} p_{a,b} d(X_a, Y_b)^2 - d(X, Y)^2 = \sum_{a,b} p_{a,b} (d(X_a, Y_b) - d(X, Y))^2 \ge 0$, multiplying by $\frac{|X||Y|}{n^2}$ gives $$\sum_{a,b} \frac{|X_a||Y_b|}{n^2} d(X_a, Y_b)^2 - \frac{|X||Y|}{n^2} d(X, Y)^2 = \sum_{a,b} \frac{|X_a||Y_b|}{n^2} \left(d(X_a, Y_b) - d(X, Y)\right)^2 \ge 0.$$ Summing over all pairs $(X, Y)$ of $P$ yields $q(P') \ge q(P)$. The Atlas ran this as an exhaustive machine check: for every graph and partition on small vertex sets, the inequality held without exception — $106,496$ checks at $n = 5$ (all $1,024$ graphs $\times$ all $52$ partitions $\times$ both ladder bounds) and $1,638,400$ at $n = 6$ (all $32,768$ graphs $\times$ the $25$ equitable $(2,2,2)$ and $(3,3)$ partitions $\times$ both bounds), zero violations — pinning the ladder $q(\text{trivial}) \le q(P) \le q(\text{singletons})$ that the proof climbs.
  2. Defects pay rent. Suppose $(X, Y)$ is not $\varepsilon$-regular, witnessed by $X_1 \subseteq X, Y_1 \subseteq Y$ with $|X_1| \ge \varepsilon|X|$, $|Y_1| \ge \varepsilon|Y|$, and $|d(X_1, Y_1) - d(X, Y)| > \varepsilon$. Partition $X = X_1 \cup X_2$ and $Y = Y_1 \cup Y_2$; by the variance identity above, the single sub-block $(X_1, Y_1)$ alone contributes at least $$\frac{|X_1||Y_1|}{n^2}\left(d(X_1, Y_1) - d(X, Y)\right)^2 > \frac{\varepsilon|X| \cdot \varepsilon|Y|}{n^2} \cdot \varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^4 \frac{|X||Y|}{n^2}$$ to the index increment. When more than $\varepsilon k^2$ ordered pairs $(V_i, V_j)$ of an equitable $k$-partition $P$ are irregular (with $|V_i| = n/k$), simultaneously refining every part $V_i$ by the common intersection of all witness cuts (producing at most $k \cdot 2^{k-1}$ subparts, then re-equitabilizing into $k' \le k \cdot 4^k$ parts at an index loss of at most $\varepsilon^5/2$) yields a net energy gain of at least $\varepsilon k^2 \cdot \varepsilon^4 \frac{(n/k)^2}{n^2} - \frac{\varepsilon^5}{2} = \frac{\varepsilon^5}{2}$, so $q(P') \ge q(P) + \varepsilon^5/2$ (the core variance identity was machine-checked over $1,125,000$ instances, zero violations). A defect is not free: exposing it deposits energy.

Now iterate. Start from an equitable partition into $m$ parts. If it is $\varepsilon$-regular, stop. If not, refine by the witnesses; the index rises by at least $\varepsilon^5/2$, and since it is capped at $1$, there are at most $2\varepsilon^{-5}$ refinement rounds before the partition must be $\varepsilon$-regular. The catch — the famous, honest catch — is the size of the partitions along the way: each refinement multiplies the number of parts by roughly $4$ per defective pair, and the part count grows like a tower of exponentials in the number of rounds. The standard bound makes $M(\varepsilon)$ a tower of $2$'s of height $O(\varepsilon^{-5})$.

Do not nod past that tower; look at it. The Atlas ran the toy iteration $k \mapsto k\cdot 2^k$ (the same shape as the refinement blow-up) from $k_0 = 2$: after one step $k = 8$, after two $k = 2048$, after three $k = 2048\cdot 2^{2048} \approx 10^{619.8}$ — already past the number of atoms in the observable universe ($10^{80}$) and past a googol ($10^{100}$) at the third step — and the fourth step overflows $64$-bit floating point arithmetic entirely. A true tower of $2$'s of height $5$ is $2^{65536} \approx 10^{19728}$. At the sample tolerances, the regularity proof's tower carries about $100,000$ levels at $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $3.2$ million at $\varepsilon = 0.05$, and ten billion at $\varepsilon = 0.01$. And the tower is not an artifact of the proof: Gowers proved partitions exist for which any regular partition genuinely needs tower height $\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1/16})$ — a proof later simplified by Moshkovitz–Shapira — and Fox–Lovász pinned the tight order of the tower height, about $\varepsilon^{-2}$, for a version of the lemma — the regularity lemma is, in a precise sense, about towers. The honest gap between the proven upper height ($\varepsilon^{-5}$) and the lower one ($\varepsilon^{-1/16}$), for the standard form of the lemma, is stated here as a gap, not smoothed over.

What the lemma does not say is as important as what it says. It is vacuous for sparse graphs (densities shrink with the noise). Its constants are galactic — no algorithm can afford $M(\varepsilon)$ — which is why the algorithmic regularity literature (finding the partition, not just proving it exists) is a separate subject the chapter only names. And the $\varepsilon$-regularity of a pair is a for-all condition over test sets: checking it naively costs exponential time, which is why the Lab's Part 1 brute-forces it only at census scale.

Lecture 2 · Counting and Removal: Densities Multiply, Copies Yield

The regularity lemma described the macro graph: parts, densities, uniform noise. This lecture turns the description into arithmetic. The counting lemma says that in an $\varepsilon$-regular world, the number of triangles is what the densities predict — the product $d_{12}\cdot d_{13}\cdot d_{23}$ times the volume. Its philosophical punchline is a dichotomy: in a regular setting, a graph's triangle count is either zero or enormous — there is no in-between, no "seven triangles hiding in a dense sea of edges". Then, run in reverse with the regularity lemma's refining power, the counting lemma proves the graph removal lemma: a graph with few copies of $H$ can be made $H$-free by deleting few edges. Few in, few out — the deletion theorem that Lecture 4 will fire at the integers.

First, the trap. Why is $\varepsilon$-regularity needed at all — aren't densities enough?

✎ Checkpoint. A tripartite graph has parts $X, Y, Z$ of size $16$, with every pairwise density exactly $1/2$: $d(X,Y) = d(X,Z) = d(Y,Z) = 1/2$. The product $1/8$ would predict $(16)^3/8 = 512$ triangles. Guess: can such a graph have zero triangles?

It can, and the Atlas built the witness: a hi/lo split. Label each part's $16$ vertices $0\dots 15$ and let $\operatorname{hi}(v)$ record whether $v \ge 8$. Put an $X$–$Y$ edge when $\operatorname{hi}(x) = \operatorname{hi}(y)$, a $Y$–$Z$ edge when $\operatorname{hi}(y) = \operatorname{hi}(z)$, and a $Z$–$X$ edge when $\operatorname{hi}(z) \ne \operatorname{hi}(x)$. Each pair carries exactly $128$ of its $256$ possible edges — every pairwise density is exactly $1/2$ — and a machine sweep confirms the triangle count is zero: a triangle needs $\operatorname{hi}(x) = \operatorname{hi}(y) = \operatorname{hi}(z)$ from the first two pairs, and then $\operatorname{hi}(z) \ne \operatorname{hi}(x)$ from the third — a flat contradiction. The pairs are maximally irregular: the test sets $X = \{0,1,2,3\}$ (all low) and $Y = \{8,9,10,11\}$ (all high) see density $0$ where the pair as a whole says $1/2$ — witness discrepancy exactly $1/2$, the maximum over $2,000$ seeded test pairs as well. Densities lie when the pairs are not regular — and that is the point: the counting lemma's hypothesis is not decoration; it is the engine.

The counting lemma (triangles, $\varepsilon$-tolerant form). Let $(X, Y), (X, Z), (Y, Z)$ be $\varepsilon$-regular pairs with densities $d_{12}, d_{13}, d_{23} \ge 2\varepsilon$. Then the number of triangles $xyz$ is within a factor $(1 \pm C\varepsilon)$ of $d_{12}\cdot d_{13}\cdot d_{23}\cdot |X||Y||Z|$; in particular, it is at least $(1 - 2\varepsilon)(d_{12}-\varepsilon)(d_{13}-\varepsilon)(d_{23}-\varepsilon)\cdot |X||Y||Z|$, with per-vertex degree-floor product window $(d_{12}-\varepsilon)(d_{13}-\varepsilon)(d_{23}-\varepsilon)\cdot |X||Y||Z|$.

Proof (greedy embedding — the chapter gives it in full). For each $x \in X$ let $d_Y(x), d_Z(x)$ be its degrees into $Y$ and $Z$. Regularity of $(X, Y)$ forces all but at most $\varepsilon|X|$ vertices $x$ to satisfy $d_Y(x) \ge (d_{12} - \varepsilon)|Y|$ — otherwise the set $A$ of low-degree vertices would have $|A| \ge \varepsilon|X|$ and $d(A, Y) < d_{12} - \varepsilon$, violating $\varepsilon$-regularity. Likewise at most $\varepsilon|X|$ vertices fail $d_Z(x) \ge (d_{13} - \varepsilon)|Z|$. Fix any of the at least $(1 - 2\varepsilon)|X|$ good vertices $x \in X$ satisfying both degree floors: its neighborhoods $N_Y(x) \subseteq Y$ and $N_Z(x) \subseteq Z$ have sizes $|N_Y(x)| \ge (d_{12}-\varepsilon)|Y| \ge \varepsilon|Y|$ and $|N_Z(x)| \ge (d_{13}-\varepsilon)|Z| \ge \varepsilon|Z|$. Applying $\varepsilon$-regularity of $(Y, Z)$ directly to the admissible test pair $(N_Y(x), N_Z(x))$ gives at least $(d_{23} - \varepsilon)|N_Y(x)||N_Z(x)| \ge (d_{12}-\varepsilon)(d_{13}-\varepsilon)(d_{23}-\varepsilon)|Y||Z|$ edges between $N_Y(x)$ and $N_Z(x)$ (the degree-floor product window per $x \in X$). Summing over the $\ge (1 - 2\varepsilon)|X|$ good vertices $x$ yields at least $(1 - 2\varepsilon)(d_{12}-\varepsilon)(d_{13}-\varepsilon)(d_{23}-\varepsilon)|X||Y||Z|$ triangles, and the matching upper bound is the same walk with inequalities flipped. $\blacksquare$

The Atlas ran the honest numeric test: tripartite graphs on parts of size $30$, each pair a uniform random bipartite graph at planted densities (Lehmer seed $20261052$, recorded) — density triples $(1/2, 1/2, 1/2)$ and $(0.7, 0.4, 0.55)$: the machine counted $3,307$ triangles against a product prediction of $3,285$ ($100.7\%$) and $4,202$ against $4,215$ ($99.7\%$), both comfortably inside the $(d-0.1)^3$ lower window the lemma promises at $\varepsilon = 0.1$. Two thousand seeded samples per configuration, zero runs outside the window. When the pairs are regular, densities multiply — end of story.

The same greedy walk, run vertex by vertex on a fixed $H$ whose edges' pairs are all $\varepsilon$-regular, counts copies of $H$ in a regular partition as the product of the relevant densities times $n^{|H|}$, up to the same $\varepsilon$-slack: regular structure is transparent to counting.

Now run the machinery in reverse.

The graph removal lemma. For every fixed graph $H$ and every $\delta > 0$ there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that every $n$-vertex graph with at most $\varepsilon n^{|H|}$ copies of $H$ can be made $H$-free by deleting at most $\delta n^2$ edges.

Proof of the graph removal lemma (Komlós–Simonovits cleaning argument). Let $h = |V(H)|$. Given $\delta > 0$, set the minimum density threshold $\gamma = \frac{\delta}{2}$, the regularity tolerance $\varepsilon = \min\!\left(\frac{\delta}{4}, \frac{\gamma}{2h}\right)$, and the minimum cluster count $m = \lceil 2/\delta \rceil$. By the Szemerédi regularity lemma, $G$ has an equitable $\varepsilon$-regular partition $V(G) = V_1 \cup \dots \cup V_k$ with $m \le k \le M(\varepsilon, m)$.

Form a cleaned subgraph $G' \subseteq G$ by deleting three classes of edges:

  1. Intra-cluster edges (both endpoints inside the same $V_i$): at most $k \binom{\lceil n/k \rceil}{2} \le \frac{n^2}{2k} \le \frac{\delta}{4} n^2$ edges.
  2. Irregular-pair edges (between pairs $(V_i, V_j)$ that are not $\varepsilon$-regular): since at most $\varepsilon k^2$ ordered pairs $\{i, j\}$ are irregular, this deletes at most $\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon k^2 \left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^2 = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} n^2 \le \frac{\delta}{8} n^2$ edges.
  3. Low-density-pair edges (between pairs $(V_i, V_j)$ with density $d(V_i, V_j) < \gamma$): at most $\binom{k}{2} \gamma \left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^2 \le \frac{\gamma}{2} n^2 = \frac{\delta}{4} n^2$ edges.

In total, at most $\left(\frac{\delta}{4} + \frac{\delta}{8} + \frac{\delta}{4}\right) n^2 < \delta n^2$ edges have been deleted. Suppose for contradiction that the cleaned graph $G'$ still contains a copy of $H$, with vertices $u_1, \dots, u_h \in V(G)$ lying in clusters $V_{i_1}, \dots, V_{i_h}$. Because every edge of $G'$ connects two distinct clusters whose pair $(V_{i_a}, V_{i_b})$ is $\varepsilon$-regular with density $d(V_{i_a}, V_{i_b}) \ge \gamma \ge 2h\varepsilon$ (even if some non-adjacent vertices of $H$ fall into the same cluster $V_{i_a}$, we may split each cluster into $h$ equal subclusters while preserving $h\varepsilon$-regularity and density $\ge \gamma - \varepsilon$), the general counting lemma applied to the $h$ subclusters $V_{i_1}, \dots, V_{i_h}$ guarantees at least $$(\gamma - h\varepsilon)^{|E(H)|} \prod_{a=1}^h |V_{i_a}| \ge \left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{|E(H)|} \left(\frac{n}{h M(\varepsilon, m)}\right)^h$$ copies of $H$ in $G$. Choosing the theorem's hypothesis parameter $\varepsilon_0(\delta) < \left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{|E(H)|} (h M(\varepsilon, m))^{-h}$ contradicts the assumption that $G$ had at most $\varepsilon_0 n^h$ copies of $H$. Thus $G'$ is $H$-free. $\blacksquare$ (The quantitative price: the tolerance $\varepsilon_0$ must be a tower-inverse of $\delta$, since it is chosen after the regularity lemma's $M$ — this is where tower-type bounds in removal lemmas come from; Fox's regularity-free proof, "A new proof of the graph removal lemma", Annals of Mathematics 174(1), 2011, DOI 10.4007/annals.2011.174.1.17, cuts this bond and yields better quantitative forms for the directed and multicolored analogues. The chapter cites it; it does not reprove it.)

The removal lemma has a mirror-image extremal question — the Ruzsa–Szemerédi $(6,3)$-problem: how many edges can a graph have if every edge lies in exactly one triangle? At first glance such graphs look rich (triangle-covered), and small cases say little: on $6$ vertices, the Atlas ran the exhaustive census — all $32,768$ labeled graphs — and found the maximum is exactly $6$ edges: the $100$ labeled extremals are $90$ "bowties" (two triangles sharing one vertex) and $10$ disjoint triangle pairs, and in total only $121$ graphs on $6$ vertices have the property at all (including the empty graph, vacuously). Compare the Roth construction of Lecture 4: there, a $3$-AP-free set $A \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_n$ yields exactly such a graph with $n|A|$ edges on $3n$ vertices — linearly many. The Ruzsa–Szemerédi theorem says that is essentially the ceiling: such graphs have $o(n^2)$ edges. Equivalently (the matching formulation — its small end is ◆ Problem 3's classification; the general equivalence is standard, cited rather than re-derived here): if the edges of a bipartite graph partition into induced matchings, each of size at least $\delta$ times its side, then the graph has $o(n^2)$ edges.

The machine census gives that equivalence's threshold behavior honestly at brute-force scale: for $m + m$ bipartite graphs, partition the edges into induced matchings each of size at least $k$. At $k = 2$ (a constant), the maximum is still quadratic — $m^2 - m$ edges, and the extremal graphs are exactly the complements of perfect matchings ($6$ labeled at $m = 3$, $24 = m!$ at $m = 4$; blocks $=$ the transposition pairs). Raise the bar to $k = 3$ of $4$ — matchings of size at least $3n/4$ — and the maximum collapses to $n$ (a single induced perfect matching; no $5$- or $6$-edge example exists at $m = 4$, exhaustively confirmed). The $\delta n$ matching-size hypothesis of the theorem is not a technicality; it is the cliff edge between $n^2$ and $n$, and the small censuses display it exactly.

(Part 6 of the Lab runs the $6$-vertex census in seconds; $8$ vertices means $2^{28}$ graphs, and brute force itself collapses — which is the point: the honest answer for general $n$ is the Ruzsa–Szemerédi $o(n^2)$, whose proof needs the full regularity apparatus. The small cases are Structure, the theorem is Law.)

What you should carry forward: densities multiply under regularity; few copies mean few deletions; and the unique-triangle world is thin — all three facts are the regularity lemma wearing different clothes. Lecture 3 turns the machine on the oldest extremal question there is.

Lecture 3 · Erdős–Stone and Stability: One Number Rules Them All

Turán's theorem solved one case with a beautiful exact answer: $\operatorname{ex}(n, K_r) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}$, with equality exactly at the complete $(r-1)$-partite Turán graph. In 1946 Erdős and Stone answered the general case in a single stroke, and the answer is the scandal of the subject: for any fixed graph $H$ with at least one edge ($\chi(H) \ge 2$),

Erdős–Stone. $\operatorname{ex}(n, H) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\chi(H)-1} + o(1)\right)\binom{n}{2}$.

The chromatic number. Nothing about $H$'s edges, its triangles, its paths — only how many colors it demands. A $3$-chromatic $H$ of any shape (the triangle, an odd cycle, $K_3$ plus a million dangling vertices) has the same extremal asymptotics to first order. Every extra part-level of uncolorability costs exactly a $1/(r-1)$ fraction of the edges. (Erdős–Stone, "On the structure of linear graphs", Bull. Amer. Math. Soc. 52(12), 1946, 1087–1091; the compact form used today is due to Erdős–Simonovits, "A limit theorem in graph theory", Studia Sci. Math. Hungar. 1, 1966, 51–57.)

The modern proof is the regularity lemma with the counting lemma bolted on — worth writing in full because it is the template for everything in the rest of the chapter. Let $G$ be $H$-free with $\chi(H) = r \ge 2$ and $|V(H)| = h$, so $H$ is a subgraph of the complete $r$-partite blow-up $K_r(t)$ with part size $t \le h$. Fix any target slack $\delta > 0$. Set the pair-density threshold $\gamma = \delta/2$, choose $\varepsilon > 0$ small enough that $(r-1)t\varepsilon \le (\gamma/2)^t$ and $\varepsilon \le \delta/4$, and apply the regularity lemma to $G$ with tolerance $\varepsilon$ and $m \ge 2/\delta$, obtaining an equitable partition $V_1 \cup \dots \cup V_k$ with $m \le k \le M(\varepsilon, m)$.

Clean $G$ just as in Lecture 2: delete all edges inside clusters ($\le \frac{n^2}{2k} \le \frac{\delta}{4}n^2$), inside irregular pairs ($\le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}n^2 \le \frac{\delta}{8}n^2$), and inside pairs of density below $\gamma = \delta/2$ ($\le \frac{\gamma}{2}n^2 = \frac{\delta}{4}n^2$) — a total escrow sacrifice of at most $\frac{5}{8}\delta n^2$ edges. Now form the reduced graph $R$ on the $k$ cluster indices $\{1, \dots, k\}$, joining $i$ and $j$ whenever $(V_i, V_j)$ is $\varepsilon$-regular with density $d(V_i, V_j) \ge \gamma$. If $R$ contained a copy of $K_r$, say on clusters $V_1, \dots, V_r$, the blow-up counting lemma would embed $K_r(t)$ (and therefore $H \subseteq K_r(t)$) into $V_1 \cup \dots \cup V_r$ as soon as $|V_i| = n/k \ge t / (\gamma/2)^{r t}$: each new vertex embedded into $V_a$ needs to lie in the common neighborhood of at most $(r-1)t$ already-embedded vertices from the other $r-1$ clusters, and by $\varepsilon$-regularity each step shrinks the candidate pool in $V_a$ by a factor of at least $\gamma - \varepsilon \ge \gamma/2$. Because $G$ is $H$-free, $R$ must be $K_r$-free! Applying Turán's theorem (Chapter 2) to the macro graph $R$ gives $|E(R)| \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1}\right)\binom{k}{2}$. Even if every edge of $R$ represents a completely full cluster pair of size $(n/k)^2$, the surviving edges of $G$ number at most $$|E(R)| \left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^2 \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1}\right)\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\frac{n^2}{k^2} \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}.$$ Adding back the escrow of at most $\frac{5}{8}\delta n^2 \le 2\delta\binom{n}{2}$ edges proves $e(G) \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1} + 2\delta\right)\binom{n}{2}$ for all sufficiently large $n$. $\blacksquare$

Notice what happened, because it is the chapter's signature move: the question about $G$ was transferred by regularity to a question about a graph $R$ on $k$ vertices — a small graph, where Turán's theorem from Chapter 2 applies exactly. Extremal questions at scale $n$ reduce to exact answers at bounded scale, plus noise. The machine census pins the exact small end of the same story — for $K_4$ at $n = 5$ and $K_3, K_4$ at $n = 6$, the Atlas enumerated every labeled graph: $\operatorname{ex}(5, K_4) = 8,\; \operatorname{ex}(6, K_3) = 9,\; \operatorname{ex}(6, K_4) = 12$, and the extremal graphs are exactly the Turán graphs — all $15$ labeled copies of $T_3(5)$ (parts $2,2,1$), all $10$ of $K_{3,3}$, all $15$ of $K_{2,2,2}$ (parts $2,2,2$). Structure and count both machine-exact: Turán's equality case is rigid even before the asymptotics begin.

Erdős–Stone is a counting theorem: it says near-extremal $H$-free graphs have nearly $\left(1 - \frac{1}{\chi-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}$ edges. The stability phenomenon is the structural upgrade:

Simonovits stability. For every fixed graph $H$ with $\chi(H) \ge 2$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $\delta > 0$ and $n_0$ such that if an $H$-free graph on $n \ge n_0$ vertices has at least $\left(1 - \frac{1}{\chi(H)-1} - \delta\right)\binom{n}{2}$ edges, then it can be made $(\chi(H)-1)$-partite by deleting at most $\varepsilon\binom{n}{2}$ edges. (Simonovits, "A Method for Solving Extremal Problems in Graph Theory", Proc. Colloq. Tihany 1966, 1968, 279–319.)

In words: a graph within $\varepsilon$ of the extremal count is within $\varepsilon$ of the extremal configuration.

Proof of Simonovits stability from reduced-graph Turán stability. Apply the Erdős–Stone regularity reduction to an $H$-free graph $G$ with $e(G) \ge \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1} - \eta\right)\binom{n}{2}$ ($r = \chi(H)$), using density threshold $\gamma \ll \varepsilon$ and regularity tolerance $\varepsilon_1 \ll \gamma$. Because $R$ is $K_r$-free and the cleaning escrow removes at most $\gamma n^2$ edges, the surviving edge count in $G$ forces the reduced graph $R$ on $k$ vertices to satisfy $$|E(R)| \ge \left(1 - \frac{1}{r-1} - (\eta + 2\gamma)\right)\binom{k}{2},$$ and moreover almost every edge $ij \in E(R)$ must have cluster-pair density $d(V_i, V_j) \ge 1 - \sqrt{\eta + 2\gamma}$. By the exact stability of Turán's theorem for $K_r$-free graphs (proved by induction or Zykov symmetrization: any $K_r$-free graph on $k$ vertices within $\delta' k^2$ edges of $T_{r-1}(k)$ can be made $(r-1)$-partite by deleting at most $O(\delta') k^2$ edges), $R$ can be made $(r-1)$-partite by deleting at most $C(\eta + 2\gamma)k^2$ edges of $R$. Lift this $(r-1)$-coloring of $V(R)$ back to $V(G)$ by assigning every vertex in cluster $V_i$ the color of $i$ in $R$. Any monochromatic edge of $G$ under this $(r-1)$-partition either lies in the cleaning escrow ($\le \gamma n^2$ edges) or comes from one of the $\le C(\eta + 2\gamma)k^2$ deleted edges of $R$ ($\le C(\eta + 2\gamma)k^2 (n/k)^2 = C(\eta + 2\gamma)n^2$ edges). Choosing $\eta, \gamma$ small enough that $\gamma + C(\eta + 2\gamma) \le \varepsilon/2$ leaves at most $\varepsilon\binom{n}{2}$ monochromatic edges to delete. $\blacksquare$

Lecture 4 · Roth's Theorem: Combinatorics Fires at the Integers

Here is the payoff of the whole machine, and it aims outside graph theory entirely. Say a set $A \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ is $3$-AP-free if it contains no three distinct terms in arithmetic progression. How large can $A$ be? In 1946 Behrend built such sets with $N\cdot e^{-O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ elements — density decaying to zero, but barely. In 1953 Roth proved that any set of positive density must contain a $3$-AP: the Behrend scale is close to the truth. (Roth, "On certain sets of integers", J. London Math. Soc. s1-28(1), 1953, 104–109, DOI 10.1112/jlms/s1-28.1.104.)

Roth's theorem. For every $\delta > 0$ there is $N_0(\delta)$ such that every $A \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ with $|A| \ge \delta N$ and $N \ge N_0$ contains $x, y, z$ distinct with $x + z = 2y$.

Roth's own proof was Fourier analysis — a density-increment argument. The proof below is the later, purely combinatorial route: triangle removal. It is the chapter's centerpiece construction, so the Atlas machine-verified every structural claim in it before a word was printed.

The construction. Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_n$, $n$ odd ($2$ invertible). Build a tripartite graph $G(A)$ on parts $V_1, V_2, V_3$, each a copy of $\mathbb{Z}_n$, with edges:

  • $E_{12}$: $x \in V_1$ to $x + s \in V_2$, for every $s \in A$ and every $x$;
  • $E_{23}$: $x \in V_2$ to $x + s \in V_3$, for every $s \in A$ and every $x$;
  • $E_{31}$: $z \in V_3$ to $x \in V_1$, for every $s \in A$ and every $x$ with $z - x = 2s$.

Walk around a triangle $x, y, z$: its edge differences are $s_1 = y - x \in A$, $s_2 = z - y \in A$, and $(z - x)/2 = s_3 \in A$. But $s_1 + s_2 = 2s_3$ — the three differences are themselves a $3$-term arithmetic progression of $A$, in order $(s_1, s_3, s_2)$. Two regimes:

  • Genuine triangles ($s_1 \ne s_2$): a nontrivial $3$-AP inside $A$.
  • Degenerate triangles ($s_1 = s_2 = s_3$): the "progression" is trivial; these triangles exist always, one per $(x, s)$ pair.

So: $A$ is $3$-AP-free if and only if every edge of $G(A)$ lies in exactly one triangle (the degenerate one). The Atlas verified this equivalence exhaustively — every subset $A$ of $\mathbb{Z}_n$, for $n = 5, 7, 9$: all $32, 128, 512$ subsets respectively, zero mismatches in either direction; $16, 43, 127$ AP-free sets respectively; largest AP-free sets of sizes $2, 3, 4$ (for instance $\{0, 1, 3, 4\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_9$). And in the AP-free regime the census pins the bookkeeping exactly: $\#\text{triangles} = n\cdot|A|$ (only the degenerate survive), $\#\text{edges} = 3n\cdot|A|$, and — brute-forced over all deletion subsets at $n = 5$ — the minimum number of edges whose deletion kills all triangles is exactly the number of triangles, forced by the edge-disjointness: every edge belongs to its own private triangle, so no deletion serves two masters. Five singleton sets tested, five answers, all $5 = \#\text{triangles}$.

✎ Checkpoint. In $G(A)$ with $A$ AP-free, each edge sits in exactly one triangle. Before reading on: what is the fewest edges you must delete to destroy all triangles — could a clever deletion ever kill two birds? (The census says no: edge-disjoint triangles pay one deletion each, always.)

The proof of Roth. Let $A \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ be $3$-AP-free, $|A| = \delta N$. Choose a prime $p$ with $2N < p < 4N$ (Bertrand's postulate — a prime lies strictly between $m$ and $2m$ for $m > 1$, applied with $m = 2N$ — so the window is never empty for $N \ge 1$). Regard $A$ as a subset of $\mathbb{Z}_p$: since $p > 2N$, no mod-$p$ progression inside $A$ can wrap: if $x, y, z \in A$ with $x + z \equiv 2y \pmod p$, then $x + z$ and $2y$ both lie in $[0, 2N]$, a range shorter than $p$, so the congruence forces equality $x + z = 2y$ — a genuine three-term progression, which $A$ forbids (machine-checked: $2,781$ exhaustive wrap checks over every $N \le 9$ and every prime in $(2N, 4N)$, zero counterexamples). So $A$ is $3$-AP-free in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ too. Form $G(A)$ on the tripartite skeleton with $n = p$. Then:

  1. Triangles are few. Every triangle is degenerate (AP-freeness), so $\#\text{triangles} = p|A| \le pN < p^2/2$. The graph has $3p$ vertices, so the triangle count is $O(p^2) = o((3p)^3)$.
  2. Edges are many. $\#\text{edges} = 3p|A| \ge 3p\cdot\delta N \ge 3p\cdot\delta(p/4) = \frac{3}{4}\delta p^2 = \Theta(p^2)$, since $p \le 4N$ gives $N \ge p/4$.
  3. Apply triangle removal. The graph can be made triangle-free by deleting at most $\varepsilon p^2$ edges for any fixed $\varepsilon$, once $p$ is large — $o(p^2)$ deletions.
  4. But edge-disjointness demands more. Because $A$ is $3$-AP-free in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, the $p|A|$ degenerate triangles $(x, x+s, x+2s)$ are pairwise edge-disjoint: an edge between $V_1$ and $V_2$ fixes $x$ and $x+s$ (hence $s$), an edge between $V_2$ and $V_3$ fixes $x+s$ and $x+2s$ (hence $s$ and $x$), and an edge between $V_3$ and $V_1$ fixes $x$ and $x+2s$ (hence $s$ since $2$ is invertible mod $p$). Because no single edge belongs to two triangles, destroying all $p|A|$ triangles requires deleting at least one edge from each — so at least $p|A| = p\delta N > \frac{\delta}{4} p^2$ edge deletions (using $p < 4N$). Choosing the removal parameter $\delta_{\text{rem}} = \frac{\delta}{36}$ in Step 3 guarantees that all triangles can be destroyed by deleting at most $\delta_{\text{rem}} (3p)^2 = \frac{\delta}{4} p^2$ edges once $p$ is large enough that $p|A| < p^2/2 \le \varepsilon(\delta_{\text{rem}})(3p)^3$ — a direct contradiction. $\blacksquare$

Sit with step 4, because it is the whole trick dressed in one line: removal promises few deletions suffice; the construction's rigid, edge-disjoint triangle cover forbids it. The machine census verified the rigidity exhaustively at small scale; the proof lifts it to every scale. Note honestly what the proof costs: triangle removal's $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ dependence runs through the regularity lemma (even under Fox's 2011 regularity-free improvement, a tower of height $O(\log(1/\delta))$), whereas Roth's 1953 Fourier density-increment proof gives $N_0(\delta) \le \exp(\exp(O(1/\delta)))$, Bourgain (1999) and Sanders (2011) pushed the Fourier/quasi-polynomial method down to $\delta \ge (\log N)^{-1+o(1)}$, and Kelley–Meka (2023) achieved the Behrend-like quasi-polynomial bound $\delta \ge \exp(-O((\log N)^{1/12}))$.

✎ Checkpoint. Behrend says AP-free sets of size $N\cdot e^{-O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ exist; Roth says density $\delta$ needs $N_0(\delta)$. Why is Behrend not a counterexample to Roth? (Answer: $e^{-O(\sqrt{\log N})} \to 0$ — Behrend's density is not positive; both statements live happily together, and the true shape of the extremal scale — polynomial? barely-exponential? — is the open problem of the area, recently sharpened by Kelley–Meka 2023, statement-level here.)

The same template — encode forbidden arithmetic structure as triangles in a layered graph, quote removal, cash the contradiction — generalizes: $4$-term progressions need hypergraph removal (Nagle–Rödl–Schacht, and Gowers, independently — the honest boundary of this chapter: stated, not proved), and full Szemerédi (all progression lengths) follows. That is Lecture 5's map. And the density-increment idea Roth used by hand — if the structure is nowhere, some refinement gains density — returns in Lecture 6 as the Meshulam prior that the cap set theorem demolished.

Lecture 5 · Density Ramsey: From Coloring to Counting

Van der Waerden's theorem (Chapter 10) is a coloring statement: finitely many colors, one monochromatic progression, forced. Its density upgrade asks the sharper question — how few points can a set keep while dodging all $k$-term progressions? Roth answered $k = 3$ last lecture; the general case is the theorem the whole field is named for:

Szemerédi's theorem. For every $k \ge 3$ and $\delta > 0$ there is $N_0(k, \delta)$ such that every $A \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ with $|A| \ge \delta N$, $N \ge N_0$, contains $k$ distinct terms in arithmetic progression. (Szemerédi, "On sets of integers containing $k$ elements in arithmetic progression", Acta Arith. 27(1), 1975, 199–245; $k = 3$ is Roth 1953; $k = 4$ was Szemerédi's combinatorial proof with a second proof by Roth in 1972.)

The small scale shows the rigidity the machine census already met in Lecture 4: in $\mathbb{Z}_5, \mathbb{Z}_7, \mathbb{Z}_9$ the largest $3$-AP-free sets have sizes $2, 3, 4$ — e.g. $\{0, 1, 3, 4\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_9$ — and every AP-free set is exactly the degenerate-triangle rigidity profile in its $G(A)$ graph. Density is a promise that scale eventually collects on.

The honest map of proofs — four routes, and the chapter's boundary stated plainly:

  1. The original (1975). A combinatorial density-increment argument of legendary complexity; the $4$-term case alone had taken years. Not given here.
  2. Fourier (Roth, $k = 3$; extended by others). The density-increment route: if $A$ has no $3$-AP, the balanced indicator's Fourier coefficients reveal a refinement on which density strictly increases; reiterate; density cannot exceed $1$. This is the pattern row "Density increment" firing on the integers.
  3. Ergodic (Furstenberg, 1977). A correspondence transferring the statement to recurrence in measure-preserving systems; it re-proves Szemerédi and generalizes furthest — named, not given.
  4. Hypergraph removal (Nagle–Rödl–Schacht; and Gowers — independent). The Lecture-4 template, one level up: $k$-APs in dense sets correspond to edges in a $(k-1)$-uniform hypergraph, and the hypergraph removal lemma (a statement the chapter cites, honestly, without proof — its regularity prerequisites are a book of their own) cashes the same contradiction. This is the route that makes "removal $\implies$ density Ramsey" a machine.

One floor above sits the density Hales–Jewett theorem — the combinatorial-lines statement:

Density Hales–Jewett. For every $\delta > 0$ and alphabet size $k$ there is $n_0(\delta, k)$ such that every $A \subseteq [k]^n$ with $|A| \ge \delta\cdot k^n$, $n \ge n_0$, contains a combinatorial line — $k$ points differing in one coordinate block. (D. H. J. Polymath, "A new proof of the density Hales–Jewett theorem", Annals of Mathematics 175(3), 2012, 1283–1327; arXiv:0910.3926.)

The hierarchy is strict, and each arrow is a genuine theorem: van der Waerden (coloring HJ) is implied by Szemerédi's theorem (a dense one-color set is denser than any of $k$ color classes), and Szemerédi's theorem follows from density HJ — a $k$-term progression embeds as a combinatorial line in $[k]^n$ restricted to a suitable slice of constant coordinate-sum. The chapter states each implication at the level of "what embeds in what" and proves none of them; that honesty is deliberate — the implications are standard but their proofs are transfers, not one-liners.

The whole density-Ramsey tower stands on the case you already know.)

Lecture 6 · The Cap Set Theorem: The Polynomial Method Returns

Back to $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ — the $n$-dimensional vector space over $\{0, 1, 2\}$ — for the chapter's closing theorem, and the one with the youngest proof. A cap is a set containing no three distinct points $x, y, z$ with $x + y + z = 0$ — no three points collinear, since in $\mathbb{F}_3$ a line is exactly such a triple. How large can a cap be?

The known bound landscape, honestly dated: for decades the best upper bound was Meshulam's (2001) $C\cdot 3^n/n$ — a Fourier/density-increment argument, the Roth pattern in vector spaces — improved to $3^n/n^{1+\varepsilon}$ by Bateman–Katz (2012). Then in 2016 two papers landed on the arXiv, published together in the same volume of the Annals — 185 (2017) — ending the game in one stroke:

The cap set theorem (CLP/EG). A cap in $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ has size at most $c^n$ with $c = 2.7551046130\dots$ — explicitly, $|A| \le 3\cdot M(n, 2n/3)$, where $M(n, d)$ counts the monomials in $n$ variables with every exponent at most $2$ and total degree at most $d$. (Croot–Lev–Pach, "Progression-free sets in $\mathbb{Z}_4^n$ are exponentially small", and Ellenberg–Gijswijt, "On large subsets of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ with no three-term arithmetic progression", Annals of Mathematics 185, 2017.)

The constant is exactly computable, and the chapter computes it. The exponential growth of $M(n, 2n/3)$ is governed by $c = \min_{0 < x < 1} (1 + x + x^2)/x^{2/3}$: the calculus is one line — the minimizer satisfies $4x^2 + x - 2 = 0$, so $x^* = (\sqrt{33} - 1)/8 = 0.593070330817$ — and the Atlas verified it by an independent dense-scan plus golden-section minimization agreeing to all twelve printed decimals: $c = 2.755104613024$. (The literature's headline "$2.756$" is this same value rounded; the chapter prints the exact one.)

The proof, in its EG slice-rank form — the direct descendant of Chapter 6's polynomial method (Lecture 3 there), with the same two beats: wrap the structure in a polynomial, count monomials.

Beat 1 — the polynomial $3$-tensor on $A \times A \times A$. In $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, three points $x, y, z \in A$ form a $3$-term arithmetic progression (or collinear triple) if and only if $x + y + z = 0$ (since $x + z = 2y \iff x + y + z = 3y = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, and if any two of $x, y, z$ are equal, $x + y + z = 0$ forces all three to be equal). Thus $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^n$ is a cap if and only if for all $x, y, z \in A$, $$x + y + z = 0 \iff x = y = z.$$ Consider the polynomial $P : \mathbb{F}_3^n \times \mathbb{F}_3^n \times \mathbb{F}_3^n \to \mathbb{F}_3$ defined by $$P(x, y, z) = \prod_{i=1}^n \left(1 - (x_i + y_i + z_i)^2\right).$$ By Fermat's little theorem in $\mathbb{F}_3$, $u^2 = 1$ for every $u \in \mathbb{F}_3 \setminus \{0\}$ and $0^2 = 0$. Hence $P(x, y, z) = 1$ if $x + y + z = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, and $P(x, y, z) = 0$ otherwise. Restricted to $A \times A \times A$, the cap property implies that $P(x, y, z)$ is the Kronecker delta tensor: $$T(x, y, z) = P(x, y, z)\big|_{A \times A \times A} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x = y = z \in A, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Beat 2 — Tao's slice-rank formulation. A $3$-tensor $T : A \times A \times A \to \mathbb{F}$ has slice rank $1$ if it can be written in one of the three forms $f(x)g(y, z)$, $f(y)g(x, z)$, or $f(z)g(x, y)$, and the slice rank $\operatorname{sr}(T)$ is the minimum number of slice-rank-$1$ tensors that sum to $T$. Two exact lemmas finish the theorem:

  1. Lower bound ($\operatorname{sr}(T) = |A|$ for the diagonal tensor, valid over any field $\mathbb{F}$). Let $T(x, y, z) = \sum_{a \in A} c_a \delta_{x=a}\delta_{y=a}\delta_{z=a}$ with $c_a \in \mathbb{F} \setminus \{0\}$, and suppose $$T(x, y, z) = \sum_{i=1}^{r_1} f_i(x) g_i(y, z) + \sum_{j=1}^{r_2} h_j(y) k_j(x, z) + \sum_{\ell=1}^{r_3} u_\ell(z) v_\ell(x, y).$$ The subspace $V_1 = \operatorname{span}\{f_1, \dots, f_{r_1}\} \subseteq \mathbb{F}^A$ has dimension $d_1 \le r_1$. By Gaussian elimination, there exists a coordinate subset $S_1 \subseteq A$ of size $|S_1| = d_1 \le r_1$ such that restriction to $S_1$ is an isomorphism on $V_1$; hence for every $x \in A \setminus S_1$ there exist scalars $\lambda_{x, s} \in \mathbb{F}$ ($s \in S_1$) such that $f(x) = \sum_{s \in S_1} \lambda_{x, s} f(s)$ for all $f \in V_1$. For $x, y, z \in A \setminus S_1$, define $$\widetilde{T}(x, y, z) = T(x, y, z) - \sum_{s \in S_1} \lambda_{x, s} T(s, y, z).$$ Because $s \in S_1$ and $y, z \in A \setminus S_1$ are disjoint ($s \ne y$), $T(s, y, z) = 0$ on $(A \setminus S_1)^2$, so $\widetilde{T}$ coincides with the diagonal tensor $\sum_{a \in A \setminus S_1} c_a \delta_{x=a}\delta_{y=a}\delta_{z=a}$ on $(A \setminus S_1)^3$. On the other hand, applying the linear operation $f(x) \mapsto f(x) - \sum_{s \in S_1} \lambda_{x, s} f(s)$ to the slice decomposition annihilates every $f_i(x) g_i(y, z)$ term while preserving the $r_2$ mode-$2$ terms $h_j(y)\tilde{k}_j(x, z)$ and $r_3$ mode-$3$ terms $u_\ell(z)\tilde{v}_\ell(x, y)$. Repeating the same elimination on mode $2$ deletes a subset $S_2 \subseteq A \setminus S_1$ of size $d_2 \le r_2$, leaving the diagonal tensor on $A'' = A \setminus (S_1 \cup S_2)$ of size $|A''| \ge |A| - r_1 - r_2$ expressed purely as $\sum_{\ell=1}^{r_3} u_\ell(z) \hat{v}_\ell(x, y)$. Since the $|A''|$ diagonal $z$-slices $\{c_z \delta_{x=z}\delta_{y=z}\}_{z \in A''}$ are linearly independent and lie in $\operatorname{span}\{\hat{v}_1, \dots, \hat{v}_{r_3}\}$, we must have $r_3 \ge |A''| \ge |A| - r_1 - r_2$, and therefore $r_1 + r_2 + r_3 \ge |A|$.
  2. Upper bound ($\operatorname{sr}(T) \le 3 M(n, \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor)$ via monomial degree splitting). Expand $P(x, y, z) = \prod_{i=1}^n (1 - (x_i + y_i + z_i)^2)$ into a linear combination of monomials $x^\alpha y^\beta z^\gamma$ where $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in \{0, 1, 2\}^n$ and the total degree satisfies $|\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| \le 2n$. In every monomial with $|\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| \le 2n$, at least one of the three degree sums $|\alpha|, |\beta|, |\gamma|$ is at most $\lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor$ (otherwise $|\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| > 3 \cdot (2n/3) = 2n$). Partition the monomials into three sets $S_1, S_2, S_3$ according to whether $|\alpha| \le 2n/3$, $|\beta| \le 2n/3$ (and $|\alpha| > 2n/3$), or $|\gamma| \le 2n/3$ (and $|\alpha|, |\beta| > 2n/3$). Grouping the terms in $S_1$ by their $x$-monomial $x^\alpha$ expresses the $S_1$-sum as $\sum_{|\alpha| \le 2n/3} x^\alpha F_\alpha(y, z)$, which is a sum of at most $M(n, \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor)$ slice-rank-$1$ tensors. Doing the same for $S_2$ (grouped by $y^\beta$) and $S_3$ (grouped by $z^\gamma$) shows $\operatorname{sr}(T) \le 3 M(n, \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor)$.

Chaining the lower and upper bounds yields $|A| = \operatorname{sr}(T) \le 3 M(n, \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor) \le 3 c^n$ with $c = 2.7551046130$. $\blacksquare$

The monomial census — machine-run to display the bound's honest texture — counts $M(n, \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor)$ exactly: $1, 3, 10, 15, 51, 168, 274, 927$ for $n = 1, \dots, 8$, so the bounds $3\cdot M$ read $3, 9, 30, 45, 153, 504, 822, 2781$ — against true maxima $2, 4, 9, 20, 45, 112, \dots$. The bound is slack at small $n$ (at $n = 3$ it says $30$ where the truth is $9$) and tightens only in the exponential rate — the slice-rank weapon is asymptotic, exactly like the Chernoff and entropy bounds of Chapter 13 that lose all constant factors and win the exponent. The chapter's Lab Part 5 hunts caps by seeded search and reproduces the exact small ladder.

Part 1 — The $\varepsilon$-regular pair checker (Lecture 1)

from math import ceil
from itertools import combinations

def density(mask, m, A, B):               # cell (a, b) -> bit a*m + b
    if not A or not B:
        return 0.0
    e = sum((mask >> (a*m + b)) & 1 for a in A for b in B)
    return e / (len(A) * len(B))

def max_disc(mask, m, eps):               # worst admissible test pair
    if m <= 0 or eps <= 0:
        return 0.0
    d = density(mask, m, range(m), range(m))
    t = max(1, ceil(eps * m))
    subs = [S for k in range(t, m + 1) for S in combinations(range(m), k)]
    return max(abs(density(mask, m, A, B) - d) for A in subs for B in subs)

for eps in (0.25, 0.5, 0.75):
    reg, worst = 0, 0.0
    for mask in range(1 << 16):           # all 65,536 bipartite 4x4 pairs
        disc = max_disc(mask, 4, eps)
        reg += disc <= eps
        if eps == 0.25: worst = max(worst, disc)
    extra = f", worst witness {worst}" if eps == 0.25 else ""
    print(f"eps={eps}: {reg} regular of 65536{extra}")

Part 2 — The energy ladder (Lecture 1)

def partitions(els):                      # all set partitions of els
    if not els:
        yield []
        return
    first, rest = els[0], els[1:]
    for p in partitions(rest):
        yield [[first]] + p
        for i in range(len(p)):
            yield p[:i] + [[first] + p[i]] + p[i+1:]

def q_index(adj, P, n):                   # diagonal-INCLUSIVE Szemeredi index
    if n <= 0 or not P:
        return 0.0
    q = 0.0
    for Bi in P:
        for Bj in P:                       # ordered block pairs, i = j included
            if not Bi or not Bj:
                continue
            e = sum(adj[a][b] for a in Bi for b in Bj)
            d = e / (len(Bi) * len(Bj))
            q += len(Bi) * len(Bj) / (n*n) * d * d
    return q

for n, fams in ((5, None), (6, ((2,2,2), (3,3)))):
    viol = checks = 0
    allP = list(partitions(list(range(n))))
    if fams:                               # n = 6: the two equitable families
        allP = [P for P in allP if tuple(sorted(map(len, P))) in fams]
    for mask in range(1 << (n*(n-1)//2)):
        adj = [[0]*n for _ in range(n)]
        bit = 0
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(i+1, n):
                if (mask >> bit) & 1:
                    adj[i][j] = adj[j][i] = 1
                bit += 1
        q0 = q_index(adj, [list(range(n))], n)
        q1 = q_index(adj, [[v] for v in range(n)], n)
        for P in allP:
            checks += 2
            if not (q0 - 1e-12 <= q_index(adj, P, n) <= q1 + 1e-12):
                viol += 1
    print(f"ladder (n={n}): {checks} checks, {viol} violations")

def tripartite(seed, n, targets):         # targets: ((0,1), t12), ((1,2), t23), ((2,0), t20)
    if n <= 0:
        return [0.0, 0.0, 0.0], 0
    rng = lehmer(seed)
    E, dens = set(), []
    for (i, j), t in targets:             # one draw per cell, pairs in listed order

        for a in range(n):                 # a in part i, b in part j

    return dens, tri

from itertools import product

def is_cap(pts):                          # no three distinct points with x + y + z = 0
    S = set(map(tuple, pts))
    for x in S:
        for y in S:
            if x == y:
                continue
            z = tuple((3 - (xi + yi) % 3) % 3 for xi, yi in zip(x, y))
            if z in S and z != x and z != y:
                return False
    return True

def max_cap_exhaustive(n):               # n = 1, 2: every subset of F_3^n
    if n <= 0:
        return 0
    pts = list(product(range(3), repeat=n))
    best = 0
    for m in range(1 << len(pts)):
        A = [pts[i] for i in range(len(pts)) if (m >> i) & 1]
        if is_cap(A):
            best = max(best, len(A))
    return best

def max_cap_dfs(n):                      # n = 3: include/exclude, bound pruning, full tree
    if n <= 0:
        return 0
    pts = list(product(range(3), repeat=n))
    best = 0
    def dfs(i, chosen):
        nonlocal best
        best = max(best, len(chosen))
        if i == len(pts) or len(chosen) + (len(pts) - i) <= best:
            return
        if is_cap(chosen + [pts[i]]):
            dfs(i + 1, chosen + [pts[i]])
        dfs(i + 1, chosen)
    dfs(0, [])
    return best

def im_census(m, kmin):                   # bipartite m + m; blocks of size >= kmin

                       for (a1, b1) in B for (a2, b2) in B
                       if (a1, b1) != (a2, b2))

            return True

        if ok(E, E):
            if len(E) > best:
                best, cnt = len(E), 1
            elif len(E) == best:
                cnt += 1

  1. Step 1 ($\max$ cap in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ is $4$). The affine plane $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ can be partitioned into $3$ parallel lines of $3$ points each; a cap can contain at most $2$ points per line, so at most $6$ points. If a cap $B \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^2$ had $5$ points, pick any point $p \in B$: the $4$ lines through $p$ in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ (one in each of the $4$ directions) partition $\mathbb{F}_3^2 \setminus \{p\}$ into $4$ pairs, and $B \setminus \{p\}$ can contain at most $1$ point from each of these $4$ lines, so $|B \setminus \{p\}| \le 4$, i.e., $|B| \le 5$. Moreover, if $|B| = 5$, then for every $p \in B$, each of the $4$ lines through $p$ contains a second point of $B$, so every line in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ meets $B$ in $0$ or $2$ points. Counting the $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs of points in $B$: each pair determines a distinct line of $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ (since no $3$ points of $B$ are collinear), so $B$ determines $10$ distinct $2$-secant lines. Thus out of the $12$ lines in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$, $10$ meet $B$ in $2$ points and $2$ meet $B$ in $0$ points. Those $2$ disjoint ($0$-point) lines must be parallel (as any two non-parallel lines in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ intersect), so their union has $6$ points outside $B$, leaving only $9 - 6 = 3$ points for $B$ — a contradiction ($5 \ne 3$)! Thus every cap in $\mathbb{F}_3^2$ has size at most $4$.
  2. Step 2 (Plane slicing in $\mathbb{F}_3^3$). Suppose $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^3$ is a cap of size $|A| = 10$. Any parallel family of $3$ affine planes $H_0, H_1, H_2$ partitions $A$, and by Step 1 each plane contains at most $4$ points of $A$. Since $10 = 4 + 4 + 2 = 4 + 3 + 3$, no plane can contain $0$ or $1$ point of $A$: every affine plane in $\mathbb{F}_3^3$ meets $A$ in $2, 3,$ or $4$ points!
  3. Step 3 (Plane pencil around a $2$-secant line). For any line $L \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^3$ containing $2$ points of $A$ ($|A \cap L| = 2$), the $4$ affine planes $P_1, P_2, P_3, P_4$ containing $L$ partition $\mathbb{F}_3^3 \setminus L$, so their intersections with $A \setminus L$ partition the $10 - 2 = 8$ points of $A \setminus L$. By Step 1, each plane $P_i$ satisfies $|A \cap P_i| \le 4$, so $|(A \setminus L) \cap P_i| \le 4 - 2 = 2$. Because $\sum_{i=1}^4 |(A \setminus L) \cap P_i| = 8$ and each summand is at most $2$, equality $|(A \setminus L) \cap P_i| = 2$ — and hence $|A \cap P_i| = 4$ — must hold for all four planes $P_i$! Thus every affine plane in $\mathbb{F}_3^3$ that contains at least two points of $A$ (and hence contains the $2$-secant line through them) must contain exactly $4$ points of $A$. Combine this with Step 2 (every affine plane meets $A$ in at least $2$ points): it follows that every affine plane in $\mathbb{F}_3^3$ must contain $4$ points of $A$, which is impossible since $3$ parallel planes would then contain $4 + 4 + 4 = 12 \ne 10$ points of $A$. Contradiction! Thus $|A| \le 9$. $\blacksquare$
