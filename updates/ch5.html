Chapter 5 · Matching, Flows & Hall

A lecture course in the spirit of the earlier chapters: the idea before the formula, the story before the proof, and the proof as a conversation. Every method of the reference canon is preserved inside the narrative. The chapter's spine is one algorithmic idea — augment until stuck, then read the stuck state as a certificate — and a family of theorems that all say the same thing: the best you can do equals the least you must pay.

How to read this chapter

Read it slowly, with a pencil. The problems look like personnel work — marry applicants to positions, send each committee its own delegate, push freight through a network — but they are secretly accounting problems. The proofs here rarely construct anything directly; instead they push a procedure until it can no longer move, then interrogate the obstruction. The obstruction always turns out to be a witness that nothing better was possible. That is the aesthetic of the whole subject: the algorithm's failure mode is the theorem's tightness.

Notation. A bipartite graph has parts $X$ (applicants) and $Y$ (positions), edge $xy$ meaning " $x$ is qualified for $y$ ". A matching is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex; it saturates $X$ if every $x \in X$ is matched. $N(S)$ is the set of neighbors of $S$; $\nu(G)$ is the maximum matching size, $\tau(G)$ the minimum vertex cover size, $\alpha(G)$ the maximum independent set size. In networks: capacities $c$, a flow $f$ with value $|f|$, an $s$–$t$ cut $(S, V\setminus S)$ with capacity $c(S, V\setminus S)$.

The exercises are graded: ●○○ easy (a direct application of one lecture idea), ●●○ moderate (two ideas, or one tricky step), ●●● tough (several chapter ideas combined). Hints travel with the problems; worked answers sit at the very end of the chapter. Try honestly before reading a hint. Beyond the graded set, ◆ pattern-unknown problems name no method and carry no hint: diagnosing which tool fits is part of the problem. And ◐ Explorations at the end are open-ended investigations with no single right answer — research training, not test preparation.

Lecture 1 · The Matchmaker's Theorem

A matchmaker has applicants $X$ and positions $Y$. Each applicant is qualified for some subset of the positions. She wants to place everyone — each applicant in a distinct position they are qualified for. When can she?

The obvious obstruction: if $k$ applicants are jointly qualified for fewer than $k$ positions, they cannot all be placed — $k$ people cannot sit in fewer than $k$ chairs. Call $S \subseteq X$ any set of applicants and $N(S)$ the positions they are collectively qualified for. The obstruction says: some $S$ with $|N(S)| \lt |S|$. Hall's Marriage Theorem (1935) says this obvious obstruction is the only one:

Theorem. A matching saturating $X$ exists if and only if every $S \subseteq X$ has $|N(S)| \ge |S|$.

The necessity direction is the observation above (a matching pairs $S$ into distinct members of $N(S)$). The miracle is sufficiency — and the proof below is worth memorizing not for the theorem but for the architecture: it is the master example of an induction that would naively die, and is saved by splitting into regimes.

1.1 The induction that almost works — and its two rescue regimes

Try induction on $|X|$ the naive way: marry one applicant $x$ to one position $y$, delete both, and appeal to induction on the rest. The catch: deleting $y$ may wound someone else. Applicant $x'$ might have been qualified only for $y$; the reduced graph no longer satisfies Hall's condition, and the induction hypothesis refuses to load.

✎ Checkpoint. Before reading on: find a tiny example where marrying $x$ to $y$ ruins the Hall condition for the survivors. (One applicant qualified only for $y$ does it.) Then ask: what extra property would make the greedy marriage safe?

There are exactly two ways the greedy step can be safe, and together they cover everything.

Regime 1 — slack everywhere. Suppose every nonempty proper $S \subsetneq X$ has $|N(S)| \ge |S| + 1$: there is a spare position in every neighborhood. Marry $x$ to any neighbor $y$. For $T \subseteq X - \{x\}$, its neighborhood in the reduced graph is $N(T) - \{y\}$ at worst: $|N'(T)| \ge |N(T)| - 1 \ge |T| + 1 - 1 = |T|$. Hall survives the deletion; induction finishes. ✓

Regime 2 — a tight set exists. Suppose some nonempty proper $S$ has $|N(S)| = |S|$ exactly — a perfectly tight block of applicants and positions. Then split the problem in two, and note that the split is clean:

  • Match $S$ into $N(S)$ by induction. Hall's condition holds inside: for $T \subseteq S$, its neighborhood within $N(S)$ is $N(T)$ itself ($T \subseteq S$ forces $N(T) \subseteq N(S)$), so $|N(T) \cap N(S)| = |N(T)| \ge |T|$. ✓
  • Match $X - S$ into $Y - N(S)$ by induction. For $T \subseteq X - S$, use Hall for $T \cup S$ in the original graph: the neighbors of $T$ outside $N(S)$ number at least $|N(T \cup S)| - |N(S)| \ge |T \cup S| - |S| = |T|$. ✓
  • The two matchings use disjoint vertices ($S \cup N(S)$ versus its complement); their union saturates $X$. ∎

If neither regime holds — no nonempty proper $S$ is tight and none has slack — the case cannot actually occur: Hall's inequalities are integer-valued, so for every nonempty proper $S$ with $|N(S)| \ge |S|$, exactly one of $|N(S)| \ge |S| + 1$ (slack, Regime 1) or $|N(S)| = |S|$ (tight, Regime 2) holds. The two regimes exhaust all cases (with $|X| \le 1$ as the trivial base). Hall's theorem is proved. (Pattern P4: the critical-set split — tight somewhere ⇒ solve both halves recursively; slack everywhere ⇒ greed is safe — reappears in matroid theory, in the Gallai–Edmonds structure theorem, and wherever a min–max theorem hides inside an induction.)

1.2 The deficiency form — how many can you place?

Hall is all-or-nothing. The refined question: if Hall fails, how many applicants can still be placed? Define the deficiency of $S$ as $|S| - |N(S)|$ and let $d = \max$ over all $S$ of the deficiency (at least $0$, achieved by $S = \emptyset$).

Theorem (deficiency form). The maximum number of $X$-vertices that can be simultaneously matched is exactly $|X| - d$.

Two directions, and the hard one is sneaky-easy. No matching beats $|X| - d$: take $S^*$ with deficiency $d$. Any matching places the members of $S^*$ into distinct members of $N(S^*)$ — at most $|N(S^*)| = |S^*| - d$ of them — so at least $d$ applicants of $S^*$ go unplaced. Some matching achieves $|X| - d$: add $d$ dummy positions, each acceptable to every applicant. The padded graph satisfies Hall: for any $S \subseteq X$, its padded neighborhood has size $|N(S)| + d \ge |N(S)| + (|S| - |N(S)|) = |S|$, since $S$'s deficiency never exceeds $d$. So Hall places all of $X$; at most $d$ of the placements use dummies (there are only $d$ of them); the remaining $\ge |X| - d$ placements are real. Delete the dummy edges. ∎ (Pattern P11 in a new costume: when a hypothesis fails by $d$, pad with $d$ universal options and it holds.)

Check it on the lab's E2 (four applicants: $x0$ and $x1$ are both qualified only for $y0$; $x2$ is qualified only for $y1$; and $x3$ is qualified for nothing). The worst set is $S^* = \{x0, x1, x3\}$: three applicants, one position — deficiency $2$. The theorem promises $|X| - d = 4 - 2 = \mathbf{2}$, and indeed a maximum matching marries two applicants ($x1$–$y0$, $x2$–$y1$, say). The lab confirms this by brute force.

What you now own: the two-regime induction (P4), the deficiency form and its padding proof (P11), and the reflex of asking how far a theorem's hypothesis can bend before it snaps.

Lecture 2 · A Theorem You Can Run

Hall tells you when everyone can be placed. This lecture's theorem tells you the exact size of the best partial placement — and its proof is a machine: run the machine, and the machine's stopping point is the tightness witness.

König–Egerváry Theorem (1931). In a bipartite graph, the maximum matching size $\nu$ equals the minimum vertex cover size $\tau$. (A vertex cover is a set of vertices touching every edge.)

2.1 The free inequality, then the engine

First the direction that costs nothing: $\tau \ge \nu$. Take a minimum cover $C$ and any matching $M$. The edges of $M$ share no vertices, so each one needs its own member of $C$: an injection from $M$-edges into $C$. Counting the same world two ways (P2) gives $|M| \le |C|$ for every matching and every cover. Equality is the theorem — and equality is where the work is.

Now the engine (P16 + P18). Take any matching $M$ — we will improve it until it cannot be improved. Call an $X$-vertex free if $M$ leaves it unmatched. An $M$-alternating path starts at a free $X$-vertex and alternates edges not-in-$M$, in-$M$, not-in-$M$, … Let $Z$ be everything reachable from the free $X$-vertices by alternating paths. Two outcomes:

  • A free $Y$-vertex is reachable. Then the alternating path to it has one more non-matching edge than matching edges: flip the path (non-$M$ edges become $M$-edges and vice versa). The matching grows by exactly one edge. Augment. Repeat.
  • No free $Y$-vertex is reachable. Then $M$ is maximum, and $Z$ is about to pay us for the trip. Define

$$C = (X \setminus Z) \cup (Y \cap Z)$$

and claim: $C$ is a vertex cover with $|C| = |M|$. If true, we have exhibited a cover of the matching's size — the free inequality then forces $\nu = \tau$ and certifies that $M$ was maximum. Three verifications, each a line:

Cover. Could an edge $xy$ go from $x \in X \cap Z$ to $y \in Y \setminus Z$? The path reaching $x$ ends with a matching edge or nothing — either way $x$ can traverse the non-matching edge $xy$, which would put $y$ in $Z$. Contradiction; so every edge either has its $X$-end in $X \setminus Z \subseteq C$, or crosses from $X \cap Z$ into $Y \cap Z \subseteq C$ and is covered by its $Y$-end. ✓

Parity of the matching. Every $M$-edge has exactly one end in $C$. Its $Y$-end $y$ enters $Z$ only via a non-matching edge; if $y \in Z$, the alternating rule immediately pulls $y$'s partner $x$ into $Z$ — so $y \in Z$ forces $x \in Z$. Conversely if $x \in Z$ is matched, $x$ entered $Z$ via its matching edge from $y$ — so $x \in Z$ forces $y \in Z$. Matched pairs stand or fall together in $Z$; $C$ keeps exactly one of each pair ($X \setminus Z$ keeps $x$ when both fall in $Z$; $Y \cap Z$ keeps $y$ when both stay out). ✓

Size. Every $C$-vertex is matched: $X \setminus Z$ contains no free vertex (free $X$-vertices all start in $Z$), and a $Y$-vertex of $Z$ sits at the end of an alternating path from a free $X$-vertex — if it were unmatched, that path would be augmenting, and we are in the "stuck" case. Since each matched pair contributes exactly one $C$-vertex and matching pairs are disjoint, $|C| = |M|$. ✓

The machine halts (each augmentation grows $|M|$, which is bounded), and on halting it prints the cover $C$. $\nu = \tau$, proved by an algorithm whose failure mode is the tightness certificate. This is pattern P18 in its purest form, and it is constructive in both directions: the matching you cannot improve and the cover you cannot shrink are the same information.

✎ Checkpoint. Run the engine by hand on E2 with $M = \{x1$–$y0, x2$–$y1\}$: the free applicants are $x0$ and $x3$; the alternating reachability set is $Z = \{x0, x3, y0, x1\}$; the certificate is $C = (X \setminus Z) \cup (Y \cap Z) = \{x2\} \cup \{y0\} = \mathbf{\{x2, y0\}}$ — a cover of size $2 = |M|$. The machine, run on three edges, just told you exactly which two vertices witness the optimum.

A remark for later. The set $C$ came in a strange dress: $X$-vertices outside the reachable set, $Y$-vertices inside it. File that shape; Lecture 3 will produce the same cover by pure thought, and Lecture 4 will produce it as a cut. Three costumes, one theorem — that is the next lecture's whole point.

What you now own: alternating reachability (P16), augment-or-certify (P18), the free-inequality reflex (P2), and the shape $(X\setminus Z) \cup (Y\cap Z)$.

Lecture 3 · Three Costumes, One Theorem

Sit back from the proofs and look at what was actually proved. Three theorems that look like different subjects:

In the König and max-flow rows the two quantities are equal; in the Hall row they sum — placed + deficiency $= |X|$, i.e. $\nu_X = |X| - d$, which is exactly the deficiency form of L1.2 — and each row is equivalent to the other rows. This is pattern P17 at full power: whenever you suspect a min–max theorem, first secure the trivial weak inequality, then hunt the alternating-path (or reachability) certificate for equality. The certificate is never far from the greedy procedure's stuck state.

Let us close the dictionary explicitly, because the translations are theorems in their own right.

3.1 König ⇒ Hall

Assume König. Given Hall's condition, show $\tau \ge |X|$: then $\nu \ge |X|$ means a matching saturating $X$ exists. Take any vertex cover $C$ and let $S = X \setminus C$. Every edge from $S$ must be covered by its $Y$-end, so $N(S) \subseteq C \cap Y$: $|C \cap Y| \ge |N(S)| \ge |S| = |X| - |C \cap X|$. Rearranged, $|C| = |C \cap X| + |C \cap Y| \ge |X|$. Every cover has size $\ge |X|$, so $\nu = \tau \ge |X|$: a maximum matching saturates $X$. ∎

3.2 Hall ⇒ König

Assume Hall — hence (by L1.2's padding proof, which used nothing but Hall) the deficiency form. Let $d$ be the maximum deficiency and $S^*$ a worst set; the deficiency form gives $\nu = |X| - d$. Now build a cover of exactly that size:

$$C = (X \setminus S^*) \cup N(S^*).$$

It covers every edge (edges out of $S^*$ land in $N(S^*)$; every other edge is covered by its $X$-end), and $|C| = |X| - |S^*| + |N(S^*)| = |X| - d = \nu$. With the free inequality $\tau \ge \nu$, this cover is minimum: $\tau = \nu$. ∎

Look at the shape of $C$ once more: $X$-vertices outside a special set, $Y$-vertices inside its neighborhood. It is the $(X\setminus Z) \cup (Y\cap Z)$ of Lecture 2 with $Z$ replaced by $S^*$ — the same cover wearing its static (worst-set) and dynamic (reachable-set) costumes. When two proofs of one fact produce the same object by different routes, you have found the theorem's true face.

3.3 What the dictionary cannot do

The honest frontier. Hall and König live in bipartite graphs because alternating paths there have a rigid side-structure. In a general graph, an alternating path can wander back to its own side — an odd cycle — and the Lecture-2 search stalls: the parity-labeled alternating walk can report "no augmenting path" while one exists. It is worth being precise about what does and does not survive the crossing. Berge's lemma (all graphs): a matching $M$ is maximum if and only if no $M$-augmenting path exists — bipartiteness plays no role. (If a larger matching $M'$ exists, the components of $M \triangle M'$ are alternating cycles and alternating paths; since $|M'| \gt |M|$, some path component carries one more $M'$-edge than $M$-edge — and that is an $M$-augmenting path.) What genuinely fails beyond bipartite graphs is the bookkeeping: a search that permanently labels vertices "$X$-side" or "$Y$-side" can miss an augmenting path hiding behind an odd alternating cycle, and then falsely reports failure. The repairs exist and are deep:

  • Tutte's theorem (1947, statement): $G$ has a perfect matching iff for every $U \subseteq V$, the number of odd components of $G - U$ is at most $|U|$.
  • Edmonds' blossom algorithm (1965, statement): contract odd alternating cycles ("blossoms") and the alternating-path engine works again — the first polynomial-time matching algorithm and the birth of polyhedral combinatorics.

Both are beyond this chapter's scope; the ◐ exploration at the end sends you to the exact wall the bipartite proofs lean on.

What you now own: the dictionary (Hall ⇄ König ⇄ max-flow), the two translation proofs, and a named reason the bipartite world is special.

Lecture 4 · The Freeway Theorem

Now the third costume in full: flows. A network is a directed graph with a source $s$, a sink $t$, and a capacity $c(e) \ge 0$ on each edge. A flow assigns each edge a value $f(e)$ with $0 \le f(e) \le c(e)$ so that at every vertex except $s$ and $t$, inflow = outflow (conservation). Its value $|f|$ is the net flow leaving $s$. An $s$–$t$ cut is a partition $(S, V\setminus S)$ with $s \in S$, $t \notin S$; its capacity is the total capacity of edges from $S$ to $V\setminus S$.

Max-flow min-cut theorem. The maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity.

4.1 The free inequality, then the engine

Value $\le$ every cut's capacity. By conservation, the value of $f$ equals the net flow across any $s$–$t$ cut: $|f| = \sum_{u\in S, v\notin S} f(u,v) - \sum_{u\notin S, v\in S} f(u,v)$ (telescope the vertex-by-vertex conservation over $S$ — P2 again). The first sum is at most the cut's capacity; the second is at most $0$ — well, it is subtracted, and it is $\ge 0$ since $f \ge 0$. So $|f| \le c(S, V\setminus S)$. ∎ Equality for some flow and some cut simultaneously is the theorem.

The engine (P18, third appearance): push flow greedily along $s$–$t$ paths that still have room, and when no path has room, the stuck state is the minimum cut. To say "room" properly, work in the residual network: for each edge $u\to v$ with capacity $c$ and current flow $f$, the residual capacity forward is $c - f$, and — this is the twist that makes everything work — the residual capacity of the reverse edge $v\to u$ is $f$ (pushing flow backward is how the algorithm changes its mind, rerouting earlier greedy choices). If the residual network has an $s$–$t$ path, push its bottleneck amount (the minimum residual capacity along it): the flow value rises. If it has none:

Stuck ⇒ the reachable set is a saturated cut. Let $Z$ be the residual-reachable vertices from $s$; $t \notin Z$. Every edge $u\to v$ from $Z$ to $V\setminus Z$ has residual $0$ (else $v$ would be reachable): $f(u,v) = c(u,v)$ — saturated. And every edge $v\to u$ from $V\setminus Z$ into $Z$ carries $f(v,u) = 0$: if it carried flow, the residual reverse edge $u\to v$ would have positive capacity and pull $v$ into $Z$. So in the net-flow-across-the-cut formula, the first sum is the full cut capacity and the second sum vanishes: $|f| = c(Z, V\setminus Z)$. The stuck flow equals the cut it exposes; the free inequality says nothing beats that cut and no flow beats this one. Both are optimal. ∎

Termination, honestly. With integer capacities, each augmentation pushes at least $1$ (the bottleneck is a positive integer, and residuals stay integers), and the value is bounded above by, say, the capacity of the cut $\{s\}$ alone. So the engine halts after finitely many augmentations — $\le$ the min-cut value of them, in fact. With arbitrary real capacities the greedy engine can chase an infinite sequence of shrinking bottlenecks and never finish (a famous four-vertex example with capacities built from the golden ratio does exactly that — a literature landmark, stated here without proof). The Edmonds–Karp refinement — always augment along a shortest residual path — terminates in polynomial time for all capacities (statement). The lab runs the greedy engine and counts its augmentations: on small networks it stops in 2–3 rounds.

Integrality, and König falls out. Integer capacities ⇒ the final flow is integral (it was built by integer pushes). Specialize: bipartite $G$, capacity $1$ on every $s\to x$ and every $y\to t$ edge, capacity $\infty$ on every $x\to y$ edge. Integral flows ↔ matchings (each $x$ receives $\le 1$ from $s$, each $y$ ships $\le 1$ to $t$, so the $x\to y$ edges used form a matching; value = size). Finite cuts ↔ vertex covers: a cut of finite capacity cannot cut any $\infty$ edge, so for every edge $xy$ the cut takes $x$ (via $s\to x$) or $y$ (via $y\to t$) — a cover — and conversely every cover cuts exactly its own size. Max-flow min-cut is König–Egerváry, and Hall follows by L3.1. The dictionary closes. ∎

Predict the min cut before running: there are four cuts to price — $\{s\}$, $\{s,a\}$, $\{s,b\}$, $\{s,a,b\}$. The lab says the value is $5$; find all three cuts that achieve it.

Lecture 5 · Delegates, Dispatchers, and the Flow Compiler

The machinery is built; this lecture cashes it in across three classic applications — and states honestly where the open problems begin.

5.1 Committees and delegates: SDR

$n$ committees, each a subset $A_i$ of a pool of people; each committee must send its own delegate, and no person delegates twice. A system of distinct representatives (SDR) is exactly a matching saturating the index side of the bipartite graph $i \leftrightarrow$ (people in $A_i$). Hall translates verbatim:

Theorem. An SDR exists iff every subcollection of the committees has a union at least as large as the subcollection itself.

Warm-up, by hand: committees $\{a,b\}$, $\{b,c\}$, $\{a,c\}$ — every pair's union has size $3 \ge 2$, the triple's union has $3 \ge 3$; an SDR exists: $a, b, c$ respectively. Failure, by hand: four committees all equal to $\{a,b\}$: the full collection has union $2 \lt 4$ — no SDR, and at most two committees can be served (L1.2's deficiency form says exactly two, $d = 4 - 2$).

This innocuous translation is load-bearing: Latin squares are built row by row out of SDRs (the ◆ exercise 18 asks you to prove it), and Dilworth's theorem for partially ordered sets is König in a mirror (exercise 11).

5.2 Schedules: k-regular bipartite graphs decompose

A k-regular bipartite graph — equivalently, a 0-1 matrix with exactly $k$ ones in every row and every column — models a schedule between two equal-sized sets in which every vertex has exactly $k$ commitments. Such a graph splits into $k$ perfect matchings:

Theorem. Every $k$-regular bipartite graph is the disjoint union of $k$ perfect matchings.

Proof. For $k = 0$ there is nothing to decompose, so assume $k \ge 1$. A $k$-regular bipartite graph satisfies Hall by double counting (P2): $k|S|$ edges leave $S$, each $Y$-vertex absorbs at most $k$ of them, so $|N(S)| \ge |S|$; and $k|X| = k|Y|$ — divide by $k \ge 1$ — gives $|X| = |Y|$, so Hall's matching saturates both sides — a perfect matching. Delete it: the remainder is $(k-1)$-regular. Induct on $k$ (P4). ∎

Three lines, and every scheduling problem that reduces to "k ones per row and column" falls with it — exam timetables, round robins, parallel-processor assignment. The lab's cousin verification enumerated every $k$-regular bipartite graph on small sides and split each into $k$ perfect matchings, zero failures.

5.3 Where the frontier begins

Two statements to carry as flags, honestly labeled:

  • Dilworth's theorem (1950, proved in the exercise): in a finite poset, the maximum antichain equals the minimum chain cover. It is König's theorem on the "comparability split graph" — exercise 11 builds the translation.
  • Menger's theorem (1927, exercised): the maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint $s$–$t$ paths equals the minimum $s$–$t$ vertex separator. It is max-flow min-cut with vertices split into in/out copies of unit capacity — exercise 9.

And one genuinely open problem, stated so you know the ground is not all paved: a full transversal of a Latin square — $n$ cells, no two in one row or column, holding all $n$ symbols — need not exist (already at order $2$ both squares fail: each diagonal repeats a symbol), so the honest question is partial. The Brualdi–Stein conjecture — the standing form — asks whether every Latin square of order $n$ has a partial transversal of size at least $n - 1$ ($n - 1$ cells in distinct rows, distinct columns, holding distinct symbols); even this is open in general. The SDR pattern you just learned is the engine of every partial result.

The flow compiler, stated as a reflex. When a problem says matching, covering, dispatch, scheduling, representatives, disjoint paths — write the small network first. Hall, König, Menger, Dilworth, and the $k$-regular decomposition are each one network away; the art is choosing vertices and capacities so that conservation means what you want it to mean.

What you now own: SDR as matching-in-disguise, the $k$-regular decomposition and its double-counting seed, the Dilworth/Menger flags, and the compiler reflex.

def edmonds_karp(cap, s, t):
    n = len(cap)
    r = [row[:] for row in cap]
    flow, augs = 0, 0
    while True:
        parent = [-1] * n
        parent[s] = s
        q = [s]
        while q and parent[t] == -1:
            u = q.pop(0)
            for v in range(n):
                if parent[v] == -1 and r[u][v] > 0:
                    parent[v] = u
                    q.append(v)
        if parent[t] == -1:
            return flow, augs
        aug = float("inf")
        v = t
        while v != s:
            u = parent[v]
            aug = min(aug, r[u][v])
            v = u
        v = t
        while v != s:
            u = parent[v]
            r[u][v] -= aug
            r[v][u] += aug
            v = u
        flow += aug
        augs += 1

def min_cut_brute(cap, s, t):
    n = len(cap)
    best = float("inf")
    for U in range(1 << n):
        if not (U >> s & 1) or (U >> t & 1):
            continue
        c = sum(cap[u][v] for u in range(n) if U >> u & 1
                          for v in range(n) if not (U >> v & 1))
        best = min(best, c)
    return best

Part 3 — Hopcroft–Karp: shortest augmenting paths, in phases

Part 2's compiler pays one full search per augmenting edge — $\nu$ searches in the worst case. Hopcroft–Karp (1973) batches the work: each phase runs one BFS that layers the alternating forest by distance, then one DFS sweep commits a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths. Each phase strictly lengthens the shortest augmenting path, and once that length passes $\sqrt{V}$, vertex-disjointness caps how many augmentations can remain — $O(\sqrt{V})$ phases in total, the engine behind the $O(E\sqrt{V})$ bound. The lab returns $(\nu, \text{phases})$:

def hopcroft_karp(adj, nX, nY):
    """One BFS per phase layers the alternating forest; the phase's
    DFS then commits a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest paths."""
    matchX, matchY = [-1] * nX, [-1] * nY
    dist = [-1] * nX
    dist_nil = [-1]                      # BFS level of a free Y vertex
    def dfs(u):
        if u == -1:                      # NIL reached: a free Y vertex
            return True
        need = dist[u] + 1
        for v in adj[u]:
            w = matchY[v]
            if ((dist_nil[0] if w == -1 else dist[w]) == need
                    and dfs(w)):
                matchX[u], matchY[v] = v, u
                return True
        dist[u] = -1                     # dead end: pruned for this phase
        return False
    phases = 0
    while True:
        for x in range(nX):
            dist[x] = -1
        dist_nil[0] = -1
        queue = [x for x in range(nX) if matchX[x] == -1]
        for x in queue:
            dist[x] = 0
        qi = 0
        while qi < len(queue):
            u = queue[qi]; qi += 1
            if dist_nil[0] != -1 and dist[u] >= dist_nil[0]:
                continue                 # stop layering past the free level
            for v in adj[u]:
                w = matchY[v]
                if w == -1:
                    if dist_nil[0] == -1:
                        dist_nil[0] = dist[u] + 1
                elif dist[w] == -1:
                    dist[w] = dist[u] + 1
                    queue.append(w)
        if dist_nil[0] == -1:            # stuck: the matching is maximum
            break
        phases += 1
        for x in range(nX):
            if matchX[x] == -1:
                dfs(x)
    return sum(1 for x in matchX if x != -1), phases

A7. (a) A vertex cover's complement is an independent set (an edge inside the complement would be uncovered): $\alpha = n - \tau$; König gives $\tau = \nu$, so $\alpha = n - \nu$. (b) From a maximum matching $M$ (size $\nu$), cover the $\nu$ matched vertices with $M$'s own edges and each unmatched vertex with any one incident edge (no isolated vertices!): total $\nu + (n - 2\nu) = n - \nu$ edges — the upper bound. For the lower bound, take any edge cover $F$ and shrink it to an inclusion-minimal one: now every component of the spanning graph $(V, F)$ is a star (a single edge counts as a two-vertex star) — if a component contained a path of three edges, the middle edge's endpoints would each be covered by another of its edges and it could be dropped; if it contained a cycle, any edge could be dropped. A star forest on $n$ vertices with $c$ components has $|F| = n - c$ edges, and one edge chosen from each star is a matching of size $c = n - |F|$. So $\nu \ge n - |F|$, i.e. $|F| \ge n - \nu$. Split every vertex $v$ into $v_{\mathrm{in}} \to v_{\mathrm{out}}$: capacity $1$ for internal $v$, capacity $\infty$ for $s$ and $t$; take source $s_{\mathrm{out}}$ and sink $t_{\mathrm{in}}$ (the unused copies $s_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{out}}$ may be omitted). An integral flow of value $k$ decomposes into $k$ $s$–$t$ paths plus cycles; discard cycles. Since every internal $v_{\mathrm{in}} \to v_{\mathrm{out}}$ has capacity $1$, at most one path uses $v$, so the paths are internally vertex-disjoint. Conversely, $k$ internally vertex-disjoint paths give an integral flow of value $k$. Thus max flow $=$ max number of internally vertex-disjoint $s$–$t$ paths. Now finite cuts and separators correspond. If $U$ is a set of internal vertices whose deletion separates $s$ from $t$, let $R$ be the vertices reachable from $s$ in $G-U$, and define $A = \{s_{\mathrm{out}}\} \cup \{v_{\mathrm{in}},v_{\mathrm{out}} : v\in R, v\notin U\} \cup \{u_{\mathrm{in}} : u\in U \text{ and } u \text{ has a neighbor in } R\}$. No $\infty$ arc leaves $A$: from $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ with $v\in R$, an edge to $w$ either has $w\notin U$, hence $w\in R$ and $w_{\mathrm{in}}\in A$, or $w\in U$, hence $w_{\mathrm{in}}\in A$ by definition. The only arcs leaving $A$ are $u_{\mathrm{in}}\to u_{\mathrm{out}}$ for $u\in U$ with $u_{\mathrm{in}}\in A$, so the cut has capacity at most $|U|$. Hence min cut $\le$ min separator. Conversely, for a finite cut $(A,V\setminus A)$, no $\infty$ arc leaves $A$. Put $U = \{v \text{ internal} : v_{\mathrm{in}}\in A, v_{\mathrm{out}}\notin A\}$. The cut capacity is exactly $|U|$, since only these unit-capacity vertex gadgets cross. If some $s$–$t$ path avoided $U$, then along that path from $s_{\mathrm{out}}\in A$ to $t_{\mathrm{in}}\notin A$ there would be a first arc leaving $A$; it cannot be an $\infty$ original arc, and it cannot be a gadget $v_{\mathrm{in}}\to v_{\mathrm{out}}$ with $v\notin U$ by definition, while $v\in U$ is forbidden. So $U$ separates $s$ from $t$. Thus min separator $\le$ min cut. Therefore max internally vertex-disjoint paths $=$ max flow $=$ min cut $=$ min separator. ∎ Machine check: on all $1,024$ graphs on $5$ vertices, over all $5,120$ unordered (graph, non-adjacent pair) cases ($10,240$ ordered), the split-network flow equaled the brute-force minimum separator every time.

A11. (a) If $x$ were comparable to neither $a$ nor $b$, then $x$ is incomparable to both members of the antichain $\{a,b\}$; since $a$ and $b$ are incomparable, $\{x,a,b\}$ would be an antichain of size $3$, contradicting maximality. Hence every element is comparable to $a$ or to $b$. (b) Build the split graph: left copy $L=\{x\}$, right copy $R=\{x^+\}$, edge $x\to y^+$ iff $x\lt y$. It is bipartite, so König applies. Let $m$ be a maximum matching. Each matched edge $x\to y^+$ means $x\lt y$; each element has at most one matched successor and at most one matched predecessor. Hence the matched edges form a directed forest of paths (a directed cycle would force $x_1\lt x_2\lt \ldots \lt x_1$, impossible), and a forest on $n$ vertices with $m$ edges has $n-m$ components; each component is a chain. Thus the minimum chain cover is at most $n-m$. Conversely, let $C$ be a minimum vertex cover, $|C|=m$ by König. Put $A=\{x: x\notin C \text{ and } x^+\notin C\}$. If $x,y\in A$ and $x\lt y$, then the edge $x\to y^+$ is uncovered, contradiction; so $A$ is an antichain. Since the $m$ cover vertices knock out at most $m$ elements (two copies of one element knock out only that element), $|A|\ge n-m$. Therefore width $\ge |A| \ge n-m$. Every chain cover has size at least width (a chain meets an antichain at most once), so min chain cover $\ge$ width. A12. (a) With integer capacities, every augmentation pushes the bottleneck amount, a positive integer, so at least $1$. Hence the engine halts after at most that many augmentations; in particular it terminates. (b) Take the diamond-plus-cross network with capacities $s\to a=2$, $s\to b=2$, $a\to b=1$, $a\to t=1$, $b\to t=2$. Shortest-first: augment $s\to a\to t$ by $1$, then $s\to b\to t$ by $2$; value $3$ in $2$ augmentations. Bad order: first augment the longer path $s\to a\to b\to t$ by $1$ (bottleneck $\min(2,1,2)=1$); this saturates $a\to b$ and leaves $b\to t$ with residual $1$. Then augment $s\to a\to t$ by $1$, and finally $s\to b\to t$ by $1$. Value $3$ in $3$ augmentations. Thus the bad order pays an extra round because it spends $b\to t$ on a longer path and later cannot send the full $s\to b\to t$ amount. A13. Part (a) — the obstruction. Color the board like a chessboard; a domino covers one square of each color, so any tiling pairs every square with a differently-colored partner. The two removed corners share a color (their row and column offsets add to the same parity) — say black — so the mutilated board has $30$ black squares against $32$ white: the $32$ white squares would need $32$ distinct black partners among the $30$ available. No tiling exists; the count alone is the proof (P2). Part (b) — the companion theorem. An $m\times n$ rectangle with $m, n \ge 2$ and $mn$ even has a Hamiltonian cycle through its squares: run down the first column, then weave back up row by row across the remaining columns (when $m$ is even this closes directly; when $m$ is odd, $n$ is even — weave column by column instead). The cycle alternates colors, because the grid is bipartite. Now delete one black square $b$ and one white square $w$: the cycle breaks into two paths, one from $b$'s successor to $w$'s predecessor, the other from $w$'s successor to $b$'s predecessor. $b$'s cycle-neighbors are white and $w$'s are black, so each path starts at one color and ends at the other — each path alternates and has EVEN length. Pair consecutive squares along each path into dominoes: a tiling exists. This is exactly where (a)'s argument breaks: the count is necessary for a tiling, and on a rectangle with one removal of each color, the snake shows the count is also sufficient — the obstruction in (a) is not "a count is close to balanced" but "the count is off by two." ∎ Machine-checked: every one-of-each-color removal tiles — all $64$ cases on $4\times 4$, $324$ on $6\times 6$, and $1,024$ on $8\times 8$, zero exceptions (and the mutilated board of (a): $30$ black, $32$ white, maximum matching $30$ — two whites must go unpaired).

A15. (a) Let $M^*$ be a maximum matching and $M$ a maximal one. (b) If some edge had neither endpoint in $M$'s endpoint set, it could be added to $M$ — maximality again — so the endpoint set, of size $2|M| \le 2\nu \le 2\tau$, covers every edge. A16. Suppose $M$ is the unique perfect matching. Step 1 — no $M$-alternating cycle: if $C$ were one, flipping along $C$ (replace $M$-edges of $C$ by the non-$M$ edges of $C$) yields a second perfect matching — vertices on $C$ keep exactly one matched edge, vertices off $C$ keep theirs. Step 2 — orient: direct each $M$-edge from $X$ to $Y$, every other edge from $Y$ to $X$. An $M$-alternating cycle is precisely a directed cycle in this orientation, so the digraph is acyclic (P16's alternating bookkeeping, re-cast as directions). An acyclic digraph has a sink — a vertex with no outgoing edge. No $x \in X$ can be a sink (its $M$-edge points out), so the sink is some $y \in Y$; and any non-$M$ edge at $y$ would point out of $y$, so ALL of $y$'s edges are $M$-edges — but $y$ is matched to exactly one $x$, so $y$ has degree $1$, its only edge being its $M$-edge $xy$. A18. The tool is Hall/SDR — but seeing it is the problem. Given an $r \times n$ Latin rectangle with $r \lt n$, extend by one row. For each column $j$, let $A_j$ be the set of symbols not yet used in that column: exactly $n - r$ of them, since the column holds $r$ distinct symbols. Each symbol occurs exactly once in each of the $r$ rows, hence in exactly $r$ distinct columns — so it is missing from exactly $n - r$ columns. Hall for the columns: take any $k$ columns and let $U$ be the union of their missing-symbol sets. Count the pairs (chosen column, symbol it misses): each of our $k$ columns misses exactly $n - r$ symbols, so there are $k(n - r)$ pairs. Every symbol of $U$ is missed by at most $n - r$ columns in the entire rectangle, so it accounts for at most $n - r$ of these pairs: $k(n - r) \le |U|(n - r)$, and since $n - r \gt 0$, $|U| \ge k$. Hall's condition holds, so the columns can be assigned distinct missing symbols — and that assignment is the next row: each column receives one new symbol, and distinctness across columns makes the row a permutation. Repeat, $r \to r + 1$, until $r = n$. ∎ The double count (P2) is the entire proof — no regimes, no case split; the symmetry of "each column misses $n - r$, each symbol is missed by $n - r$" is exactly what makes Hall automatic. A19. The engine inside is Exercise 6's $k$-regular decomposition — but the graph need not be regular. Make it regular by padding (P11). Suppose $\Delta \ge 1$ (for $\Delta = 0$ there are no edges to color). Add isolated dummy vertices to the smaller side until both sides have the same number $N$ of vertices; every degree stays $\le \Delta$. Now give each vertex $v$ exactly $\Delta - d(v)$ unfilled degree slots. The two sides own equally many unfilled slots — each side has $N\Delta$ target slots, and the $m$ real edges fill the same number on each — so pair the left slots with the right slots arbitrarily, adding one edge per pair (parallel added edges are permitted). The result is a $\Delta$-regular bipartite multigraph $H$ containing $G$. Exercise 6's proof runs unchanged in a multigraph: $k|S|$ edges leave $S$ (counted with multiplicity), each $Y$-vertex absorbs at most $k$ of them, so $|N(S)| \ge |S|$; and $k|X| = k|Y|$ with $k = \Delta \ge 1$ gives $|X| = |Y|$, so Hall's matching is perfect. A20. Birkhoff–von Neumann. Let $M$ be doubly stochastic. First, the support graph (rows and columns, positive entries as edges) has a perfect matching by Hall: if a set $S$ of rows had $|N(S)| \lt |S|$ positive-neighbor columns, then summing the entries in the rows of $S$ gives $|S|$, while all those positive entries lie in the columns $N(S)$, whose column sums total $|N(S)| \lt |S|$ — impossible. Choose a permutation matrix $P$ supported on such a perfect matching, and let $\lambda$ be the minimum positive entry of $M$ on the support of $P$. If $\lambda = 1$, then every entry of $M$ on $P$'s support is $1$; since each row and column of $M$ sums to $1$, all off-support entries are $0$, so $M = P$. If $\lambda \lt 1$, set $M' = (M - \lambda P)/(1-\lambda)$. The numerator is entrywise nonnegative, has row and column sums $1-\lambda$, and $M'$ is doubly stochastic; moreover at least one positive entry of $M$ (where $\lambda$ is attained) has become zero, so $M'$ has fewer positive entries than $M$. Induct on the number of positive entries. Unrolling gives $M = \lambda_1 P_1 + \lambda_2 P_2 + \ldots + \lambda_k P_k$ with $\lambda_i \gt 0$ and $\sum \lambda_i = 1$. ∎ Machine check: the peel run to ground on fixed $3\times 3$ and two $4\times 4$ instances — $3$, $4$, and $5$ rounds respectively, weight sums exactly $1$, reconstruction error at machine zero.
