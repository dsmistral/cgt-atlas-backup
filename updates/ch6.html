Chapter 6 · Linear-Algebraic Methods

Part of the Combinatorics & Graph Theory Atlas (see the Atlas canvas for the chapter map and progress report).

The great discovery of 20th-century combinatorics: many counting bounds are secretly dimension bounds. Encode sets as vectors, and "you can't have too many" becomes "vectors can't exceed the dimension of the space". This edition is a lecture course in four lectures — Fisher's inequality two ways, Oddtown and Eventown (where the field chooses the answer), the polynomial method, and the friendship theorem — every proof complete, every claimed number machine-checked. The polynomial method — a one-line proof of Oddtown's descendants and the engine behind the best known Ramsey lower bounds — gets a full lecture of its own, and returns in Chapter 8, running on the Hamming cube. The eigenvalue half of the subject — eigenvalues as averaging machines, spectral Turán, two-distance sets, and the modern toolkit of interlacing, the Hoffman bounds, Lovász theta, and Delsarte's linear program — is the subject of Chapter 8, Spectral Methods and the Modern Toolkit; this chapter closes exactly where that one picks up.

How to read this chapter

Notation. $[n] = \{1, \dots, n\}$; a family $\mathcal{F} = \{A_1, \dots, A_m\}$ of subsets has characteristic ($0/1$) column vectors $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (or $\mathbb{F}_p^n$). $G = (V, E)$ is a simple graph on $n$ vertices with $m$ edges, adjacency matrix $A$, eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$; $d$-regular means every vertex has degree $d$; $J$ is the all-ones matrix, $I$ the identity, $\mathbf{1}$ the all-ones vector, and $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ the hyperplane of vectors with coordinate sum $0$. $\omega$ is the clique number, $\alpha$ the independence number. $\binom{a}{b}$ is the binomial coefficient (with $\binom{a}{b} = 0$ for $b < 0$ or $b > a$); $\mathbb{F}_p$ the field of integers mod $p$; $\|x\|$ the Euclidean norm. Letters are re-stated locally where a lecture needs its own ($m$ in particular is the number of sets in the intersection families of L1–L3).

The exercises are graded: (Easy) easy (a direct application of one lecture idea), (Moderate) moderate (two ideas, or one tricky step), (Tough) tough (several chapter ideas combined). Hints travel with the problems; worked answers sit at the very end of the chapter. Try honestly before reading a hint. Beyond the graded set, (Pattern-Unknown) pattern-unknown problems name no method and carry no hint: diagnosing which tool fits is part of the problem. And (Exploration) Explorations at the end are open-ended investigations with no single right answer — research training, not test preparation.

Lecture 1 · Fisher's Inequality, Two Ways

Problem. $A_1, \dots, A_m$ are distinct subsets of an $n$-element set; every pair intersects in exactly $\lambda$ points ($\lambda \ge 0$); and no $|A_i|$ equals $\lambda$. Then $m \le n$.

Two engines solve this, one getting $m \le n + 1$ and the other closing the last notch. They are worth learning as a pair: the first is the seed of the Frankl–Wilson theory (Lecture 3); the second is the Gram-matrix machine you will reuse all chapter.

1.1 Bose's polynomials — honest, and one short of the goal

To each set $A_i$ associate the polynomial in $n$ variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$ (one per ground element):

$$p_i(x) = \sum_{k \in A_i} x_k - \lambda.$$

Evaluate at the $0/1$ vectors $v_j$: $p_i(v_j) = |A_i \cap A_j| - \lambda$. For $i \ne j$ that is $\lambda - \lambda = 0$; for $i = j$ it is $|A_i| - \lambda \ne 0$. So the evaluation matrix $(p_i(v_j))$ is diagonal with nonzero diagonal entries — invertible — and the $p_i$ are linearly independent.

Where do they live? Each $p_i$ is a linear combination of $1, x_1, \dots, x_n$: a space of dimension $n + 1$. Independence forces

$$m \le n + 1.$$

One notch short — and honestly so: nothing in the argument uses that the coefficients of the $x_k$ in $p_i$ are all $1$. Machine check on the two classical tight examples: the Fano plane's evaluation matrix is $2I$ (each line has $|A| - \lambda = 3 - 1 = 2$ on the diagonal), determinant $2^7 = 128$; the $4$-point near-pencil's is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1, 1, 1)$, determinant $2$.

1.2 The Gram matrix — positive definite, $\operatorname{rank} \le n$, done

Take the characteristic vectors $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and the $m \times m$ matrix of dot products $M = (v_i \cdot v_j) = (|A_i \cap A_j|)$:

$$M = \lambda J + D, \quad \text{where } D = \operatorname{diag}(|A_1| - \lambda, \dots, |A_m| - \lambda),$$

with all diagonal entries $d_i = |A_i| - \lambda > 0$ (since for $m = 1$, $m \le n$ is trivial when $n \ge 1$, and for $m \ge 2$, $|A_i| \ge |A_i \cap A_j| = \lambda$ with $|A_i| \ne \lambda$ forces $|A_i| > \lambda$).

Claim 1: $M$ is positive definite. For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^m \setminus \{0\}$:
$$x^T M x = \lambda \left(\sum_{i=1}^m x_i\right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^m (|A_i| - \lambda)x_i^2.$$
The first term is $\ge 0$ (since $\lambda \ge 0$); the second is $> 0$ because $|A_i| - \lambda > 0$ and at least one coordinate satisfies $x_i \ne 0$. So $x^T M x > 0$: $M$ is positive definite, and $\operatorname{rank} M = m$.

Claim 2: $\operatorname{rank} M \le n$. $M = V V^T$ where $V$ stacks the row vectors $v_i^T$ as rows ($V$ is $m \times n$), and $\operatorname{rank}(V V^T) \le \operatorname{rank} V \le n$.

Combining: $m = \operatorname{rank} M \le n$. $\blacksquare$

Checkpoint — determinants, computed two ways. For $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_m)$ with every $d_i > 0$, the determinant lemma states

$$\det(\lambda J + D) = \det(D)\left(1 + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{1}{d_i}\right).$$

Proof of the determinant lemma. Since $d_i > 0$, $D$ is invertible and $\lambda J + D = D(I + \lambda D^{-1}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T)$. Here $\lambda D^{-1}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$ is a rank-$1$ matrix whose unique potentially nonzero eigenvalue is its trace $\operatorname{tr}(\lambda D^{-1}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T) = \lambda \mathbf{1}^T D^{-1}\mathbf{1} = \lambda \sum_{i=1}^m 1/d_i$, with the remaining $m - 1$ eigenvalues equal to $0$. Adding $I$ shifts the eigenvalues to $1 + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^m 1/d_i$ (once) and $1$ ($m - 1$ times), so $\det(I + \lambda D^{-1}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T) = 1 + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^m 1/d_i$, and multiplying by $\det(D) = \prod_{i=1}^m d_i$ gives the formula. $\blacksquare$

This lemma prices both tight examples with $d_i = |A_i| - \lambda$. Fano plane (seven $3$-point lines on seven points, $\lambda = 1$, $d_i = 2$): $\det = 2^7 \cdot (1 + 7/2) = 128 \cdot 4.5 = \mathbf{576} = 24^2$. Near-pencil on $4$ points (one $3$-point line plus the three $2$-point lines through the fourth point; $D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1, 1, 1)$): $\det = 2 \cdot (1 + 1/2 + 1 + 1 + 1) = \mathbf{9}$. Both check against direct elimination; both families have $m = n$, so Fisher is tight. And Sylvester's criterion runs live on the Fano Gram: its leading principal minors are $3, 8, 20, 48, 112, 256, 576$ — all positive, positive definiteness confirmed without writing a single quadratic form.

1.3 Corollary — de Bruijn–Erdős (P19 applied to geometry)

$n \ge 3$ non-collinear points determine at least $n$ distinct lines.
Proof. Let $\mathcal{L}$ be the set of lines determined by the $n$ points, with $|\mathcal{L}| = N$. For each point $p$, let $A_p \subseteq \mathcal{L}$ be the set of lines passing through $p$. Any two distinct points determine a unique line, so $|A_p \cap A_q| = 1 = \lambda$ for $p \ne q$. Because the $n$ points are not all collinear, no single line contains all $n$ points: for each point $p$, picking a line $\ell \in A_p$ and a point $r \notin \ell$, the line $pr$ is a second line in $A_p$, so $|A_p| \ge 2 \ne \lambda$. In particular, the sets $A_p$ are distinct (if $A_p = A_q$, then $|A_p \cap A_q| = |A_p| \ge 2$, contradicting $|A_p \cap A_q| = 1$). Applying Fisher's inequality to the $n$ sets $A_p \subseteq \mathcal{L}$ gives $n \le |\mathcal{L}| = N$. $\blacksquare$

Checkpoint. Enumerate the linear spaces on $4$ labeled points (any two points on exactly one line, each line of size $\ge 2$, not all $4$ points collinear): there are exactly $5$ — four near-pencils (choose the point off the $3$-point line, giving $4$ lines) and one affine plane (all six pairs as lines, giving $6$ lines). The brute-force census is Exercise 15; every one of the five satisfies $N \ge n$, as de Bruijn–Erdős demands.

What you now own: the evaluation-matrix reflex (a polynomial per set, vanishing on the others) and the Gram template — dot-product pattern $\Rightarrow$ positive definiteness $\Rightarrow \operatorname{rank} = m \Rightarrow m \le n$ — a machine you can retarget at dozens of theorems (Exercises 5, 8, and 11; Chapter 8's two-distance lecture runs it in geometry).

Lecture 2 · Oddtown and Eventown: The Field Chooses the Answer

Problem (Oddtown). A family of subsets of an $n$-set, each of odd size, with even pairwise intersections. Then the family has at most $n$ members.

2.1 Independence over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (P19$'$)

Take characteristic vectors $v_i$ in the vector space $\mathbb{F}_2^n$ — work mod $2$, because the problem is stated in parities. Then
$$v_i \cdot v_i = |A_i| \equiv 1 \pmod 2 \quad \text{(odd)}, \qquad v_i \cdot v_j = |A_i \cap A_j| \equiv 0 \pmod 2 \quad \text{(even) for } i \ne j.$$
Suppose $\sum_{i=1}^m c_i v_i = 0$ is a linear dependence over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Dot with $v_j$: every cross term dies, and the only survivor is $c_j (v_j \cdot v_j) = c_j \cdot 1 = c_j$. So $c_j = 0$ for all $j$: the $v_i$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and $\mathbb{F}_2^n$ has dimension $n$, so $m \le n$. $\blacksquare$

The census confirms the bound is tight: on $[3]$ the maximum Oddtown has $3$ members, on $[4]$ it has $4$ (Exercise 1 — the four $3$-subsets of $[4]$ do it).

2.2 The twist — Eventown, and the field's revenge

If instead all sizes and all intersections are even, the family can have $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ members: partition the ground set into $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ disjoint pairs (leaving one element unused if $n$ is odd) and take all $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ unions of pairs. Every set has even size; every intersection is even. So flipping one parity changes the answer from linear to exponential. The rank argument explains why it must collapse:

Eventown upper bound. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, Eventown's vectors satisfy $v_i \cdot v_j = 0$ for all $i, j$ — including $i = j$, since every $|A_i|$ is even. So the symmetric bilinear form $(x, y) \mapsto x \cdot y$ vanishes on the entire span $W = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}_2}\{v_1, \dots, v_m\} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n$: for $x = \sum_i a_i v_i$ and $y = \sum_j b_j v_j$, $x \cdot y = \sum_{i,j} a_i b_j (v_i \cdot v_j) = 0$. Every vector of $W$ is orthogonal to every vector of $W$, i.e., $W \subseteq W^\perp$. Because the dot product on $\mathbb{F}_2^n$ is nondegenerate, $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = n$, whence $2 \dim W \le n$ and $\dim W \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$. Distinct sets have distinct characteristic vectors inside $W$, so $|\mathcal{F}| \le |W| = 2^{\dim W} \le 2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$. $\blacksquare$ (Exercise 2; census: maximum $2$ on $[3]$, $4$ on $[4]$ — both matching the formula.)

2.3 The same trick over any prime

Nothing is special about $2$. Fix a prime $p$ and a residue $k \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$: if every $|A_i| \equiv k \pmod p$ and every pairwise intersection $|A_i \cap A_j| \equiv 0 \pmod p$, the same dotting argument over $\mathbb{F}_p$ gives $v_i \cdot v_i = k \ne 0$ and $v_i \cdot v_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ — linear independence again, so $m \le n$ for every prime. Machine census for $p = 3, k = 1$ on $[4]$: maximum family size $4 = n$, attained by the singletons. The lesson generalizes: the modulus is not decoration — it is the theorem (Exercise 6).

2.4 One more parity flip — symmetric differences

Suppose instead all pairwise symmetric differences are even: $|A_i \triangle A_j| \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ for all $i, j$. Since $|A \triangle B| = |A| + |B| - 2|A \cap B| \equiv |A| + |B| \pmod 2$, every $|A_i|$ has the same parity — so for $n \ge 1$ the whole family sits inside one parity class of $2^{n-1}$ sets, and that is the sharp bound (Exercise 12; census: maximum $8 = 2^3$ on $[4]$, attained by all the odd sets). Three parity conditions, three answers: $n$, $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$, $2^{n-1}$.

What you now own: the reflex of doing inner products over the field the congruences point to (P19$'$), and the habit of asking how the answer changes with the modulus — the contrast is usually the real theorem.

Lecture 3 · Polynomials That Count: Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson, Frankl–Wilson, and Ramsey Graphs

The evaluation-matrix idea of Lecture 1 was deliberately wasteful: the polynomials $p_i = \sum_{k \in A_i} x_k - \lambda$ lived in a space of dimension $n + 1$, but we never asked what the smallest natural space is. This lecture asks, and the dividends are the strongest intersection theorems known — and, via a construction, quasipolynomial lower bounds for Ramsey numbers.

3.1 The multilinear lemma

On the cube $\{0,1\}^n$ every function $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \mathbb{F}$ (any field $\mathbb{F}$) is given by a unique multilinear polynomial (each variable to degree $\le 1$): $x_i^2 = x_i$ on $\{0, 1\}$ kills higher powers, and the $2^n$ multilinear monomials $x_S = \prod_{i \in S} x_i$ ($S \subseteq [n]$, with $x_\emptyset = 1$) are linearly independent as functions — evaluating $x_S$ at the indicator vectors $1_T$ ordered by nondecreasing size $|T|$ gives $x_S(1_T) = 1$ iff $S \subseteq T$, an upper-triangular matrix with $1$s on the diagonal. So the space of multilinear polynomials of degree $\le s$ has dimension

$$\sum_{i=0}^{s} \binom{n}{i}$$

— one basis monomial $x_S$ per subset $S \subseteq [n]$ of size $|S| \le s$.

3.2 The non-uniform warm-up: Bose again, generalized

Let $A_1, \dots, A_m \subseteq [n]$ be distinct sets whose pairwise intersections all lie in a set $L = \{\ell_1, \dots, \ell_s\}$ of $s$ allowed values, with $|A_i| \notin L$ for all $i$. Define over $\mathbb{R}$

$$f_i(x) = \prod_{\ell \in L} \left(|A_i \cap x| - \ell\right), \quad \text{where } |A_i \cap x| = \sum_{k \in A_i} x_k.$$

Then $f_i(v_j) = 0$ for $i \ne j$ (since $|A_i \cap A_j| \in L$, one factor vanishes) and $f_i(v_i) = \prod_{\ell \in L}(|A_i| - \ell) \ne 0$ (since $|A_i| \notin L$). Expand each $f_i(x)$ and replace every power $x_k^r$ ($r \ge 2$) by $x_k$ to obtain a multilinear polynomial $\tilde{f}_i(x)$ of degree $\le s$ that agrees with $f_i$ on $\{0, 1\}^n$. The evaluation matrix $(\tilde{f}_i(v_j))$ is diagonal with nonzero diagonal entries — invertible — so the $\tilde{f}_i$ are linearly independent in the space of multilinear polynomials of degree $\le s$, giving

$$m \le \sum_{i=0}^{s} \binom{n}{i}.$$

Bose's $m \le n + 1$ is the case $s = 1$ with $L = \{\lambda\}$. Fisher's $m \le n$ (uniformity not required!) is the Gram matrix's extra notch.

3.3 Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson: the slice trick

Theorem (Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson). Let $\mathcal{F} = \{A_1, \dots, A_m\}$ be a family of $k$-subsets of $[n]$ whose pairwise intersections all lie in a set $L \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, k - 1\}$ of size $|L| = s$ (with $s \le k \le n$). Then

$$m \le \binom{n}{s}.$$

Proof. Since $L \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, k - 1\}$, we have $k \notin L$, so the multilinearized polynomials $\tilde{f}_i$ from §3.2 satisfy $\tilde{f}_i(v_i) = \prod_{\ell \in L}(k - \ell) \ne 0$ and $\tilde{f}_i(v_j) = 0$ for $i \ne j$. Thus $\tilde{f}_1, \dots, \tilde{f}_m$ are linearly independent even as functions restricted to $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}$. Now restrict to the slice $\binom{[n]}{k} = \{x \in \{0, 1\}^n : \sum_{j=1}^n x_j = k\}$ where all $v_j$ live. On this slice, any multilinear monomial $x_S = \prod_{i \in S} x_i$ of degree $|S| < k$ can be rewritten as a linear combination of multilinear monomials of degree $|S| + 1$: using $x_j^2 = x_j$ on $\{0, 1\}^n$,

$$k x_S = x_S \sum_{j=1}^n x_j = \sum_{j \in S} x_S x_j + \sum_{j \notin S} x_S x_j = |S| x_S + \sum_{j \notin S} x_{S \cup \{j\}},$$

and since $|S| < k$ we have $k - |S| > 0$, so

$$x_S = \frac{1}{k - |S|} \sum_{j \notin S} x_{S \cup \{j\}}$$

for all $x$ on the slice $\binom{[n]}{k}$. Iterating this substitution for every monomial of degree $0, 1, \dots, s - 1$ (since $s \le k$, every step has $|S| \le s - 1 < k$), every multilinear polynomial of degree $\le s$ coincides on the slice $\binom{[n]}{k}$ with a linear combination of the $\binom{n}{s}$ multilinear monomials of degree exactly $s$. Since $\tilde{f}_1, \dots, \tilde{f}_m$ remain linearly independent as functions on $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\} \subseteq \binom{[n]}{k}$, they span an $m$-dimensional subspace of a function space spanned by $\binom{n}{s}$ monomials, forcing $m \le \binom{n}{s}$. $\blacksquare$

The slice identity is the whole trick: uniformity converts "degree $\le s$" into "degree exactly $s$", saving the sum over lower degrees. Machine tightness checks ($n = 6, k = 3$): for $L = \{0, 2\}$ the maximum family has $4$ members (bound $\binom{6}{2} = 15$); for $L = \{0, 1\}$ it is again $4$ (Exercise 14 closes this one by hand and finds the truth beneath the bound); for $L = \{1, 2\}$ the maximum is $10 = \binom{5}{2}$ — all triples containing a fixed point — the bound $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ is honest but not always attained.

3.4 Frankl–Wilson: Fermat does the bookkeeping (P22 over $\mathbb{F}_p$)

Theorem (Frankl–Wilson, modular form). Fix a prime $p$ and an integer $k$. If $A_1, \dots, A_m \subseteq [n]$ satisfy $|A_i| \equiv k \pmod p$ for all $i$ and $|A_i \cap A_j| \not\equiv k \pmod p$ for all $i \ne j$, then

$$m \le \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{n}{i}.$$

Proof. Work over $\mathbb{F}_p$ and define, for each set $A_i$, the Fermat polynomial

$$p_i(x) = 1 - \left(\sum_{r \in A_i} x_r - k\right)^{p-1}.$$

By Fermat's little theorem in $\mathbb{F}_p$, $t^{p-1} = 1$ if $t \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ and $t^{p-1} = 0$ if $t \equiv 0 \pmod p$ (since $p - 1 \ge 1$). Thus at the characteristic vectors $v_j \in \{0, 1\}^n$, $p_i(v_i) = 1 - (|A_i| - k)^{p-1} = 1 - 0 = 1$ (since $|A_i| \equiv k \pmod p$), while for $i \ne j$, $p_i(v_j) = 1 - (|A_i \cap A_j| - k)^{p-1} = 1 - 1 = 0$ (since $|A_i \cap A_j| \not\equiv k \pmod p$). After multilinearizing via $x_r^2 = x_r$, the evaluation matrix $(\tilde{p}_i(v_j))$ is the $m \times m$ identity matrix $I_m$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so $\tilde{p}_1, \dots, \tilde{p}_m$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{F}_p$. Since $p_i(x)$ has degree $\le p - 1$, each $\tilde{p}_i(x)$ lies in the span of the multilinear monomials $\prod_{r \in S} x_r$ with $|S| \le p - 1$, which has dimension $\sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{n}{i}$. $\blacksquare$

For $p = 2$ the Fermat polynomial is $p_i(x) = 1 - (\sum_{r \in A_i} x_r - k)$: the independence argument is Oddtown's, in disguise — the polynomial method did not replace the linear algebra; it organized it.

3.5 The payoff: explicit Ramsey graphs

Turn the theorem into graphs: for a prime $p$ and integers $1 \le k \le m - 1$, let the vertices be the $\binom{m}{k}$ $k$-subsets of $[m]$ (all of size $\equiv k \pmod p$), joining $A \ne B$ by an edge iff $|A \cap B| \not\equiv k \pmod p$. A clique is then a family of $k$-subsets whose pairwise intersections avoid the residue class $k \bmod p$ — Frankl–Wilson caps it at $\sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{m}{i}$ (or even $\binom{m}{p-1}$ via the slice reduction when $k \ge p - 1$). An independent set is a family of $k$-subsets whose pairwise intersections all lie in $L = \{\ell \in \{0, \dots, k - 1\} : \ell \equiv k \pmod p\}$ — Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson caps it at $\binom{m}{|L|}$ where $|L| = \lfloor k/p \rfloor$. Two worked instances, fully machine-checked:

$p = 2, m = 5, k = 3$. Vertices: the $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ $3$-subsets of $[5]$; join $A \ne B$ iff $|A \cap B|$ is even, i.e. (since distinct $3$-subsets of $[5]$ meet in $1$ or $2$ points) iff $|A \cap B| = 2$. Complementing each vertex ($A \mapsto [5] \setminus A$) turns this into the graph on $2$-subsets of $[5]$ where adjacency means intersecting — the complement of the Kneser graph $KG(5,2)$, better known as the complement of the Petersen graph. The machine finds $\omega = 4$ (a maximum clique is the set of four triples inside a fixed $4$-set, e.g. all triples of $\{2,3,4,5\}$) and $\alpha = 2$. The theory's caps — Frankl–Wilson $\sum_{i \le 1}\binom{5}{i} = 6$ for cliques, RCW $\binom{5}{1} = 5$ for independent sets — are honest but loose here: $6$ and $5$ versus the true $4$ and $2$.

$p = 3, m = 7, k = 4$. Vertices: the $\binom{7}{4} = 35$ $4$-subsets of $[7]$; join $A \ne B$ iff $|A \cap B| \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$ — distinct $4$-subsets of $[7]$ meet in $1, 2,$ or $3$ points, so this joins exactly the pairs meeting in $2$ or $3$ points. Machine: $\omega = 15$ (all $\binom{6}{4} = 15$ $4$-subsets avoiding a fixed point — any two of them share $\ge 2$ points), $\alpha = 2$; caps: Frankl–Wilson $\sum_{i \le 2}\binom{7}{i} = 29$, RCW $\binom{7}{1} = 7$. Again valid, again loose.

Checkpoint — parameters must not degenerate. Try $p = 3, m = 7, k = 5$: two distinct $5$-subsets of $[7]$ meet in $3$ or $4$ points — residues $0$ and $1 \bmod 3$, never $2 \equiv k \pmod 3$. So every pair gets joined: the construction collapses to $K_{21}$ ($\omega = 21, \alpha = 1$) and proves nothing. In general, two distinct $k$-subsets of $[m]$ intersect in sizes ranging over $\{\max(0, 2k - m), \dots, k - 1\}$, an interval of $\min(k, m - k)$ consecutive integers; whenever $\min(k, m - k) < p$ and $k - p < \max(0, 2k - m)$, the residue $k \bmod p$ never occurs among pairwise intersections and the graph degenerates into $K_{\binom{m}{k}}$. Check a purported construction for degeneracy before believing its bounds.

The Ramsey corollary (cited, not proven here). Run the same construction at scale: take $k = p^2 - 1$ (so $k \equiv -1 \pmod p$, and the only intersection sizes $< k$ congruent to $k \pmod p$ are $p - 1, 2p - 1, \dots, p^2 - p - 1$, giving $|L| = p - 1$). Then both the clique number and the independence number are bounded above by $\binom{m}{p-1}$, while the vertex count is $N = \binom{m}{p^2 - 1}$. Choosing $m = p^3$, Frankl and Wilson (1981) obtained explicit graphs with no clique or independent set of size $K = \binom{p^3}{p-1} + 1$ on

$$N = \binom{p^3}{p^2 - 1} = \exp\left(\Omega\left(\frac{\log^2 K}{\log \log K}\right)\right)$$

vertices — a quasipolynomial constructive Ramsey lower bound that stood for a quarter century, until Barak–Rao–Shaltiel–Wigderson (2006) improved it. The two worked graphs above are this construction at toy scale; the full optimization is cited to the papers, flagged as beyond this chapter's scope.

What you now own: the multilinear lemma, the slice trick (uniformity as a dimension discount), Fermat polynomials as bookkeeping, and the reflex of testing a construction's parameters for degeneracy before trusting its bounds.

Lecture 4 · The Friendship Theorem

Problem. If in a simple graph $G$ on $n \ge 3$ vertices every two distinct vertices have exactly one common neighbor, then some vertex is adjacent to all others — and the graph is a "windmill": $t \ge 1$ triangles glued at one central vertex ($n = 2t + 1$).

The proof has three load-bearing parts, each complete here (the middle one was a flagged sketch in the reference literature for years; it is a degree-class argument, and it assembles cleanly).

4.1 Claim 1 — non-adjacent vertices have equal degrees

Claim 1. If $x \not\sim y$ then $d(x) = d(y)$.
Proof. For any two distinct vertices $a \ne b$, denote by $\operatorname{cn}(a, b)$ their unique common neighbor. Let $z = \operatorname{cn}(x, y)$ be the unique common neighbor of the non-adjacent pair $x, y$. The natural idea is to send each $u \in N(x) \setminus \{z\}$ to the unique common neighbor $\alpha(u) = \operatorname{cn}(u, y)$ of the pair $(u, y)$ — but the codomain needs care: $\alpha(u)$ can be $z$ itself. In $W_2$, with $x = a_1, y = a_2, u = b_1$, the pair $(u, y)$ has common neighbor the hub — which is $z$. So $\alpha$ does not in general land in $N(y) \setminus \{z\}$; the argument must excise one vertex from each side, and the excised vertices are forced. The members of $N(x) \setminus \{z\}$ adjacent to $z$ are exactly the common neighbors of the pair $(x, z)$: there is at most one (two would give the pair $(x, z)$ two common neighbors) and at least one (that pair's common neighbor $u_0 = \operatorname{cn}(x, z)$ is adjacent to $x$ and to $z$, and is not $z$ itself) — so exactly one, $u_0 = \operatorname{cn}(x, z) \in N(x) \setminus \{z\}$. Mirror-wise, $v_0 = \operatorname{cn}(y, z)$ is the unique $z$-adjacent member of $N(y) \setminus \{z\}$. Now take $u \in N(x) \setminus \{z, u_0\}$. Then $u \not\sim y$ (else $u \ne z$ would be a second common neighbor of $x$ and $y$), so $u \ne y$ and the pair $(u, y)$ has its unique common neighbor $\alpha(u) = \operatorname{cn}(u, y) \in N(y)$. Moreover: $\alpha(u) \ne z$, because $z \not\sim u$ — $u_0$ is the only $z$-adjacent member of $N(x) \setminus \{z\}$; $\alpha(u) \ne x$, because $x \not\sim y$ while $\alpha(u) \sim y$; and $\alpha(u) \ne v_0$ — if $\alpha(u) = v_0$, then since $v_0 \sim z$ and $\alpha(u) \sim u$, the pair $(u, z)$ would have the two common neighbors $x$ and $v_0$, which are distinct because $x \not\sim y$ while $v_0 \sim y$. Finally, $\alpha$ is injective on $N(x) \setminus \{z, u_0\}$: if $\alpha(u_1) = \alpha(u_2) = w$ with $u_1 \ne u_2$, then $w$ is adjacent to both $u_1$ and $u_2$, and so is $x$ — the pair $(u_1, u_2)$ would have the two common neighbors $x$ and $w$, which are distinct because $x \not\sim y$ while $w \sim y$. Thus $\alpha$ is an injection from $N(x) \setminus \{z, u_0\}$ into $N(y) \setminus \{z, v_0\}$, giving $d(x) - 2 \le d(y) - 2$. The mirror construction (swapping $x$ and $y$, $u_0 \leftrightarrow v_0$) gives an injection the other way, so $d(y) - 2 \le d(x) - 2$: $d(x) = d(y)$. $\blacksquare$ (The census below runs the diagnostic both ways: the un-excised codomain claim fails in every one of the $120$ census graphs that have non-adjacent pairs — $\alpha(u_0) = z$ in each of the $1{,}320$ pairs — while the $u_0 / v_0$ excision is a bijection on all of them.)

Contrapositive, worth enshrining: distinct degrees force adjacency. (Machine: the claim holds in every graph of the census below — $121$ friendship graphs, zero exceptions.)

4.2 Claim 2 — no universal vertex is impossible (the ERS brick)

Claim 2. Every friendship graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices has a universal vertex.
Proof. Suppose not. Since $n \ge 3$ and every pair of vertices has a common neighbor, no vertex has degree $0$, so every degree lies in $\{1, \dots, n - 2\}$. Partition $V$ into degree classes. If some degree class is a singleton $\{x\}$, then every $y \ne x$ has $d(y) \ne d(x)$, so by Claim 1's contrapositive $x$ is adjacent to every other vertex: $d(x) = n - 1$, universal, contradiction. So every nonempty degree class has $\ge 2$ members; in particular, picking any nonempty degree class $B$ (of common degree $k$), $B$ contains at least two distinct vertices $b_1, b_2$.

Now watch the cross-class edges. Every $w \notin B$ has degree $\ne k$, so $w$ is adjacent to every vertex of $B$ (Claim 1's contrapositive). In particular, every $w \notin B$ is a common neighbor of the pair $(b_1, b_2)$ — which has exactly one common neighbor. So $|V \setminus B| \le 1$.

Two cases. If $V \setminus B = \{z\}$: $z$ is adjacent to all of $B$ (distinct degrees), so $d(z) = |B| = n - 1$ — universal, contradiction. If $V \setminus B = \emptyset$: the graph is $k$-regular, and the spectral lemma below (§4.4) forces $k = 2, n = 3$ — the triangle $K_3$, in which every vertex has degree $2 = n - 1$ and is universal. Contradiction again. $\blacksquare$

4.3 Assembly — the universal vertex pins down the windmill

Let $u$ be universal. Put $H = G - u$. If $H$ contained a $3$-vertex path $w\text{–}x\text{–}y$ with $w \ne y$, the pair $(w, y)$ would have common neighbors $x$ and $u$ — two, contradiction. (This also rules out any triangle $w\text{–}x\text{–}y$ in $H$.) So $H$ has maximum degree $\le 1$: a matching plus isolated vertices. But an isolated vertex $z$ of $H$ has $N_G(z) = \{u\}$, while $N_G(u) = V \setminus \{u\}$ — the pair $(z, u)$ has $N_G(z) \cap N_G(u) = \emptyset$, zero common neighbors. Contradiction. So $H$ has no isolated vertices and is a perfect matching: $n - 1$ is even, $n = 2t + 1$ ($t \ge 1$), and $G$ is exactly $t$ triangles sharing $u$: the windmill $W_t$. $\blacksquare$

Figure 1 draws $W_4$ alongside the deleted state $H = G - u$; check all three pair types: two vertices from different blades share only $u$; the two vertices of one blade share only $u$; and the pair $(u, a_i)$ shares only the blade-partner $b_i$.

4.4 The spectral lemma — friendship graphs are (almost) never regular

Lemma. A $k$-regular friendship graph on $n \ge 3$ vertices exists only for $k = 2, n = 3$ (the triangle $K_3$).
Proof. Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix. "Exactly one common neighbor per pair" is a matrix equation: $(A^2)_{uv} = 1$ for $u \ne v$ and $(A^2)_{uu} = d(u) = k$ on the diagonal:

$$A^2 = J + (k - 1)I.$$

Applying both sides to the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$ gives $k^2 \mathbf{1} = A^2 \mathbf{1} = (J + (k - 1)I)\mathbf{1} = (n + k - 1)\mathbf{1}$, so $n = k(k - 1) + 1$. Since $A$ is real symmetric and $J\mathbf{1} = n\mathbf{1}$, $\mathbf{1}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $k$, and $A$ preserves the orthogonal complement $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ (of dimension $n - 1$). On $\mathbf{1}^\perp$, $J$ acts as $0$, so $A^2$ acts as $(k - 1)I$; thus $A$'s eigenvalues on $\mathbf{1}^\perp$ are $\pm\sqrt{k - 1}$ with nonnegative integer multiplicities $a, b$ satisfying $a + b = n - 1$. Because $G$ is a simple graph, the trace of $A$ is $0$:

$$\operatorname{tr} A = k + (a - b)\sqrt{k - 1} = 0.$$

Since $n \ge 3$, we have $k \ge 2$ (as $k = 1$ gives $n = 1$). If $k - 1$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{k - 1}$ is irrational, so $a - b = 0$ and $k = 0$ — impossible. Thus $k - 1 = t^2$ for a positive integer $t$, giving $k = t^2 + 1$ and $(b - a)t = t^2 + 1$. Then $t \mid (t^2 + 1)$ forces $t \mid 1$, so $t = 1, k = 2$, and $n = k(k - 1) + 1 = 3$, which is $K_3$. $\blacksquare$

A counting cross-check of the regular case — honest label: it re-derives the $n$–$k$ ledger of the matrix equation above by double counting (P2) but is not a complete second proof, because it excludes no even $k \ge 4$ (parity kills only the odd $k$: $n = k(k-1) + 1$ is always odd, so $nk = 2|E|$ even forces $k$ even — $k = 4, 6, \dots$ survive; the trace ledger above is what excludes them). Length-$2$ walks between distinct ordered pairs $(u, v)$ number $n(n - 1)$ — one per ordered pair — and also $\sum_{w \in V} d(w)(d(w) - 1) = n k(k - 1)$ (choosing the middle vertex $w$ and two distinct neighbors). Dividing by $n$ gives $n - 1 = k(k - 1)$, i.e. $n = k(k - 1) + 1$. For $k = 2$: $n = 3$.

4.5 Census, spectra, and the shape of the answer

Machine census, all labeled graphs on $n = 3 \dots 7$ vertices: friendship graphs number $1, 0, 15, 0, 105$ — every single one a windmill with a universal vertex, Claim 1 holding throughout. The count has a clean formula: for $n = 3$ ($t = 1$), $W_1 = K_3$ has all $3$ vertices universal, giving $1$ labeled graph; for odd $n = 2t + 1 \ge 5$ ($t \ge 2$), the universal hub has degree $2t > 2$ and is unique ($n$ choices), and the remaining $n - 1$ vertices form a perfect matching ($(n - 2)!!$ ways), giving $n(n - 2)!!$ labeled friendship graphs: $5 \cdot 3!! = 15$, $7 \cdot 5!! = 105$.

The windmill's spectrum is explicit (Exercise 9 derives it): $W_t$ has eigenvalues $1$ ($t - 1$ times), $-1$ ($t$ times), and $(1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 8t})/2$. So $W_2$ carries $\{(1 \pm \sqrt{17})/2, 1, -1, -1\}$ and $W_3$ carries $\{3, 1, 1, -1, -1, -1, -2\}$. Non-regular friendship graphs satisfy the matrix equation in its honest form $A^2 = J + (D - I)$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(\text{degrees})$ — the lab verifies it entry by entry on $W_2$, and on the triangle $K_3$ it reads $A^2 = I + J$ with $\det A = 2$.

from itertools import combinations
import math

# 1. Rank machine over F_p (p prime), guarding empty inputs
def fp_rank(rows, p):
    if not rows or not rows[0]:
        return 0
    M = [[x % p for x in r] for r in rows]
    r0, rank = 0, 0
    for c in range(len(M[0])):
        pvt = next((i for i in range(r0, len(M)) if M[i][c] % p), -1)
        if pvt < 0:
            continue
        M[r0], M[pvt] = M[pvt], M[r0]
        inv = pow(M[r0][c], p - 2, p)  # Fermat inverse in F_p
        M[r0] = [(a * inv) % p for a in M[r0]]
        for i in range(len(M)):
            if i != r0 and M[i][c] % p:
                f = M[i][c]
                M[i] = [(a - f * b) % p for a, b in zip(M[i], M[r0])]
        r0 += 1
        rank += 1
        if r0 == len(M):
            break
    return rank

def vec(A, n):
    return [1 if i in A else 0 for i in range(n)]

def det_lemma_int(d_list, lam=1):
    if any(d <= 0 for d in d_list):
        raise ValueError("Every d_i = |A_i| - lam must be strictly positive.")
    m = len(d_list)
    return math.prod(d_list) + lam * sum(
        math.prod(d_list[j] for j in range(m) if j != i) for i in range(m)
    )

# 4. L3.5 Frankl-Wilson Ramsey graph check (exact clique/independence numbers & degeneracy guard)
def fw_ramsey_stats(p, m, k):
    if not (1 <= k <= m - 1):
        raise ValueError("Require 1 <= k <= m - 1.")
    verts = [set(c) for c in combinations(range(m), k)]
    N = len(verts)
    adj = [0] * N
    for i in range(N):
        for j in range(i + 1, N):
            if len(verts[i] & verts[j]) % p != k % p:
                adj[i] |= 1 << j
                adj[j] |= 1 << i

    def max_clique_size(cands):
        if not cands:
            return 0
        best = 0
        while cands and len(cands) > best:
            v = cands.pop()
            best = max(best, 1 + max_clique_size(cands & {u for u in cands if (adj[v] >> u) & 1}))
        return best

    all_v = (1 << N) - 1
    omega = max_clique_size(set(range(N)))
    # Switch to complement adjacency for independence number alpha
    for i in range(N):
        adj[i] = (~adj[i]) & all_v & ~(1 << i)
    alpha = max_clique_size(set(range(N)))
    fw_cap = sum(math.comb(m, i) for i in range(p))
    rcw_cap = math.comb(m, k // p)
    degen_complete = min(k, m - k) < p
    return N, omega, alpha, fw_cap, rcw_cap, degen_complete

A11. Partition lower bound. Suppose the edges of $K_n$ are partitioned into $r$ complete bipartite subgraphs $K_{A_i, B_i}$ ($i = 1, \dots, r$). Discarding any empty bicliques, we may assume $|A_i| \ge 1$ and $|B_i| \ge 1$ for all $i$. The edge partition is the matrix identity $\sum_{i=1}^r (a_i b_i^T + b_i a_i^T) = J - I$, where $a_i = 1_{A_i}$ and $b_i = 1_{B_i}$. For any $x \in V = \mathbf{1}^\perp = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_{u=1}^n x_u = 0\}$, we have $J x = 0$, so $x^T(J - I)x = -\|x\|^2$, which reads

$$\sum_{i=1}^r 2 s_{A_i}(x) s_{B_i}(x) = -\|x\|^2, \quad \text{where } s_A(x) = a^T x = \sum_{u \in A} x_u.$$

Now define $z_i = |B_i| a_i - |A_i| b_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, r$. Each $z_i$ lies in $V$ because $z_i \cdot \mathbf{1} = |B_i||A_i| - |A_i||B_i| = 0$. Since $\dim V = n - 1$, if $r \le n - 2$ there exists a nonzero vector $x \in V$ orthogonal to $z_1, \dots, z_r$. Orthogonality $z_i \cdot x = 0$ gives $|B_i| s_{A_i}(x) = |A_i| s_{B_i}(x)$, and since $|A_i|, |B_i| \ge 1$,

$$s_{A_i}(x) s_{B_i}(x) = \frac{|B_i|}{|A_i|} s_{A_i}(x)^2 \ge 0 \quad \text{for every } i = 1, \dots, r.$$

Summing over $i$ yields $-\|x\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r 2 s_{A_i}(x) s_{B_i}(x) \ge 0$, forcing $x = 0$, a contradiction. A14. Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson with $n = 6, k = 3, L = \{0, 1\}$ gives the upper bound $|\mathcal{F}| \le \binom{6}{2} = 15$ — valid, but the true maximum is $4$. To prove $|\mathcal{F}| \le 4$ by hand: since $|A_i \cap A_j| \le 1$ for $i \ne j$, no $2$-subset of $[6]$ is contained in more than one block of $\mathcal{F}$. Each $3$-subset contains $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ pairs, so $3|\mathcal{F}| \le \binom{6}{2} = 15$, giving $|\mathcal{F}| \le 5$. Suppose $|\mathcal{F}| = 5$; then all $15$ pairs of $[6]$ are covered once, so every point $x \in [6]$ must pair with the other $5$ points in blocks of size $3$. Since each block containing $x$ covers $2$ of those pairs, $2$ would have to divide $5$, which is impossible! Thus $|\mathcal{F}| \le 4$, and $4$ is attained by $\{1,2,3\}, \{1,4,5\}, \{2,4,6\}, \{3,5,6\}$ (pairwise intersections all equal to $1$).

A15. A linear space on $[4] = \{1,2,3,4\}$ requires every pair of points to lie on a unique line, every line to have size $\ge 2$, and no line to have size $4$. Case 1: there is a line of size $3$, say $\{1,2,3\}$. That line covers the $3$ pairs inside $\{1,2,3\}$, so the remaining $3$ pairs $\{1,4\}, \{2,4\}, \{3,4\}$ must each be their own $2$-point line (no line through $4$ can contain two points of $\{1,2,3\}$ without covering a pair twice). This gives the near-pencil ($N = 4$ lines), and there are $\binom{4}{3} = 4$ such labeled spaces, one for each choice of the point off the $3$-point line. Case 2: every line has size $2$. Then the lines must be all $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs of $[4]$ — the $4$-point affine plane $AG(2, 2)$ ($N = 6$ lines), giving $1$ space. Total: $5$ labeled linear spaces on $[4]$. The $4$ near-pencils have $N = 4 = n$ (making de Bruijn–Erdős tight), while the affine plane has $N = 6 > 4 = n$; all five satisfy $N \ge n$.

A16. In the Petersen graph $KG(5, 2)$, vertices are the $2$-subsets of $[5]$ and edges join disjoint pairs, so an independent set in $KG(5, 2)$ is a clique in its complement $L(K_5)$ — namely, a family $\mathcal{I}$ of $2$-subsets of $[5]$ that pairwise intersect. By the exact case analysis proved in A13 (every pairwise-intersecting family of edges in $K_5$ is either contained in a triangle $K_3$ of size $3$ or in a star of size $4$), we have $\alpha(KG(5, 2)) = 4$, and the independent sets of size $4$ are precisely the five stars $\mathcal{S}_a = \{e \in \binom{[5]}{2} : a \in e\}$ for $a = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. $\blacksquare$
